AWS CERTIFICATION PREP · CHAPTER 08

RDS, Aurora
& Database Choices

The exam's single most confused pair lives in this chapter: Multi-AZ and read replicas both sound like "a second copy of the database," and both questions get answered wrong by candidates who never separate why the copy exists from what the copy is for. Get that distinction solid, add Aurora's real storage-layer difference on top, and this chapter's decision framework covers most of the managed-database scenario questions on both exams.

This chapter also widens the lens past "which RDS feature" to the full data-layer decision: relational (RDS or Aurora) versus DynamoDB versus a cache layer in front of either, because a growing share of real scenario questions on both exams are actually testing whether you'll force a relational shape onto a workload that never needed one — or the reverse.

You will understandWhy Aurora's decoupled storage layer changes failover and replica-promotion speed, not just "is it faster."
You will predictWhat happens, second by second, when a Multi-AZ primary and a read-replica setup each lose their primary instance.

WHY THIS MATTERS ON TEST DAY

If you remember nothing else from this chapter, remember this sentence: Multi-AZ answers "what happens when the primary dies," read replicas answer "how do I serve more reads." Every other fact in this chapter is detail hanging off that one distinction.

ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"Multi-AZ and read replicas are both just extra database copies"

Here's the plausible-sounding wrong instinct: a candidate sees "improve database resilience" and "improve database read performance" as two flavors of the same underlying idea — "add more copies of the database" — and assumes either feature would reasonably solve either problem. Under exam pressure, that collapses into guessing: enabling Multi-AZ to handle read traffic growth, or standing up a read replica and assuming it also protects against the primary failing.

It's an understandable mistake, because both features involve a second RDS instance holding a copy of your data, provisioned in the same console tab, described with similar-sounding words. The exam exploits exactly that surface-level similarity — almost every SAA practice pool includes at least one question built entirely around this confusion.

THE TELL

"Read replica" literally contains the word that should anchor its purpose: reads. "Multi-AZ" literally contains the word that should anchor its purpose: availability zones — i.e., surviving the loss of one. If a scenario says "reduce read latency for a reporting dashboard," that's replicas. If it says "the database must survive an AZ outage with minimal manual intervention," that's Multi-AZ. They are not interchangeable, and — this is the part that trips people — enabling one does not give you the other's benefit for free.

The same "surface similarity, opposite mechanism" trap shows up again one layer down, once you bring Aurora into the picture: candidates who've internalized "Aurora replicas have very low lag" sometimes overgeneralize that into "so Aurora Replicas must give you the same automatic-failover guarantee Multi-AZ gives a stock engine." They're closer to true than the RDS case — Aurora Replicas genuinely can serve as fast, low-lag failover targets — but the exam still wants you to know that's a property of Aurora's shared-storage architecture specifically, not a property that generalizes back to stock RDS engines just because both services use the word "replica."

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

The mechanism: synchronous failover vs asynchronous scaling

The two features are actually built on opposite replication mechanics, and that mechanical difference is exactly why they can't substitute for each other:

  • Multi-AZ replication is synchronous. RDS Multi-AZ maintains a standby in a different AZ that receives every write synchronously before RDS considers the transaction durable — the standby is always caught up, by construction. That's precisely why it's usable for failover: at the moment the primary dies, the standby has (barring an in-flight write at the exact instant of failure) everything the primary had. RDS detects the failure and automatically promotes the standby, repointing the same DNS endpoint — typically within about one to two minutes, though the exact duration depends on the failure mode. But that same synchronous write path is a cost paid on every write, and the standby is not readable — it exists purely as a failover target, not as extra read capacity.
  • Read replica replication is asynchronous. A read replica applies changes after the primary commits them, which means it can lag — usually by a small, variable amount, but never guaranteed to be zero. That lag is exactly why it's safe to point read traffic at it (the replica isn't blocking the primary's writes) and exactly why it's the wrong thing to promote blindly during an outage without accepting some possible data loss for whatever hadn't replicated yet. Read replicas can be manually promoted to a standalone writable instance, but that's a deliberate, non-automatic operation — not the automatic failover Multi-AZ provides.
  • They compose. A production-grade design commonly runs both at once: Multi-AZ for the primary's failover safety net, plus one or more read replicas (which can themselves be Multi-AZ) for read scaling — the two are not exclusive, they answer different questions on the same database.
QUANTIFY IT

Multi-AZ: synchronous, standby not directly readable, automatic failover. Read replica: asynchronous, directly readable, lag is normal and variable, promotion is manual. Confusing "synchronous" with "readable" or "asynchronous" with "unsafe for reads" is the exact wrong turn this section exists to block.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

What the correct approach still has to respect

  • A read replica can lag arbitrarily under heavy write load. There is no hard bound the exam expects you to cite — the exam-relevant point is that lag is a real, monitorable metric (replica lag), not a fixed duration, and an application reading from a replica must tolerate eventually-consistent reads.
  • Promoting a read replica breaks the replication link permanently. Once promoted to standalone, it becomes its own independent writable database — it does not automatically resume as a replica of anything, which matters for any "how do we recover after promoting during an incident" follow-up question.
  • Multi-AZ's standby cannot be queried directly. If a scenario wants both failover protection and an offload target for reporting queries, Multi-AZ alone doesn't provide the second half — that needs a read replica (which itself can be configured Multi-AZ for its own resilience) layered on top.
  • Aurora's storage auto-scales, but compute (the writer/reader instances) does not scale storage capacity planning away entirely — you still pick and can resize compute instance classes; what Aurora removes is manual provisioning of the underlying distributed storage volume itself.
  • Cross-region read replicas exist (for RDS engines that support it, and for Aurora Global Database) specifically for disaster-recovery and global-read-latency scenarios — a different tool from same-region replicas, aimed at surviving a regional event rather than an AZ event (chapter 13 goes deeper on the DR tiers this feeds into).
  • DynamoDB and ElastiCache are not "smaller RDS" — they're structurally different data models (key-value/document at effectively unbounded horizontal scale; in-memory cache or data store) and belong in this decision framework specifically because some scenarios' real bottleneck isn't relational query complexity at all, it's raw key-based access speed or scale, which chapter 9 goes much deeper on for DynamoDB specifically.
  • Automated backups and manual snapshots are a third, separate resilience mechanism from Multi-AZ. RDS takes automated backups within a configured retention window and lets you take on-demand snapshots at any time; restoring either one always creates a brand-new DB instance with a new endpoint — it is not an in-place rewind of the existing instance, and it is not automatic the way Multi-AZ failover is. A scenario asking for point-in-time recovery from human error (an accidental DROP TABLE, say) is testing backups/snapshots, not Multi-AZ or read replicas — a third bucket in this chapter's toolkit, aimed at a third distinct failure mode: data corruption, not instance or AZ loss.
  • Aurora Serverless adds a fourth axis — compute that scales itself, orthogonal to everything above. It doesn't change how Multi-AZ, replicas, or backups behave; it changes how many database capacity units are running at any given moment in response to load, which is a cost-optimization lever more than a resilience one, and worth recognizing by name without over-indexing on it in this resilience-focused chapter.
  • Maintenance windows and engine-version upgrades still touch a Multi-AZ pair, not just a single instance. A scenario describing an unexpected brief interruption during a scheduled maintenance window is usually pointing at how RDS applies certain updates — patching the standby first, failing over, then patching the former primary — rather than at an actual failure. Recognizing "this was maintenance, not an outage" is its own small but real exam distinction.

WHY THE SHAPE MATTERS

Writes always go to the primary, which is protected against AZ failure by its synchronous standby. Reads are free to fan out — to replicas for anything that must hit real relational data, or to a cache in front of the whole thing for the hottest, most repeatable queries. Each layer is solving a different problem; conflating them is exactly this chapter's central trap.

THE THREE-WAY TRAP

The exam likes to test all three resilience mechanisms against each other in one scenario: a stated requirement to "survive an AZ outage" (Multi-AZ), a separate requirement to "scale reporting reads" (read replicas), and a separate requirement to "recover from an accidental bad write" (backups/snapshots). A scenario naming all three in one question is not redundant — it's checking whether you'll reach for one mechanism and assume it silently covers all three failure modes. It never does; each mechanism answers exactly one of the three questions.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three original vignettes, in the exam's own phrasing style

Case 1 — the SaaS billing platform. A B2B SaaS company runs its core billing database on RDS for PostgreSQL. The team's primary worry is a full outage of the AZ hosting the primary during month-end billing runs, when even a few minutes of downtime means missed invoices. Reporting queries currently run directly against the primary and have started causing lock contention that slows down billing writes. A candidate might reach for "just enable Multi-AZ, that solves resilience and takes reporting load off the primary too" — but Multi-AZ's standby isn't queryable, so it does nothing for the reporting contention. Answer shape: enable Multi-AZ for the failover requirement, and separately add a read replica and point the reporting workload at it — two different problems, two different features, both needed.

Case 2 — the global news site. A news publisher serves the same read-heavy article database to users worldwide and wants to cut read latency for users far from the primary region, while keeping a single region for all writes (editorial staff are centralized). Answer shape: Aurora with cross-region read replicas (or Aurora Global Database, for the tightest lag and fastest promotion characteristics) — this specifically targets geographically distributed read latency, which same-region replicas or Multi-AZ alone don't address.

Case 3 — the flash-sale retailer, revisited. The same retailer from chapter 7 now needs its product catalog lookups (a classic high-volume, simple key-based read pattern: "get this product by ID") to survive a 10× spike during the Friday sale without over-provisioning a relational fleet for a pattern that doesn't need relational queries at all. Answer shape: DynamoDB for the catalog lookup table, sized for on-demand or provisioned capacity as chapter 9 details, rather than scaling read replicas of a relational database for a workload that was never relationally shaped to begin with.

NOTE

These three vignettes are original constructions written for this course to model the real exam's scenario style — they are not reproductions of any actual exam question. Notice that all three resolve by matching a specific stated constraint (a protocol, a data-shape, a geography) to a specific mechanism — never by reaching for "the most powerful-sounding option." That pattern holds across virtually every scenario question on both exams, not just database ones.

WHY THE EXAM CARES

Not the exact flags — the exam checks whether you recognize --multi-az as a property of the primary instance (not a separate resource), that a read replica names its own source-db-instance explicitly, that promotion is a one-way, deliberate action with no "un-promote" button, and that restoring a snapshot always produces a brand-new instance identifier and endpoint rather than rewinding the original in place.

ARC 16 · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

What to actually remember on test day

Anchor everything to one sentence: Multi-AZ is synchronous failover, read replicas are asynchronous read scaling — and they compose rather than substitute. Remember that Aurora's real difference is a distributed, self-healing storage layer decoupled from compute, which is why its failover and replica promotion are faster and why storage auto-scales without manual provisioning. Remember that backups and snapshots are a third, separate mechanism aimed at a third failure mode — data corruption or human error — and that restoring one always produces a new instance rather than rewinding an existing one in place. And remember the broader decision framework this chapter sits inside: relational (RDS/Aurora) for structured, query-rich data; DynamoDB for key-based access at large or unpredictable scale; ElastiCache for shaving latency off the hottest reads in front of whichever database is the actual system of record. When a scenario names a specific failure domain — one instance, one AZ, one Region, or simply "a human made a mistake" — match the tool to that exact domain rather than reaching for the most resilience-sounding option by default.

Before moving on, run the three-question filter from this chapter against any database scenario you read from now on, in this order: what exact failure or scale problem is named (an AZ dying, read traffic growing, or a bad write happening); is the data even relational, or would forcing it into RDS/Aurora be over-engineering a simple key-based access pattern; and what is the smallest, cheapest combination of Multi-AZ, replicas, caching, and backups that actually satisfies everything the scenario stated — not more, not less. That third question is this whole course's recurring theme, formalized properly in chapter 13's DR tiers and chapter 14's cost-optimization framework.
