Elastic Load Balancing
& Auto Scaling

Two services the real exam always tests as a pair, because in real architectures they always ship as a pair: a load balancer decides where a request goes, an Auto Scaling group decides how many servers exist to receive it. Pick the wrong load balancer type and requests fail in ways a green health check won't explain. Misconfigure a scaling policy's timing and you either overspend nightly or fall over during the one spike that mattered.

ARC 01 · EXAM CONTEXT

Where this sits on the real blueprint

This chapter maps to two SAA-C03 domains at once — the exam treats load balancing and scaling as inseparable: Design Resilient Architectures (26%), because a load balancer plus an Auto Scaling group spanning multiple Availability Zones is the single most common "how do you survive an AZ failure" answer on the exam, and Design High-Performing Architectures (24%), because choosing the right load balancer type and the right scaling policy is what keeps performance flat as demand changes. A large fraction of both exams' scenario questions describe a web tier under variable load and ask you to pick the load-balancer/ASG combination that satisfies a stated constraint — this is exactly that pattern, practiced explicitly.

WHY THIS MATTERS ON TEST DAY

Every scenario in this chapter follows the same shape: a stated protocol or performance requirement (HTTP path routing? raw TCP at extreme throughput? a partner firewall demanding a fixed IP?) plus a stated traffic pattern (steady? spiky? predictable by clock, or not?). ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"It's a load balancer — they're basically interchangeable"

Here is the plausible-sounding first instinct a lot of candidates bring into this material: Application Load Balancer is the newest, most feature-rich load balancer AWS sells, so it's the safe default answer for any "distribute traffic across instances" scenario — HTTP or not, extreme throughput or not, static-IP requirement or not. Pair it with an Auto Scaling group set to target tracking on CPU utilization, and assume that as long as scaling is "on," the exact numbers — cooldown period, warm-up time, health-check threshold — are implementation detail that doesn't change the correct answer.

It's an understandable instinct: ALB genuinely is the more capable, more modern service for the majority of web workloads, and target tracking genuinely is the recommended default scaling policy type. The instinct is wrong only at the edges — but the exam's scenario questions live almost entirely at those edges, because a question with no edge case has no wrong answer to test.

THE TELL

Watch for scenario language like "a partner's firewall must allow-list a fixed IP," "the game client speaks UDP," "millions of requests per second with the lowest possible added latency," or "the workload's peak happens every day at 9 AM, predictably." Each of those phrases is the exam pointing you away from the default answer — toward NLB, toward scheduled or predictive scaling, or toward a specific health-check setting — and each is explored below.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

The mechanism the "just use ALB + default settings" instinct ignores

Three concrete mechanisms explain why "always ALB, defaults are fine" fails specific scenarios:

  • ALB operates at Layer 7 — it terminates and parses HTTP/HTTPS (and gRPC/WebSocket). It cannot front raw TCP or UDP traffic that isn't HTTP, and it does not hand you a fixed IP address per Availability Zone the way a scenario demanding IP allow-listing requires. Network Load Balancer operates at Layer 4: it passes TCP/UDP/TLS traffic through with extremely low added latency, preserves the client's source IP by default, and gives you one static IP (or an Elastic IP you assign) per subnet/AZ — which is precisely the feature a "partner firewall needs a fixed IP" or "this is a gaming UDP protocol" scenario is testing.
  • The cooldown/warm-up period is not friction to eliminate — it's the mechanism that prevents scaling thrash. CloudWatch metrics for a newly launched instance take real wall-clock time to appear and stabilize, and an Auto Scaling group's default cooldown period is 300 seconds. Zero it out and a simple-scaling policy will launch another wave of instances before the first wave's improved metrics have even been reported — overshooting capacity, then thrashing back down once the aggregate catches up. Step scaling and target tracking instead lean on an instance warm-up estimate: a newly launched instance's metrics are excluded from the group's aggregate until it's finished warming up, specifically so a not-yet-ready instance can't make the policy think the fleet is cooler than it is and scale in again too soon. Misreading either knob is the classic "did it over-scale or under-scale" exam trap.
  • A CPU-utilization target-tracking policy is the right default — until CPU isn't the bottleneck. A workload bottlenecked on request count, network throughput, or a custom application metric (queue depth, requests-per-target) will scale on the wrong signal if you leave the metric as CPU by default; target tracking supports request-count-per-target and custom CloudWatch metrics specifically for this reason.
QUANTIFY IT

Default Auto Scaling cooldown: 300 seconds. Default target-group deregistration delay (connection draining): 300 seconds — the time an instance being removed from rotation is given to finish in-flight requests before it's actually terminated. Both numbers are exam-relevant precisely because "300 seconds" is long enough that a candidate who assumes scaling and deregistration are instantaneous will misjudge a scenario's timing.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

What the correct approach still has to respect

Getting the load-balancer type and scaling-policy type right doesn't end the design work — several operational details still trip candidates who otherwise reasoned correctly:

  • Cross-zone load balancing defaults differ. ALB always distributes evenly across all registered AZs — you cannot turn it off. NLB has cross-zone load balancing off by default; enabling it can route traffic across AZ boundaries, which is architecturally important to know exists even though the exact data-transfer pricing for that path is the kind of number this course won't pretend to fix in place.
  • Sticky sessions (session affinity) pin a client to one target — useful for a stateful app that hasn't externalized session state, but it actively works against even load distribution, which is exactly why the exam pairs "sticky sessions" scenarios with "why is one instance overloaded" follow-ups.
  • ELB health checks vs EC2 status checks inside an ASG are not the same signal. By default an Auto Scaling group only replaces instances that fail the basic EC2 status check. If you want the ASG to replace an instance that's running but failing your application's own health endpoint, you must explicitly turn on ELB health checks for the group — otherwise a "healthy at the OS level, broken at the app level" instance sits in rotation indefinitely.
  • Health check grace period vs health check interval/threshold are two different clocks. The grace period gives a freshly launched instance time to boot and initialize before failed checks count against it at all; the interval and healthy/unhealthy threshold then govern ongoing monitoring. Set the grace period shorter than your application's real startup time and the ASG will loop: launch, fail health checks before the app is even up, terminate, launch again.
  • Target group and listener-rule counts are finite per load balancer and are the kind of account-level quota that's raisable via Service Quotas — a scenario describing an unusually large number of routing rules or target groups is testing whether you know to check quotas, not a specific number to memorize.
  • Instance Refresh rolls out a new launch template version without manual termination. Rather than hand-terminating instances one by one to pick up a new AMI, Instance Refresh replaces a group's instances in controlled batches, respecting a minimum-healthy-percentage you set, so capacity never drops below a safe floor mid-rollout. The exam's favorite framing: "how do you update every running instance to a new AMI with zero manual termination and a bounded capacity drop" — that's Instance Refresh, not a script.
  • Specific ASG processes can be suspended independently. You can suspend just AZRebalance during a deliberate maintenance window (so the group won't "helpfully" rebalance instances across AZs while you're working), or suspend ReplaceUnhealthy temporarily while debugging a failing health check — without turning off scaling entirely. A scenario describing "we need to pause one specific automatic behavior without disabling the whole group" is pointing at process suspension, not at deleting the ASG.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three original vignettes, in the exam's own phrasing style

Case 1 — the mobile game studio. A studio runs a real-time multiplayer game server over a custom UDP protocol, with match state held in memory on each game-server process. Several corporate customers require the studio's servers to sit behind a small, fixed set of IP addresses so their office firewalls can allow-list them, and the studio's SRE team is also worried about added latency, since the whole gameplay experience degrades noticeably past a small number of milliseconds of extra round-trip. A tempting wrong answer some candidates reach for is "put it behind an ALB and use a Network Load Balancer only if UDP support turns out to be required" — but the UDP requirement alone already eliminates ALB before latency or IP allow-listing even enter the decision. Answer shape: Network Load Balancer — UDP support plus a static IP per AZ (or an assigned Elastic IP) satisfies both stated constraints, and NLB's Layer 4 design is exactly what minimizes added latency; ALB satisfies none of the three requirements simultaneously.

Case 2 — the flash-sale retailer. An e-commerce company runs a predictable weekly flash sale every Friday at 12:00 in its local time zone, plus occasional unannounced viral spikes triggered by social-media mentions on other days. A candidate might reach for "just set the ASG's maximum size very high and let target tracking handle everything" — which technically works but means the fleet spends the first several scaling intervals of every Friday spike playing catch-up instead of already being ready at 12:00 sharp, and the exam scores that as a worse answer than one that's actually ready in advance. Which scaling configuration covers both patterns well? Answer shape: a scheduled scaling action pre-provisioning extra baseline capacity shortly before the known Friday spike, layered with a target-tracking policy handling the unpredictable viral spikes reactively on the other days — the two policy types are additive within one Auto Scaling group, not mutually exclusive.

Case 3 — the fintech API. A company exposes a REST API and a WebSocket-based live-quotes feed from the same backend fleet, and needs to route /quotes traffic to a differently-tuned target group than everything else, based purely on the URL path — while also wanting a single public endpoint and a single managed TLS certificate for both. Which load balancer? Answer shape: Application Load Balancer — path- based routing rules and native WebSocket support are both Layer-7 capabilities NLB cannot provide, and ALB's listener can hold both the default HTTPS certificate and the routing rule in one place.

These three vignettes are original constructions written for this course to model the real exam's scenario style — they are not reproductions of any actual exam question.

# illustrative
aws autoscaling put-scaling-policy \
  --auto-scaling-group-name app-asg --policy-name tt-cpu-50 \

# illustrative
aws autoscaling start-instance-refresh \

WHY THE EXAM CARES

Not the exact flags — the exam checks whether you know --health-check-type ELB is the switch that makes an ASG trust application-level health over bare EC2 status, that a target-tracking policy's TargetValue is the thermostat setpoint driving the desired-capacity math from §8, and that MinHealthyPercentage on an Instance Refresh is the knob that bounds how much capacity is allowed to disappear mid-rollout — the same "never drop below a safe floor" idea as min-size, applied to deployments instead of scaling.

ARC 16 · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

What to actually remember on test day

Boil this chapter down to five things: pick ALB for HTTP-aware Layer 7 routing and NLB for raw TCP/UDP, static IPs, or extreme low-latency throughput — Classic Load Balancer is almost never the right new-design answer. Remember that a cooldown/warm-up period exists to stop scaling thrash, not to be minimized, and that ASGs only auto-replace app-level failures if you've explicitly enabled ELB health checks. Default to target tracking as your scaling policy unless the pattern is predictable (scheduled/predictive) or needs graduated thresholds (step). And remember the two 300-second defaults — cooldown and deregistration delay — because the exam uses them to test whether you think scaling and connection draining are instantaneous. One more habit worth building now: whenever a scenario names a specific operational behavior you haven't memorized a dedicated feature for — "roll out a new AMI without dropping capacity," "pause just one automatic ASG behavior during a maintenance window," "keep a session pinned to one server" — assume AWS has a named, purpose-built mechanism for it (Instance Refresh, process suspension, sticky sessions) rather than assuming you'd need to script it yourself. That instinct alone eliminates a large share of the distractor answers on both exams.
