WHATSAPP ARCHITECTURE · CHAPTER 08

Presence, Typing &
Read Receipts at Scale

Seven chapters in, this course has been about payloads that matter: encrypted messages (ch. 3-4), a store-and-forward pipeline built to never lose one (ch. 5), fan-out to hundreds of group members (ch. 6), and a content-addressed media pipeline for the bytes riding alongside them (ch. 7). This chapter is about the opposite kind of data — a boolean, a timestamp, a three-dot ellipsis — that individually mean almost nothing, but collectively generate a request volume that dwarfs the messaging pipeline this whole course has been building. We will derive, from the shape of the problem itself, why presence has to live entirely in RAM, why it is allowed to lie for a few seconds, and why the correct engineering response to "the phone died mid-conversation" is to do nothing and let time solve it.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

The cheapest-looking, most expensive subsystem in the stack

Every subsystem this course has covered so far shares one property: a message is written once and read once (or a small, bounded number of times — once per group member, at most). The delivery pipeline in chapter 5 exists to move that single write to its handful of readers reliably, in order, exactly once as far as the UI is concerned. Presence inverts that shape entirely. A single state change — Alice's phone goes from foreground to locked — is written once and must be delivered to every person currently interested, and "every person currently interested" recomputes itself dozens of times a minute across a population of a billion devices. Presence, typing, and read receipts are, per byte of user-perceived value, one of the highest-event-rate workloads the backend runs — not because any single event is expensive, but because there are staggeringly more of them than there are messages.

Look at what the feature actually looks like on screen: a small grey or green dot, a "last seen today at 14:02" string, a three-dot typing ellipsis, and two grey ticks turning blue. None of that reads as engineering-hard. It reads as the kind of feature you'd expect an intern to ship in a sprint. That mismatch between apparent triviality and actual operational cost is the whole reason this subsystem deserves its own chapter: a system that looks like a rounding error in the product spec turns out, once you count events instead of pixels, to be structurally the largest-volume traffic class in the entire application.

  • Write-once, read-by-N-interested-parties, not read-by-N-recipients. A message has a fixed, known recipient set at send time (the other member(s) of the conversation). A presence change has no fixed recipient set at all — the set of people who currently care is whoever has your chat open right now, which changes from second to second and which the sender's device has no way to know in advance.
  • Frequency, not payload size, is the cost driver. A presence update is a few bytes: a user id, a status enum, a timestamp. Compare that to an encrypted message body, a thumbnail, or a video chunk — presence wins on payload size by orders of magnitude and still costs more in aggregate, because a phone's screen state changes far more often than a person sends a message.
  • Low value per event, high volume of events. Losing one message is a user-visible incident. Losing one presence blip — a green dot that should have flickered off for 400ms during a cell-tower handoff — is invisible. That asymmetry, counterintuitively, is what makes the engineering problem tractable: it licenses an entire class of storage and delivery guarantees (namely: none) that would be unacceptable for messages.
  • The typing indicator and the read receipt are the same shape of problem wearing different clothes. Both are high-frequency, low-durability, best-effort broadcasts of ephemeral human activity, layered on top of the same subscription and delivery substrate presence uses. Understanding presence gets you typing and read-receipts for free, structurally — this chapter treats all three as one mechanism.
THE FRAMING FOR THIS CHAPTER

Every design decision from here on is a consequence of one sentence: presence must be treated as a fundamentally different class of data than a message — ephemeral, in-memory only, lossy by design, and priced to be nearly free per event, because the event rate makes "expensive per event" catastrophic in a way it simply isn't for the messaging pipeline. Keep that sentence in mind through sections 3 and 4: the naive solution fails not because it's wrong in spirit, but because it prices presence like a message.

ARC 02 · HISTORICAL CONTEXT

From XMPP roster broadcast to a subscribe-on-open model

The chat server this course has referenced since chapter 1 began life as a fork of ejabberd, an open-source Erlang implementation of XMPP, informally diverged internally into what protocol reverse-engineering researchers have dubbed FunXMPP — a simplified, binary-framed cousin of the XML-stanza protocol it started from. XMPP came with a presence model already built in: roster-based presence, where your status is broadcast to your entire contact list the moment it changes, whether or not anyone is looking. That model made sense for the IM clients XMPP was designed around, where roster sizes were small and session counts were in the thousands, not the hundreds of millions. It is also, as sections 3-4 derive in detail, the exact model that cannot survive contact with a billion-user roster graph. Presence's history is largely the history of walking away from that inherited default, one architectural decision at a time.

A few of these episodes are worth dwelling on because they show the tension this whole chapter is about playing out in public, not just in server logs. The blue-tick read-receipt rollout in particular is a rare case where a purely internal delivery-semantics decision (should the recipient's read state be visible to the sender) became a front-page privacy story within weeks of shipping, and the fix — a user-facing on/off toggle — arrived fast enough to suggest the backend already had the plumbing to suppress delivery of a signal it was otherwise generating for every read event. That detail matters for section 8: the server evidently always knows the true state; a privacy toggle changes what it is willing to disclose, not what it computes.

The industry-wide pattern here is not unique to this stack. Slack, Discord, and most large-scale chat systems built after this era converged on the same conclusion independently: presence is ephemeral infrastructure, not a persisted product feature, and it should be architected — and budgeted — accordingly. That convergence is strong evidence this isn't one company's idiosyncratic choice; it's close to the only design that survives contact with hundreds of millions of concurrently connected clients.

ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

The strawman: broadcast every change to the whole roster

The obvious, XMPP-native solution requires no invention at all — it's the protocol's default behavior. Every time a user's connection state changes (comes online, goes offline, or the client explicitly publishes a status), the server walks that user's full contact/roster list and pushes a presence stanza to every entry, exactly as vanilla XMPP roster presence specifies. No subscription negotiation beyond "you're in my roster," no notion of who's currently paying attention — the server's job is simply to fan a state change out to everyone who might conceivably care.

pseudo-XMPPnaive roster broadcast — the strawman
// Alice's connection process, on any state transition
on connection_state_change(Alice, NewState) {
    // NewState ∈ { available, unavailable, away }
    Roster = load_full_roster(Alice.user_id);   // every contact, no filter

    for Contact in Roster {                       // O(roster size) per toggle
        Stanza = <presence> {
            from: Alice.jid,
            to:   Contact.jid,
            type: NewState == available ? "available" : "unavailable",
        };
        // fired unconditionally — regardless of whether Contact has
        // Alice's chat open, backgrounded, or hasn't looked in months
        deliver_or_queue(Contact.jid, Stanza);
    }
}

This is not a bad-faith strawman — it is exactly how roster presence in stock XMPP is specified to behave, and it is a completely reasonable design for the problem XMPP was originally built to solve: a desktop IM client with a roster of dozens of contacts, refreshing state a handful of times per session. The next section quantifies precisely why the same logic, run unmodified against a contact graph and toggle frequency three to four orders of magnitude larger, stops being reasonable and starts being existential.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

The math: trillions of events a day, from a feature that looks free

Run the naive broadcast model's cost forward using numbers that are unremarkable for an actual user. Take a single person with roughly 200 contacts — an ordinary, not even especially social, phone's contact list. Every screen unlock, every cellular-to-WiFi handoff, every few-second network flap on a train, every time the OS suspends and resumes the app in the background, is a plausible online/offline transition. A conservative estimate of 40 such toggles a day is not an exaggeration for an always-in-your-pocket device — it is, if anything, low for a heavy user whose phone roams between cell towers and WiFi APs all day.

THE ARITHMETIC

200 contacts × 40 toggles/day = 8,000 presence-delivery events/day — from one user's toggling alone. Scale that across a population on the order of a billion active users and the naive broadcast model is generating somewhere in the range of several trillion presence-delivery events per day. Compare that against the oft-cited figure of roughly 100 billion messages/day flowing through the entire messaging pipeline studied in chapters 5-6 — presence, under the naive model, is generating one to two orders of magnitude more events than the feature the whole rest of this course has been built around, using a mechanism nobody thinks of as the "real" backend workload.

That number alone would be bad enough if these were cheap, fire-and-forget UDP packets with no state attached. They are not, under the naive model, because roster presence as XMPP specifies it is delivered with the same reliability expectations as any other stanza — meaning a receiving server (or a receiving connection process) is expected to queue it for an offline contact, just like a message. Multiply the trillions-of-events figure by even a modest per-event storage or queueing cost and you get a subsystem that would need infrastructure comparable to, or larger than, the entire message-delivery pipeline — to move data that is, individually, nearly worthless and stale within seconds of being generated.

The conclusion this section is building toward is not "make broadcast cheaper." It is a category change: presence must be treated as a fundamentally different class of data from a message — ephemeral, held only in RAM, never written to durable storage, lossy by design, and delivered best-effort with no retry guarantee. That's not a performance optimization bolted onto the XMPP model; it's a rejection of the premise that presence deserves message-grade delivery guarantees at all. No durable-storage-backed system, and no delivery pipeline with message-grade retry and persistence semantics, could economically absorb a write rate in the trillions/day at this price point — and critically, none of it needs to, because losing a stray presence blip has close to zero user-visible consequence, unlike losing a message, which is precisely the asymmetry section 1 opened with.

ARC 05 · BETTER SOLUTION

Eliminating candidates before arriving at subscribe-on-open

Three candidate fixes are worth examining and rejecting in turn before landing on the one that actually holds up, because each of the rejected ones is a natural first instinct and each fails for a genuinely instructive reason.

The third option — deliver presence for a peer only while a watcher currently has that peer's chat open, or was recently active there — is the one this chapter builds out in full for the rest of the sections. It collapses fan-out from "everyone who has your number saved" down to "the handful of people currently looking at your chat," which for almost every user, almost all the time, is a single-digit or low-double-digit number, not hundreds. That collapse in fan-out width is the entire engineering win; everything from section 6 onward is about what that collapse still costs and how the remaining cost is paid for.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL COSTS

Subscribe-on-open is cheaper, not free

Collapsing the fan-out width from "contact list" to "current watchers" is a huge win, but it does not make the problem free, and a Staff-level design review should be able to name exactly what it still costs before signing off on it.

  • Subscription sets are themselves distributed, mutable, shared state. A user's watchers are not necessarily connected to the same physical server the user's own connection lives on — in a geo-distributed fleet, Alice's connection process might be on a server in one region while three of her current watchers are connected to servers in two others. The subscription set has to be either replicated, routed through, or looked up across machine boundaries, and that routing has to happen on every single presence change, not just at subscribe time.
  • Garbage collection of subscriptions is a hard problem precisely because there is no clean unsubscribe. A user closing a chat screen is a clean signal. A user's phone dying, losing signal permanently for the rest of the session, or the app being force-killed by the OS, is not — no unsubscribe message is ever sent in the overwhelmingly common case on mobile. The server has to detect subscription staleness from connection death, not from an explicit protocol message, which means the subscription manager's correctness depends on the connection layer's crash/death detection being reliable and prompt (§8 makes this concrete).
  • Cross-datacenter presence routing adds real latency and real bandwidth cost. Even after fan-out width shrinks from hundreds to single digits, those single digits can be spread across every region the service operates in, and every presence change has to cross those regional boundaries to reach them — a cost that scales with geographic spread of a user's social graph, not with its size.
  • There is a genuine tuning tension in refresh/debounce intervals. Refresh too aggressively (to keep "online" feeling instantaneous) and you reintroduce a smaller-scale version of the event storm this whole chapter is trying to escape. Refresh too lazily and presence starts looking stale — a green dot for a user who went offline four minutes ago — which erodes the feature's entire reason for existing.

None of these costs are reasons to reject the subscribe-on-open model — they're the residual engineering problems that remain after the model has already eliminated the dominant one (fan-out width). Section 7 names the concrete components that absorb each of these costs, and section 8 walks through exactly how each is handled.

ARC 08 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

Subscriptions, debounce, batching, and the honesty of "online"

This is the mechanism section. Four things happen here in sequence: the subscribe/unsubscribe lifecycle and its unclean-disconnect edge case, the split between coarse persisted "last seen" and live in-memory "online," the typing-indicator debounce protocol and its client-side self-healing property, and read-receipt batching with its N² group-fan-out ceiling.

The subscribe / unsubscribe lifecycle

Opening a chat with peer X sends a subscribe(X) request to the server. The presence layer adds the requester's connection id to X's entry in the subscription table — conceptually an ETS-backed mapping from user id to the set of connection ids currently watching them — and immediately returns X's current cached status from the presence table, so the requester's UI has an answer before X's next state change ever happens. Closing the chat, or backgrounding the app past a short timeout, sends unsubscribe(X), which removes that same entry. This is the clean path, and it is cheap: two ETS operations, no cross-process coordination beyond locating the table (or its owning process) for X's shard.

The path that actually dominates in practice on mobile is not the clean one. When a watcher's TCP connection simply dies — app killed by the OS, battery pulled, radio drops permanently for the rest of the session — no unsubscribe message is ever sent. The server has no explicit signal that the watcher is gone; it only has the connection process's own death. This is the same idea this course's earlier chapters established for crash handling more broadly: process death is itself an observable, structured event, not silence to be timed out. The connection process that owned that socket terminates, its supervisor observes the termination, and the termination handler — not a separate timeout sweep over the subscription table — is what triggers garbage collection of every subscription that connection held. The subscription manager never polls "is this watcher still alive"; it reacts to a death notification exactly once, at the moment the connection layer already knows the connection is gone.

"Last seen" is coarse and persisted; "online" is live and never touches disk

These are two different kinds of fact wearing the same UI treatment, and conflating them is a common junior mistake. "Online" is a live signal that exists only as long as the in-memory presence entry does — the moment the server process holding it dies, or the connection drops, the fact disappears with it. It is never persisted, because there is nothing meaningful to persist: "online" is true only as a statement about the current instant, and a stale "online" written to disk five seconds ago is actively misleading rather than merely outdated. "Last seen" is the opposite: a single coarse timestamp, updated at low frequency (not on every heartbeat), persisted just durably enough to survive a client reconnect and answer "when were they last around" even after a server restart. It's the one piece of presence state that earns a spot outside pure RAM, precisely because it's low-frequency and low-precision by design — updating it once a minute rather than once a second is invisible to the user and saves the one persistence path this subsystem has from becoming a second trillion-events-a-day problem.

Privacy settings shape disclosure, not knowledge

The everyone / contacts / nobody privacy setting is enforced as a filter applied at delivery time, inside the component this chapter labels the privacy filter (§7) — it sits between "the presence table has a true answer" and "this specific watcher is allowed to receive it." Be precise about what that setting does and does not change: the server necessarily computes and holds the true underlying state regardless of any watcher's privacy configuration, because computing it is what the connection layer does as a side effect of a socket being open at all. What the privacy setting changes is whether that already-known fact is disclosed to a given requester. This is not a cynical observation — it's structurally unavoidable: you cannot select which contacts don't get a presence update without already knowing what update would have been sent to everyone.

Typing indicators: fire-and-forget, debounced, self-healing on the receiving end

A typing indicator carries no acknowledgment, no retry, and no durability contract. If a typing-start event is lost in flight, nothing recovers it — and that is an intentional design choice, not an oversight, because the cost of a rare missed typing flicker is zero and the cost of adding retry infrastructure to guarantee its delivery would be wildly disproportionate to that near-zero value.

The client does not send a typing event per keystroke — at 5-10 keystrokes/second for an average typist, that would reproduce the exact event-storm shape section 4 just spent a page quantifying, just scoped to one conversation instead of one contact list. Instead: one event fires on typing-start, then a refresh event fires every few seconds while typing continues, and the receiving client's UI is responsible for expiring the indicator locally — typically after a few seconds with no refresh — rather than waiting for an explicit "stopped typing" event from the network.

WHY THE EXPIRY IS CLIENT-SIDE, NOT SERVER-DRIVEN

This is the detail worth defending in an interview. If "stopped typing" were its own discrete event sent over the same unreliable, fire-and-forget channel as everything else in this section, then losing that one event — exactly as likely as losing any other — would leave a permanently-typing ghost indicator on the recipient's screen with no mechanism to ever clear it. Making expiry a property of the receiving client's own local timeout guarantees self-healing regardless of what the network did or didn't deliver: worst case, the indicator lingers for one extra timeout window and then clears itself, with no dependency on any specific packet having arrived. The correctness of the UI does not depend on delivery of any single message — only on the absence of continued refreshes, which is a property the receiving client can observe entirely locally.

Quantify the win: naive per-keystroke sending at roughly 5-10 events/second per active typist, against one start-event plus a refresh every 3-5 seconds, is roughly one to two orders of magnitude fewer events for a typical typing burst of a few seconds — the same shape of reduction subscribe-on-open bought against roster broadcast, applied one layer down, inside a single conversation instead of across a contact graph.

Read receipts: batched acks, symmetric privacy, and the N² wall on large groups

A user scrolling through a backlog of thirty unread messages does not generate thirty individual read-receipt stanzas — the client batches the message ids that were read together into a single receipt, coalescing what would otherwise be a burst proportional to backlog size into one event. That receipt feeds the tick state machine established in chapter 5: single tick means server custody, double tick means device-level delivery ack, and blue double tick means this batched read receipt was received and the recipient's own privacy setting allows disclosing it — this chapter does not re-derive that state machine, only its presence-layer plumbing.

The privacy setting for read receipts is deliberately symmetric: turning off your own read receipts also turns off your ability to see other people's. This is an explicit anti-free-rider design choice, not an accidental coupling — without it, a user could observe everyone else's read state while disclosing nothing about their own, which is exactly the asymmetric information advantage the symmetric rule exists to close off.

WHY LARGE-GROUP READ RECEIPTS ARE CAPPED

Derive the blowup directly: in a 256-member group, if every member reads a message, that's up to 256 distinct read-receipt events generated. In the naive per-member fan-out model, each of those 256 events then has to be delivered to the other 255 members so everyone can see who-read-what — an event count that scales with the square of group size, not linearly. At 256 members that's on the order of 256 × 255 ≈ 65,000 receipt-delivery events for a single message, growing quadratically as the group grows further. Real systems cap this hard: large groups get a coarse "read by N" count, or no detailed per-member receipt fan-out at all, because paying O(N²) traffic for a feature whose marginal value per additional confirmation is close to zero is not a trade a well-run backend makes at any group size where N is in the hundreds or more.

Why RAM and ETS, not a database, is the only viable storage choice

Holding presence state and subscription sets in RAM for hundreds of millions of concurrently connected users is not a performance shortcut layered on top of a "real" persistent design — it is the only storage model that survives the event rate derived in section 4. In-memory Erlang process state and ETS tables give O(microseconds) reads and writes with no disk I/O and no replication-consistency machinery to keep synchronized, at the explicit cost of losing everything the instant a process or node dies. That cost is acceptable specifically because presence is cheap to reconstruct: a client that loses its server-side state simply reconnects and resubscribes within seconds, and "online" becomes accurate again the moment its connection re-establishes — there is no recovery procedure to write, because the client already re-derives the correct state as an ordinary consequence of reconnecting, which is a property "last seen" (and certainly a message) does not share.

ARC 09 · TRADEOFFS

How this compares across the industry

Presence design is a genuine product-and-architecture tradeoff space, not a solved problem with one right answer — every mainstream messenger has picked a different point on the fan-out-width/feature-richness curve.

SystemFan-out shapeNotable tradeoff
XMPP roster presenceFull broadcast to entire roster, unconditionally unbounded The ancestor model this stack explicitly diverged from as connection counts scaled past hundreds of millions (§2-§4) — simple to reason about, structurally incompatible with this scale.
WhatsApp-style (this chapter)Subscribe-on-open, watcher-set bounded by concurrently open chats small Fan-out scales with interested-watcher count, not contact-list size; pays the residual costs enumerated in §6 (cross-DC routing, GC-on-death) in exchange.
SlackWorkspace/channel-scoped bounded Fan-out is bounded by fixed workspace or channel membership rather than an open contact graph — a structurally smaller and more bounded problem than a global social graph from the start.

The common thread across every entrant except the XMPP ancestor: bound the fan-out width structurally, either by shrinking the interested-party set (subscribe-on-open) or by shrinking the eligible-audience set itself (workspace/guild scoping). The systems that didn't converge on one of those two strategies simply don't operate at a scale where the naive model's cost becomes visible yet.

ARC 10 · PERFORMANCE CHARACTERISTICS

Cost per connection, cost per fan-out, cost per keystroke

Four cost axes matter here, and they compound rather than trade off against each other.

  • Memory per connection. A lightweight Erlang/OTP process has historically been cited as carrying a minimal stack/heap footprint on the order of a couple of kilobytes at rest. Multiply that by hundreds of millions of concurrently connected devices and the connection layer's baseline memory cost is already material before a single ETS subscription-set entry is added — each watched relationship adds a further small, fixed-size entry to the subscription table, so the true per-user memory cost scales with (their own connection state) + (number of peers currently watching them), not with their full contact-list size.
  • Fan-out multiplier as a function of subscription model. Under roster broadcast, fan-out scales with contact-list size — hundreds, regardless of interest. Under subscribe-on-open, fan-out scales with concurrently-interested-watcher count, typically a single-digit or low-double-digit number. That difference compounds directly into every other axis below: it's not a one-time discount, it's a multiplier applied to every single presence change, every day, for every user.
  • Radio and battery cost on the receiving device. Every pushed presence or typing event that reaches a phone is a wake-up of its radio and, depending on OS scheduling, potentially its application processor — a cost invisible to the backend's own metrics but directly billed to the user's battery. High-frequency, low-value pushes (the naive per-keystroke typing model, or roster-broadcast presence) impose this cost on every watching device, which is a second, independent reason (beyond server-side load) to minimize event rate at the source rather than merely compress or batch it after the fact.
  • CPU cost per event dispatch. Each presence or typing event dispatched at the connection-process level costs a small, roughly constant amount of CPU to look up the subscription set, apply the privacy filter, and hand off to the transport — cheap per event, but multiplied by the fan-out multiplier above, which is exactly why shrinking that multiplier structurally (§5) matters far more than optimizing the constant-factor cost of any single dispatch.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD PRODUCTION

What's actually published, and what has to be reasoned out

Exact current connections-per-server figures, fleet topology, and any presence-specific incident postmortems are not public. What is public, and what this section leans on, is a small set of well-known engineering milestones plus the general operational reality of running ephemeral state at this scale.

  • Roughly 2 million concurrent connections per physical server. Widely published in WhatsApp engineering talks — including Rick Reed's Erlang Factory / Code BEAM presentations — as a later-era successor to the earlier, equally well-known "1 million is so 2011" milestone. The number describes a heavily tuned FreeBSD host using kqueue-based I/O multiplexing under a BEAM runtime; it's cited here as evidence for the connection-layer scale that presence's subscription and delivery machinery has to operate on top of, not as a presence-specific figure.
  • Roughly 50 engineers supporting on the order of 900 million users shortly after the 2014 acquisition. This ratio of users to engineers is only achievable when subsystems like presence are deliberately cheap per unit — in-memory, ephemeral, with no durability tax — because a subsystem generating trillions of events a day that required database-grade operational care (backups, replication monitoring, schema migrations) would by itself have needed a team the acquisition-era headcount could not have supported.
  • The 2014-2015 blue-tick read-receipt backlash. A real, widely reported product episode: read receipts shipped without an off switch, users objected loudly to the loss of plausible-deniability around message-reading, and an explicit privacy toggle followed quickly. Section 2's timeline covers this in more detail; it's referenced again here as a rare public data point confirming the server already computed the signal universally and the fix was disclosure policy, not new computation.
  • A server crash or redeploy drops all in-memory presence and subscription state for its share of users — and this is an accepted, designed-for cost, not an incident. Because clients simply reconnect and resubscribe within seconds, the system is briefly "wrong" about a sliver of users' presence during that window and then self-corrects with no operator intervention and no data-recovery procedure, which is exactly the property that makes RAM-only storage viable in the first place (§8).

(a) Subscribe / unsubscribe against the ETS subscription table

Erlang-flavored pseudocodesubscription_mgr.erl
handle_call({subscribe, Watcher, Target}, _From, State) ->
    %% ets:insert_new keeps this idempotent — resubscribing is a no-op, not a bug
    ets:insert(subs_table, {{Target, Watcher}, erlang:monotonic_time()}),
    ets:update_counter(watch_count, Target, {2, 1}, {Target, 0}),
    CurrentStatus = presence_server:lookup(Target),
    {reply, {ok, CurrentStatus}, State};

handle_call({unsubscribe, Watcher, Target}, _From, State) ->
    ets:delete(subs_table, {Target, Watcher}),
    ets:update_counter(watch_count, Target, {2, -1}),
    {reply, ok, State}.

(b) Connection-death cleanup — the path that actually dominates

Erlang-flavored pseudocodesubscription_mgr.erl — supervisor-triggered
%% invoked by the supervisor's 'DOWN' handler when a connection process
%% terminates for ANY reason — crash, socket close, OOM-kill, no explicit
%% unsubscribe ever required
handle_info({'DOWN', _Ref, process, ConnPid, _Reason}, State) ->
    Watcher = conn_registry:owner_of(ConnPid),
    Targets = ets:match(subs_table, {{'$1', Watcher}, '_'}),
    lists:foreach(fun([Target]) ->
        ets:delete(subs_table, {Target, Watcher}),
        ets:update_counter(watch_count, Target, {2, -1})
    end, Targets),
    {noreply, State}.

(c) Typing-debounce state machine

Erlang-flavored pseudocodetyping_debounce.erl — per (user, conversation)
%% states: idle -> typing -> refresh_wait -> expired -> idle
on_keystroke(idle) ->
    broadcast_to_watchers(typing_start),
    start_refresh_timer(RefreshMs),
    next_state(typing);

on_keystroke(typing) ->
    %% keystrokes while already 'typing' do NOT re-send — this is the debounce
    reset_local_idle_deadline(),
    next_state(typing);

on_refresh_timer_fire(typing) ->
    broadcast_to_watchers(typing_refresh),   %% one wire event per interval, not per key
    start_refresh_timer(RefreshMs),
    next_state(typing);

on_local_idle_deadline(typing) ->
    %% no explicit 'stopped typing' event required — sender simply stops
    %% refreshing; RECEIVER expires the indicator on its own timeout (§8)
    cancel_refresh_timer(),
    next_state(idle).

(d) Read-receipt batching / coalescing flush

Erlang-flavored pseudocodereceipt_batcher.erl — per conversation
on_message_read(ConvId, MsgId, State) ->
    Buffer = [MsgId | State#state.pending],
    case flush_timer_active(State) of
        true  -> State#state{pending = Buffer};   %% keep coalescing
        false ->
            start_flush_timer(BatchWindowMs),
            State#state{pending = Buffer}
    end;

on_flush_timer_fire(ConvId, State) ->
    case State#state.pending of
        []   -> ok;
        Ids  ->
            Receipt = #receipt{conv = ConvId, msg_ids = Ids, at = now()},
            %% ONE stanza for the whole batch, not one per message id
            privacy_filter:maybe_deliver(Receipt)
    end,
    State#state{pending = []}.
WHY THE ENGINEERS CHOSE RAW ETS OVER ANY DATABASE HERE

This is the Staff-level design instinct worth internalizing beyond this one chapter: match the durability guarantee to the actual cost of losing the data. A message has a near-infinite loss cost — losing one is a support ticket, a broken promise, occasionally worse — so it earns a durable, replicated, carefully-acked storage path (ch. 5). Presence has a near-zero loss cost — losing a subscription entry for a few seconds until the client reconnects and resubscribes is invisible to everyone involved — so it deserves a near-zero-cost storage path: no disk, no replication protocol, no consistency machinery, just process-local memory that a supervisor can throw away and rebuild on demand. Reaching for a database here would not make the system more correct; it would just make it slower and harder to operate, for a durability guarantee nobody asked for.

You can now derive why presence is the structural inverse of message delivery — write-once, fan-out-to-many, high-frequency, low-value-per-event — and why that asymmetry forces it entirely into RAM, entirely best-effort, and entirely disposable on process death. You can explain why subscribe-on-open beats roster broadcast, why typing-indicator expiry has to be client-side to self-heal, and why large-group read receipts hit an O(N²) wall that forces a hard cap.
