UBER ENGINEERING INTERNALS · CHAPTER 13

One Small App,
a Thousand Services Behind It

Chapters 8 and 9 gave the backend thousands of independently deployed services and a mesh that lets them call each other safely. None of that is directly reachable from a rider's or driver's phone — and it shouldn't be. This chapter derives, from first principles, why every mobile request instead crosses exactly one edge boundary, the API gateway, and why that gateway's hardest job isn't routing traffic — it's remembering, for months at a time, what every previous version of the app still believes the backend looks like.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

A phone is not a peer of the backend — it is a stranger you cannot redeploy

Every other service-to-service call in this course happens between two processes the same company controls, deployed from the same pipeline, on the same private network. A rider's phone breaks every one of those assumptions at once: it runs code the backend cannot update on demand, it talks over a public, lossy, high-latency cellular network, and it is, from the backend's point of view, an untrusted client exactly like any other internet caller.

Concretely, the mobile edge has to solve five problems that no internal service-to-service call in chapters 8–9 had to solve at all:

  • The client can't be redeployed on your schedule. An internal service's contract can change the moment both sides ship; a mobile app's contract is frozen the instant it's built, and stays frozen on some installs for months, regardless of what the backend does next.
  • One logical screen may need many backend services. Opening the app's home screen alone can require the rider's profile, nearby driver availability, an ETA, a promotions/pricing check, and active-trip state — up to five or more independent internal calls behind one visible screen.
  • The network itself is hostile. Cellular radios sleep and wake, tunnels and elevators cause total blackouts, and packet loss of a few percent is routine — nothing like the private, mostly-reliable network chapter 9's mesh was designed to run on.
  • Retries are unavoidable and dangerous. A phone that doesn't hear back from "request a ride" cannot tell if the request failed before or after the backend acted on it — and naively retrying a state-changing call risks requesting two trips instead of one.
  • Every internal service boundary must stay hidden. If the mobile app is allowed to know that "get fare" is served by the pricing service at some specific address, then chapter 8's whole point — being free to refactor internal service boundaries — is lost the day that knowledge ships inside an app binary you cannot force anyone to update.
WHY THIS ISN'T JUST "CHAPTER 9 AGAIN"

It's tempting to think the mobile edge is solved by the same sidecar/mesh machinery chapter 9 built for service-to-service calls. It isn't — for one structural reason: a sidecar assumes both ends of a call can be redeployed together by the same organization. The entire hard part of this chapter is a client the backend cannot touch after it ships. That single fact — not retries, not load balancing — is what forces a fundamentally different piece of infrastructure to exist at the edge.

DEEP CONTEXT · WHAT'S DOCUMENTED VS. WHAT THIS CHAPTER GENERALIZES

Uber has publicly discussed, at a high level, operating a large mobile-facing fleet with a long tail of old app versions still in the field, and the general pressure that put on its backend API surface as the service count grew (chapters 8 and 9's migration is the well-documented backdrop here). This chapter does not claim to know the exact current name, internal source code, or precise architecture of Uber's present-day edge gateway — that level of detail isn't public. What it teaches instead is the general API gateway / backend- for-frontend pattern: a single, versioned, mobile-facing contract that fans out to internal services server-side. That pattern is extremely well documented across the industry (Netflix's and Amazon's public writing on gateway and BFF patterns are the widely cited examples), and it is exactly what any company at Uber's scale, with Uber's shape of mobile client base, needs to build for the same reasons derived below.

ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: the app just calls each service it needs, directly

Start from the honest, obvious approach: the mobile app knows the addresses of the services it needs and calls each one over HTTPS, exactly the way an internal caller might before chapter 9's mesh existed.

pseudocoderider_app/home_screen.swift — the strawman, annotated
func loadHomeScreen() {
    // Four direct calls, straight to internal services, sequentially —
    // because that's the order the screen happens to need the data in.
    let profile  = http.get("https://profile-svc.internal/v1/me")
    let nearby   = http.get("https://dispatch-svc.internal/v1/nearby?loc=...")
    let eta      = http.get("https://eta-svc.internal/v1/estimate?loc=...")
    let promo    = http.get("https://promo-svc.internal/v1/active?rider=...")
    render(profile, nearby, eta, promo)
}

This is completely reasonable for a first version: it's simple, each team owns its own endpoint, and there's no extra infrastructure to build or operate. It also works fine in a demo, on office WiFi, with one app version installed on every test phone.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

The math of sequential cellular round-trips, and a promise the backend can't keep

Two independent failures, and either one alone would be disqualifying at Uber's real scale:

  • Latency is the sum of every round trip, on the worst network you have. A cellular round-trip is commonly on the order of 100–150 ms even on a healthy connection (versus low single-digit milliseconds on the private network chapter 9's calls run over) — and that's before TLS handshake overhead on a fresh connection to each distinct host. Four sequential calls at ~120 ms each is already ~480 ms of pure network time, before any service does a millisecond of work, and before a single retry. Add a fifth or sixth call as the home screen grows more features — routine over an app's lifetime — and the user is staring at a spinner for the better part of a second, on a good network.
  • The backend can never promise a stable set of addresses again. The instant the mobile app hardcodes dispatch-svc.internal, that hostname is now a public contract frozen inside an app binary. Every future internal refactor this course has described — splitting a service (chapter 8), renaming one, moving a capability behind a different owner — either breaks every installed copy of the app that hasn't updated, or requires the backend team to keep the old hostname alive, forever, as a compatibility shim, indefinitely duplicating the very deploy-coupling problem chapter 8 broke the monolith apart to escape.
THE NUMBERS THAT MATTER MOST: THE LONG TAIL

App Store and Play Store releases are opt-in and gradual — a new version typically reaches a majority of active devices only after days to weeks of staged rollout, and a real long tail of installs never updates until the device itself is replaced. That means at any given moment the backend is serving a genuine mixture of app versions spanning months of history, all simultaneously, all expecting whatever contract existed when they were built. A naive direct-call design has no way to serve five different contract shapes from five different backend states at once — every naive fix (branch server-side on app version, forever) is exactly the kind of permanent, uncoordinated special-casing chapter 9 rejected for internal calls, now happening at the one boundary the backend can least afford to get informally.

ARC 05 · BETTER SOLUTION

One edge boundary: the API gateway, aggregating and translating server-side

The fix follows directly from naming the two failures precisely: fan the calls out server-side, in parallel, on the fast private network — and give the mobile app a small, stable, versioned contract that the gateway is responsible for satisfying, regardless of which internal services or shapes currently sit behind it.

From four direct calls to one gateway callSTEP-BY-STEP

Two distinct capabilities fall out of this move, and it's worth naming them separately because they solve different halves of §4's problem: aggregation (one client call fans out to N internal calls in parallel, server-side, over the fast network) attacks the latency problem; version translation (the gateway adapts whatever the current backend produces into the exact shape a given app version was built expecting) attacks the compatibility problem. A gateway that only aggregated, without translating, would still break every time an internal contract changed underneath an old app version.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL HURTS

The gateway doesn't remove the cost — it concentrates it in one place, forever

  • It becomes the single hop 100% of mobile traffic must pass through. It can be horizontally scaled and made highly available, but it is still, logically, one chokepoint standing between every phone on Earth and the entire backend — an outage here is a total outage, in a way no single internal service's outage (contained by chapter 9's circuit breakers) usually is.
  • Version-translation logic accumulates and almost never gets deleted. Every old app version still active in the field needs its adapter kept working, for as long as that version has installs — and because of the long tail from §4, "as long as" can mean years. The gateway team inherits a permanently growing pile of translation logic for contracts nobody internally uses anymore.
  • It becomes a hidden dependency for every backend team's roadmap. A backend team that wants to change a response shape can't just ship — they must coordinate with whoever owns the gateway to add or update a translation adapter, turning what should be an internal, single-team change into a cross-team one.
  • It adds a real hop's worth of latency and serialization cost to every single request, forever — small per call, but paid by every mobile request that will ever be made, which is the overwhelming majority of all traffic the backend serves.
THE HONEST FRAME

Just like chapter 9's sidecar, the gateway doesn't make the underlying problem — a stranger you can't redeploy, on a hostile network — disappear. It moves the cost of handling that stranger into one place, owned by one team, instead of leaving it scattered across every backend service and every app version's ad-hoc workarounds. That's a genuinely good trade at Uber's scale of mobile traffic and app-version diversity. It is not free, and the final architecture below is built explicitly around managing these costs rather than pretending they don't exist.

ARC 08 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

Version translation, and idempotent writes over a flaky network

Two mechanisms do essentially all of the gateway's real work: an adapter layer that reshapes one backend response into whatever contract a given app version expects, and an idempotency-key protocol that lets a phone safely retry a state-changing request without risking a duplicate effect.

(a) Version detection and response translation

Every request carries the app version that sent it (a header, in practice). The gateway looks up which contract that version was built against and runs the current backend response through that version's adapter before replying:

One backend response, three app versions, three shapesSTEP-BY-STEP

(b) Idempotent writes: safe retries over a hostile network

A phone that requests a ride and hears nothing back — a dropped cellular connection, a timeout — cannot distinguish "my request never arrived" from "my request succeeded and only the response was lost." The fix: the client generates a unique idempotency key up front and sends it with every attempt of the same logical request; the gateway remembers keys it has already completed and returns the original result instead of re-executing:

pseudocode (illustrative)gateway/idempotency.py
def handle_write(request):
    key = request.headers["Idempotency-Key"]      # client-generated, e.g. a UUID
    cached = idem_store.get(key)
    if cached is not None:
        return cached                              # same key seen before — replay the ORIGINAL result

    result = execute_once(request)                # the real, effectful call — runs exactly once
    idem_store.set(key, result, ttl_seconds=86400)  # remembered for a bounded window
    return result

ARC 09 · TRADEOFFS

Four ways to bridge a phone and a fleet of services

APPROACHWHAT IT BUYSWHAT IT COSTS
Direct client-to-service calls (§3) zero extra infrastructure no gateway team dependency sequential cellular latency (§4) internal hostnames become a permanent public contract
Shared mobile client SDK one, consistent client-side implementation no new server infrastructure still frozen the instant the app is built — doesn't solve version skew every internal contract change still needs an SDK release
Central API gateway / BFF (this chapter's endpoint) server-side parallel fan-out version translation without an app update internal refactors stay invisible to clients single logical chokepoint for all mobile traffic translation-adapter debt accumulates indefinitely
GraphQL federation gateway client asks for exactly the fields it needs, aggregated server-side reduces some version-skew pain (unknown fields are simply not requested) a new query language and execution engine to operate still needs a real deprecation policy for fields old clients depend on

The pattern from chapter 9 repeats: no row is simultaneously infrastructure-free, instantly upgradeable, and free of a central chokepoint. Uber's real, publicly discussed pressure — a huge, geographically spread mobile fleet with a long tail of old versions, calling a backend built from thousands of independently deployed services — is exactly the situation that pushes an organization toward the third or fourth row, at real, ongoing cost.

ARC 10 · PERFORMANCE / SCALE CHARACTERISTICS

What a gateway buys back, quantified

Two numbers matter here: how much latency parallel server-side fan-out saves versus the naive sequential client calls from §4, and how that gap grows as a screen needs more backend calls.

  • Sequential mobile calls scale linearly with call count. N sequential cellular round-trips at ~120 ms each cost roughly N × 120ms — a screen needing 6 calls is pushing three quarters of a second before any actual work happens.
  • Parallel server-side fan-out scales with the slowest call, not the sum. Once the gateway issues all N internal calls concurrently over the low-latency private network, total latency is close to max(call times) + one mobile round trip instead of their sum — the mobile round-trip is paid exactly once, for the gateway call itself, regardless of how many internal calls it fans out to.

MODEL, NOT MEASUREMENT

The two curves are a first-principles arithmetic model — one round trip per call summed versus one round trip plus the slowest internal call, using a widely cited general cellular RTT figure — not a claimed measured Uber production number. The point is the shape: the gap between the two lines widens every time a screen needs one more backend call, which is exactly the direction real product features move over time.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS / CONTROVERSIES

Two failure patterns every mobile-facing gateway eventually meets

Uber has not published a detailed public postmortem naming a specific internal gateway incident, so this section teaches two realistic, industry-standard patterns this class of system is well known to produce across the industry, and says so plainly rather than inventing a specific internal Uber event.

Pattern 1: the retry stampede after a gateway blip

Mobile apps, especially older versions, often implement their own client-side retry logic on top of whatever the gateway itself does — sometimes aggressively, with short fixed intervals rather than the backoff-and-jitter discipline chapter 9 derived for internal calls. A brief gateway blip (a deploy, a spike in one dependency) can cause a large fraction of concurrently active devices to retry in a tight, synchronized window right as the gateway is recovering — the same retry-amplification math from chapter 9, except now the source is millions of phones the backend team does not control the code of, rather than services it does.

Pattern 2: the silent breakage of a long-tail version

A backend team changes an internal response shape, updates the gateway's current adapter, and ships — but forgets (or was never told) that an old, still-active app version's adapter assumed a field that no longer exists in the same form. Because the affected version is a small, easy-to-miss slice of total traffic, the breakage can go unnoticed in aggregate metrics for days, surfacing only as a spike in support complaints from a specific, older app-version cohort — a failure mode unique to the mobile edge's long tail, with no equivalent in chapter 9's all-internal service graph where every caller can simply be redeployed.

THE OPERATIONAL LESSON BOTH PATTERNS SHARE

Both incidents trace back to the same root cause named in §1: code the backend cannot redeploy. The fix in both cases is process, not just engineering — treating every version adapter as a real, tracked, tested contract with its own regression suite, and instrumenting metrics per app version, not only in aggregate, so a long-tail-only breakage is visible before it becomes a support fire.

ARC 13 · CODE WALKTHROUGH

Reading the gateway: illustrative reference pseudocode

Uber's exact internal gateway source isn't public. What follows is precise, representative pseudocode for how a system like this is actually built — labeled illustrative because it is a reconstruction of the well-documented general pattern, not a copy of proprietary source.

gateway/
├── edge/
│ ├── ● tls_terminator.py — TLS termination, auth-token validation
│ └── ● version_detector.py — reads client app-version header, resolves a contract id
├── core/
│ ├── ● router.py — maps a stable public endpoint to a fan-out plan
│ ├── ● fanout_orchestrator.py — issues internal calls concurrently, joins results
│ ├── ● idempotency.py — key store for safe write-retries (§8b)
│ └── ratelimiter.py — per-client and per-endpoint quota enforcement
├── adapters/
│ ├── ● v3_adapter.py — translates current shape → v3 app contract
│ ├── ● v4_adapter.py — translates current shape → v4 app contract
│ └── v5_adapter.py — current app contract; usually the identity transform
└── registry/contract_schemas.yaml — one schema definition per supported app-version range
pseudocode (illustrative)router.py + fanout_orchestrator.py
def handle_home_screen(request):
    version = detect_version(request)             # e.g. "v3.1", "v4.0", "v5.2"
    adapter = ADAPTERS[version]                     # resolved once per request

    # Fan out CONCURRENTLY on the private network — this is the whole latency win from §10.
    profile, nearby, eta, promo = await_all(
        profile_svc.get_me(request.rider_id),
        dispatch_svc.get_nearby(request.loc),
        eta_svc.estimate(request.loc),
        promo_svc.active_for(request.rider_id),
    )

    current_shape = assemble(profile, nearby, eta, promo)  # today's canonical internal shape
    return adapter.to_client_contract(current_shape)   # §8a: reshape for THIS app version
pseudocode (illustrative)adapters/v3_adapter.py
class V3Adapter:
    def to_client_contract(self, shape):
        # v3 app was built BEFORE "surge_multiplier" existed as its own field —
        # it expects fare adjustments folded into a single "fare_note" string.
        fare_note = None
        if shape.surge_multiplier > 1.0:
            fare_note = f"Prices are higher due to demand ({shape.surge_multiplier}x)"

        return {
            "driver_count": len(shape.nearby_drivers),   # v3 never learned about driver TYPES
            "eta_minutes": shape.eta_minutes,
            "fare_note": fare_note,               # synthesized — v3 has no surge_multiplier field at all
        }
WHY IT WAS BUILT THIS WAY — TWO CHOICES TO STEAL

1. Adapters translate OUT of one canonical shape, never re-derive it. Every internal service always produces its single current, best representation of the truth; every adapter's only job is reshaping that one truth for one contract. This keeps N adapters linear in complexity instead of letting each one reimplement its own aggregation logic. 2. The idempotency key lives in the gateway, not in each internal service. Exactly like chapter 9's decision to centralize retry/circuit-breaking logic in a sidecar rather than duplicating it per service, putting the client-retry safety net at the one edge boundary every mobile write passes through means every internal service can assume "this call only happens once" without implementing that guarantee itself.
