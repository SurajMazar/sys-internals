BLOCKCHAIN FUNDAMENTALS · CHAPTER 09

Proof of Stake &
Classical Byzantine Fault Tolerance

Chapter 8 priced proof-of-work's security in joules: hash rate is a scarce, external, physically rivalrous resource, and that scarcity is exactly what makes Sybil resistance and fork resolution work — which is also exactly why proof-of-work is expensive, hardware-centralizing, and tied to an issuance schedule that must eventually taper toward zero. This chapter asks the obvious next question — what else could serve as the scarce resource? — and follows the answer through two families of consensus that both reuse the idea: proof-of-stake, where the resource is capital already living inside the system, and classical Byzantine fault tolerance, the pre-blockchain lineage whose finality machinery proof-of-stake's "finality gadgets" quietly reimplement.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

Proof-of-work's bill comes due — what else could pay it?

Chapter 7 established the real requirement: an open, permissionless network needs Sybil resistance, and Sybil resistance requires making influence expensive to fabricate, because identities themselves are free. Chapter 8 picked computation — hashing — as the expensive thing, and derived Nakamoto consensus from that single choice. It is worth pricing that choice honestly before asking what else could have been picked:

  • Energy. Bitcoin's steady-state electricity draw is estimated by the Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance at roughly 100–150 TWh/year depending on the hardware mix and year — comparable to a mid-sized industrialized country's total consumption, spent on a computation whose entire purpose is to be hard, not to be useful.
  • Hardware centralization. Mining profitability collapses to whoever can buy the newest ASIC in bulk and site it next to the cheapest power, concentrating hash rate into a handful of manufacturers (Bitmain has historically dominated ASIC supply) and pools (Foundry USA and AntPool alone have, at various points, jointly commanded well over half of Bitcoin's hash rate) — the opposite of the "anyone with a laptop" story often told about mining's early years.
  • A security budget tied to issuance. Miners are paid in newly issued coins plus fees; issuance halves every 210,000 blocks (~4 years). Every halving cuts the nominal security budget unless price or fee revenue compensates — which makes "will fees alone ever secure the chain once issuance approaches zero?" a genuinely open, actively debated question, not a solved one.

None of these costs are accidents — they are the direct, physical price of using a rivalrous external resource as voting weight. So restate chapter 7's question one more time, more sharply: is there a resource that is (a) scarce, (b) cheap to verify someone holds, and — this turns out to be the property that actually matters — (c) something the protocol itself can take away if its holder misbehaves? Proof-of-work satisfies (a), and satisfies (b) only weakly (a solved hash is instantly checkable; the hash rate someone controls is only inferable by watching them over time). It categorically fails (c): there is no way to reach into the past and un-spend electricity. A resource that satisfies all three is sitting inside every one of these systems already — the very capital the chain exists to track.

THE PROPERTY THAT ACTUALLY MATTERS: FORFEITABILITY

In proof-of-work, punishing a lying miner is physically impossible — you cannot claw back joules already converted to heat and lost history's opportunity cost. In proof-of-stake, punishing a lying validator is a bookkeeping operation: the protocol decrements a number in its own ledger. That single difference — a resource that lives inside the system's own accounting, and can therefore be destroyed by the system's own rules — is what "proof of stake" means underneath the name. Section 5 turns this into the entire mechanism (slashing); sections 2 through 4 first show what goes wrong if you build proof-of-stake without it.

Concretely: a stake is a bonded, at-risk deposit of the chain's own native asset — locked into a deposit mechanism (on Ethereum, a smart contract on the execution layer; minimum 32 ETH per validator) that entitles the depositor to participate in consensus — propose blocks, vote on their validity — and earn rewards for correct participation, in exchange for accepting that specific, protocol-defined misbehavior will destroy some or all of the deposit. Everything else in this chapter is commentary on that one sentence.

ARC 02 · HISTORICAL CONTEXT

From "coin age" to a $100B chain finalizing without a single joule of proof

Proof-of-stake and classical BFT arrived at the same problem from opposite directions: proof-of-stake started inside cryptocurrency and had to import Byzantine fault tolerance's rigor over a decade; classical BFT was solved theory from the 1980s that blockchains eventually rediscovered they needed wholesale. DEEP CONTEXT · WHY "CLASSICAL" BFT?

Lamport, Shostak & Pease formalized the Byzantine Generals Problem in 1982, and Castro & Liskov's Practical BFT (1999) made it fast enough for real systems — both built for permissioned, small, known validator sets (a bank's replicated database, not an open p2p network). Chapter 6 already met the 3f+1 bound this work produced. What blockchains added forty years later wasn't new theory — it was the observation that if you already have a fixed, identity-bearing validator set (which proof-of-stake gives you, unlike proof-of-work's anonymous hash-rate free-for-all), you can run 1980s BFT directly on top of it and get its strongest property: deterministic, instant finality, not Nakamoto's probabilistic kind.

ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: just swap hash rate for stake

Derive it the way an engineer skimming chapter 8 would. Nakamoto consensus picks a block proposer probabilistically, weighted by hash rate, and a puzzle stops anyone from proposing more often than their share of hash rate allows. The one-line "obvious" replacement: pick a proposer probabilistically, weighted by stake, using public randomness instead of a puzzle — nobody can propose more often than their share of stake allows, because nobody can fake owning more coins than they own. No mining, no ASICs, no energy bill. Here is that idea, in the fewest lines that make it work at all:

Pythonnaive_pos.py — the strawman: same lottery, different ticket
def choose_proposer(validators, seed):
    # weighted-random pick, proportional to stake -- structurally identical
    # to picking a miner proportional to hashrate. Looks like a free win:
    # no energy, and you can't fake owning coins you don't have.
    ticket = hash(seed) % total_stake(validators)
    return weighted_pick(validators, ticket)

def on_new_block_seen(validator, block):
    # naive rule: sign whatever extends a chain you've seen, no questions asked
    if block.height > validator.last_signed_height:
        validator.sign(block)              # costs a signature: microseconds, ~0 joules
        broadcast(block.signed_by(validator))
    # NOTE: nothing here stops `validator` from ALSO signing a competing
    # block at the same height, on a different fork. There is no rule
    # against it anywhere in this file, and no penalty is defined.

This captures Sybil-resistance (you cannot vote with stake you don't hold) and it captures the low-energy property in one move. It also has a silent, structural hole visible right there in the second function: a valid block still needs a signature to prove a real validator approved it, and a signature is a few hundred bytes and microseconds of CPU regardless of which chain it signs. That non-rivalrous property — the exact thing that made stake attractive over hash rate — is what section 4 turns into a name.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

Nothing-at-stake: when voting is free, everyone votes for everything

Make the failure precise with expected value. A validator holding stake s sees two blocks, A and B, both extending the same parent — ordinary propagation-delay forks, exactly the kind chapter 10 shows happen on every fast chain with zero malice required. Signing a block that ends up canonical pays a fixed reward r; signing one that ends up orphaned pays nothing — but in this naive scheme, signing also costs nothing. Compare two strategies:

  • Sign only A: expected payoff = r · P(A wins).
  • Sign both A and B: expected payoff = r · P(A wins) + r · P(B wins) = r · 1 = r, always — because exactly one of the two forks eventually becomes canonical with certainty, signing both guarantees collecting the reward no matter which one wins.

Signing both weakly dominates signing one: it is never worse, and strictly better under any real uncertainty about the outcome. A rational, non-cooperative validator therefore signs every fork it ever sees — and so does every other validator reasoning the same way, independently, with no coordination required. The result: every fork accumulates close to 100% of the network's signing stake, simultaneously. Weight, in this scheme, isn't scarce — it's a number attached to a free action, and a free action gets taken everywhere there's a place to take it.

Contrast with proof-of-work, where hash rate is genuinely rivalrous: pointing 30 TH/s at chain A is 30 TH/s not pointed at chain B, so mining both forks halves your expected reward on each rather than doubling your guaranteed reward. Nakamoto's fork-choice rule (most accumulated work) works because "voting" for a fork consumes the very resource used to vote. Naive proof-of-stake's fork-choice rule has nothing to bite down on, because voting costs the voter nothing at all.

THE NUMBERS

With n validators and k simultaneous forks, naive proof-of-stake doesn't slow fork resolution by a factor of k — it removes fork resolution as a concept. Every one of the n validators' full stake weight applies to every one of the k branches at once, so the "heaviest chain" calculation returns the same enormous number for all k of them. This is not a rare edge case triggered only by an attacker; propagation-delay forks are routine on any reasonably fast chain (Bitcoin orphans roughly one block in a few hundred even under honest operation — chapter 10), so a naive proof-of-stake chain running this rule would fail to converge during completely ordinary operation. The failure's name, informally coined on Bitcointalk and formalized by Andrew Poelstra's 2014 essay "On Stake and Consensus," is nothing-at-stake — and it is precise: the problem is not that validators are malicious, it's that they have nothing at stake in choosing correctly.

ARC 05 · BETTER SOLUTION

Slashing: make the free action cost something

The fix is not a smarter fork-choice rule — no rule can distinguish honest replication from opportunistic double-signing when both produce the exact same artifact, a validly signed message. The fix is to change the payoff calculation from section 4 by making "sign both" no longer free. Attach a bonded deposit d to every validator, and add exactly one new protocol rule: if two conflicting signed messages from the same validator are ever published together, anyone may submit them as evidence, and the protocol destroys some or all of d.

Redo the comparison from section 4 with this rule in place. Signing only A: still r · P(A wins). Signing both A and B — where evidence is trivial to construct (it's just the two already-signed, already-public messages), so detection probability p ≈ 1: expected payoff ≈ r − p·d ≈ r − d. Deposits are set large relative to a single block's reward (Ethereum's is 32 ETH; a single correct attestation earns a tiny fraction of one ETH per year of validating), so r − d is deeply negative for any reasonable P(A wins) — "sign everything" stops being dominant and becomes dominated by "sign one thing and keep your deposit."

Notice exactly what did the work: not a bigger reward, not a cleverer selection rule, but converting a free action into an economically punishable one by attaching it to a resource the protocol itself can destroy — the forfeitability property from section 1, made mechanical.

WHY THE PENALTY SCALES WITH HOW MANY OTHERS GET CAUGHT

Ethereum's actual penalty is not a flat fee: an isolated validator slashed alone (the common real-world cause: a misconfigured backup validator signing the same key twice) loses a modest, near-symbolic amount, while dozens or hundreds slashed in the same window — the signature of a coordinated attack — lose a much larger fraction, up to the entire deposit, via a "correlation penalty" that scales with the fraction of the total validator set slashed simultaneously. The design goal: make an honest operational mistake survivable and a deliberate, capital-scale attack ruinously expensive, using the size of the violation as the very signal that distinguishes which one occurred.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL COSTS

The bill arrives twice: long-range history and operational complexity

Slashing punishes a validator for misbehaving while their stake is locked and at risk. That parenthetical is load-bearing. Follow a validator who withdraws their 32 ETH and exits the active set. The coins are no longer bonded; nothing the protocol can do touches them anymore. Signing an alternate version of history from before their exit — even an entirely fictitious chain forked from genesis — now costs this ex-validator literally nothing today, because there is no physical parallel to "having to redo all that work." Proof-of-stake's security comes from stake being locked and forfeitable right now, not from irreversible physical work sunk irretrievably into the past.

So an attacker who quietly accumulates old, no-longer-bonded private keys — bought, leaked, or simply never deleted by operators who assumed they no longer mattered — can construct a fully valid-looking alternate chain starting from some old checkpoint, complete with real signatures from the validator set that was actually active at that height. A new node syncing from genesis with no other input has no cryptographic way to tell this fabricated history from the real one: both are signed by the validator set the rules say was in charge at that point. This is the long-range attack. Contrast with proof-of-work: rewriting history there requires literally redoing the proof-of-work for every block — an unavoidable, physically unfakeable cost proportional to chain length times difficulty. Proof-of-stake's finality-by-signature has no such physical anchor once the signers' capital is no longer on the line.

Two fixes exist, and neither is a clean return to proof-of-work's guarantees:

  • Weak subjectivity (Buterin & Griffith, "Proof of Stake: How I Learned to Love Weak Subjectivity," 2014). A node offline longer than the weak-subjectivity period, or syncing for the first time, cannot safely determine the correct chain from the genesis block and the rules alone. It needs one extra piece of information from outside the protocol — a recent (block hash, epoch) checkpoint from a source it already trusts: a friend who was online, a block explorer, a client team's hardcoded default. With that anchor, everything forward from it verifies with ordinary signature checks; the anchor exists only to rule out an alternate history built on stale keys.
  • Finality gadgets (built precisely in §8). Once a checkpoint is finalized, honest clients treat it as permanently settled — not because reverting it is expensive in proof-of-work's sense, but because doing so provably requires at least one-third of all validators active at that point to violate an explicit slashing condition simultaneously. That is both economically suicidal for them individually and cryptographically detectable, so the actual backstop against a successful finality attack isn't "the honest chain wins automatically" — it's "the community coordinates a response that deletes the attackers' balances," a social/governance backstop layered on top of the cryptoeconomic one.
THE HONEST ADMISSION

This means proof-of-stake gives up the property that made Nakamoto's original pitch so clean: a brand-new node, given nothing but the genesis block and the rules, can independently verify the entire history of the chain using only its own CPU time — nothing to trust, nothing taken on faith. A proof-of-stake node needs a checkpoint from somewhere. Whether that is a fatal regression, or simply an honest formalization of an assumption Bitcoin users already make silently (essentially nobody verifies from genesis with zero external input either — they trust their client's hardcoded checkpoints, their OS vendor, their download mirror), is a genuinely disputed question in the field. This chapter will not pretend there's a clean answer. What isn't disputed: it is a different trust model, not a strictly worse or strictly better version of the same one.

The second cost is quieter but shows up in every incident report: operational complexity. Casper FFG plus LMD-GHOST plus committee sampling plus slashing detection plus the inactivity leak (§8) is a far larger spec surface than Nakamoto consensus's roughly page-long rule — longest valid chain wins. And because dozens of independent operators frequently run the same client software (Prysm has, at times, represented well over 40% of Ethereum's validators), a single logic bug can afflict a large fraction of the validator set at once, in a way structurally unlike proof-of-work's hardware diversity — a buggy ASIC firmware costs its owner revenue, not the network's liveness. Section 11's Medalla incident is this risk made concrete.

ARC 07 · FINAL ARCHITECTURE

Ethereum's constellation: two layers, one validator set, three mechanisms

The concrete, shipping answer to sections 1–6 is Ethereum post-Merge: an execution layer that still runs the EVM and holds account state exactly as before, wired via the Engine API to a consensus layer (the "beacon chain") that does nothing but decide, and finalize, whose blocks count. WHY SPLIT INTO TWO LAYERS AT ALL?

Because it makes the Merge — swapping Nakamoto consensus for Casper FFG + LMD-GHOST — a change that touches only "whose block counts," not "what a block does." Transactions, gas, the EVM, account balances: all untouched. The Engine API's entire job is a thin contract — the consensus layer tells the execution layer which payload is canonical; the execution layer tells the consensus layer whether that payload is valid — small enough that Ethereum shipped the biggest consensus change in its history as effectively invisible to application developers.

ARC 08 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

The actual mechanisms, precisely

Five pieces, in the order a slot actually uses them: (a) committees and the randomness that assigns them, (b) Casper FFG's justify/finalize vote, (c) LMD-GHOST's fork-choice walk, (d) the slashing conditions that make §5 enforceable, and (e) Tendermint's alternative — instant finality bought a different way — plus HotStuff's fix for its message cost.

(a) Committees: sampling instead of asking everyone, every time

Ethereum's active validator set has numbered in the hundreds of thousands. If every validator attested every slot to every other validator directly, message volume would be unworkable. Instead: once per epoch (32 slots, 12 seconds each, ≈6.4 minutes), the active set is pseudorandomly partitioned into committees, one or more assigned to each of the epoch's 32 slots, and every validator attests exactly once per epoch. Committees are floored at 128 members (TARGET_COMMITTEE_SIZE) — small enough to bound message volume, but deliberately not smaller: with fewer than roughly 128 samples, even an adversary holding a minority of total stake would have a non-negligible chance of a single unlucky random draw handing them a local majority within that one committee, by the hypergeometric tail bound. (b) Casper FFG: a two-phase vote over checkpoints, not blocks

A checkpoint is the pair (block root, epoch number) for the first slot of an epoch. Every attesting validator's vote each epoch includes an FFG link: (source checkpoint → target checkpoint). A link is justified once validators controlling ≥2/3 of total effective balance have voted for that exact link. A checkpoint is finalized once it is justified and the very next checkpoint is also justified via a direct link between them. Because that requires two full epochs' worth of supermajority attestation, the theoretical minimum time to finality is 2 epochs ≈ 12.8 minutes — commonly quoted as "about 15 minutes" once real-world attestation propagation slack is included. ACCOUNTABLE SAFETY — THE HEADLINE PROPERTY

For two conflicting checkpoints to both satisfy the 2/3 finalization rule, at least 1/3 of total stake must have attested to both — which, by construction, violates the double-vote slashing condition below for every validator in that overlap, provably and irrevocably. Casper FFG's actual security claim isn't "finality can never be violated" — it's "finality can never be violated without leaving unforgeable proof of exactly who did it," which is what makes the social-slash backstop from §6 actionable rather than hand-wavy.

(c) LMD-GHOST: the fork-choice rule between finalized checkpoints

Between checkpoints, someone still has to decide what to build on — that's a liveness question, separate from finality's safety question, and Ethereum answers it with a different rule entirely. Each validator's latest attestation (its most recent message — hence "LMD," latest-message-driven) casts a vote of weight equal to that validator's effective balance for a specific block. To find the canonical head: start at the last finalized checkpoint, then repeatedly walk to whichever child has the greatest total vote-weight in its subtree — the "greedy heaviest observed subtree" (GHOST) part — until reaching a leaf. Unlike Nakamoto's accumulated work, a vote here doesn't accumulate across blocks: a validator's old attestation is simply overwritten the moment it issues a new one, so LMD-GHOST reorganizes freely above the last finalized checkpoint and never below it — finality, not the fork-choice rule, is what makes old blocks immutable. (d) Slashing conditions: the two ways to equivocate

Two, and only two, conditions make an attestation pair slashable — both are exactly the shapes of lying that section 5's economics need to catch:

  • Double vote. The same validator casts two different attestations targeting the same epoch. This is the direct case from §4 — signing two competing forks at the same height.
  • Surround vote. One attestation's (source, target) range fully contains another one from the same validator: source₁ < source₂ < target₂ < target₁. This blocks a subtler attack: a validator can't first vote honestly, then later try to "erase" that vote by justifying a shorter span that contradicts it, or vice versa — either direction of surrounding is slashable.

(e) Tendermint: buying finality by never building without it

Ethereum decouples "what to build on" (LMD-GHOST) from "what's permanent" (Casper FFG). Tendermint — the classical-BFT-derived alternative used across the Cosmos ecosystem — refuses to draw that line at all: a block isn't proposed as the new head until it is already finalized. Every height runs one or more rounds:

A Tendermint round, step by stepSTEP-BY-STEP

The same 3f+1 bound from chapter 6 governs it exactly: with fewer than 1/3 of voting power Byzantine, two disjoint 2/3-precommit supermajorities for conflicting blocks at the same height is arithmetically impossible (two-thirds plus two-thirds exceeds the whole), so a committed block is safe the instant it commits — no reorganization, ever, by construction rather than by probability. The price shows up under partition: if fewer than 2/3 of voting power can hear each other, no round at any height can ever gather a precommit supermajority, so the chain halts entirely rather than producing a fork. Tendermint chooses safety over liveness, deliberately and unconditionally.

HotStuff (Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta & Abraham, 2018/2019) keeps Tendermint's safety bound but fixes its communication cost. Instead of every validator broadcasting votes to every other validator (all-to-all, the source of Tendermint's O(n²) message load), each validator sends its vote to a single leader, who aggregates 2/3 of them into one threshold-signed quorum certificate and broadcasts that one certificate back out — O(n) messages per round instead of O(n²). Pipelining the phases across consecutive blocks (each block's proposal doubles as the vote-carrier for the previous phase) then hides most of the latency this leader round-trip would otherwise add. This is the design Meta's Diem shipped as DiemBFT, and that Aptos runs in production today.

DELEGATED PROOF-OF-STAKE: THE THIRD FAMILY, BRIEFLY

EOS and (originally) Steem take a different path entirely: token holders vote for a small fixed number of block producers (EOS: 21) who then run something close to classical BFT among themselves — small n means cheap O(n²) messaging and sub-second finality. The tradeoff is concentrated, not distributed, trust: with only 21 seats, capturing a plurality of votes captures the chain's governance outright, which is precisely what happened to Steem in February 2020 (§11 and the timeline above) and is a standing, structural criticism of the model rather than a one-off incident.

ARC 10 · PERFORMANCE & SECURITY CHARACTERISTICS

What you can predict before running any of this in production

Energy, compressed to order of magnitude. The Ethereum Foundation's own post-Merge accounting put the network's energy draw roughly 99.95% below its pre-Merge, proof-of-work figure — the bars below are log-compressed on purpose, because a linear bar for proof-of-stake would round to a hairline:

proof-of-work — ~100–150 TWh/yr network-wide
PoS / BFT — a home server per validator

Bandwidth. BLS12-381 signature aggregation (chapter 4's curve math, reused) lets an entire 128+ member committee's individual attestations collapse into a single constant-size aggregate signature — bandwidth for attestations grows with the number of committees, not the number of individual validators inside them, which is exactly why Ethereum can scale validator count into the hundreds of thousands without attestation traffic scaling in step.

Message complexity is the sharpest, most quantifiable difference between the BFT family and Ethereum's sampling approach. Tendermint's all-to-all gossip costs every validator a message to every other validator, twice per round (prevote + precommit) — O(n²). HotStuff's leader-aggregated quorum certificate costs O(n). Ethereum sidesteps the question by never letting any single round's messaging depend on total validator count at all — committees cap it. Drag the validator count and watch the curves separate:

Attack cost is where forfeitability shows up one more time. A proof-of-work 51% attack costs whatever it takes to rent or acquire enough hash rate — and every joule of that spend is gone whether the attack succeeds or not, but the hardware remains fully valuable and reusable afterward. A proof-of-stake attack big enough to threaten liveness (~1/3 of stake) or finality (~1/3, provably, per §8's accountable-safety property) requires acquiring a comparable fraction of the total staked value on the open market — and using it destroys most of that capital's value twice over: buying it visibly moves the market (signaling the attack before it happens), and actually executing it triggers slashing that burns the very asset spent to mount it. Proof-of-work's attack tool survives the attack; proof-of-stake's does not.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS

Three times this chapter's mechanics were tested in production

Incident 1: Medalla testnet's four-day finality collapse (August 2020)

Ethereum's largest public proof-of-stake testnet, Medalla, stopped finalizing for roughly four days. Root cause: a leap-second-adjacent NTP time discrepancy interacted with a bug in Prysm — at the time the dominant client, run by a large majority of testnet validators — causing a mass simultaneous crash. With well under 2/3 of validators able to attest, Casper FFG's justification threshold was unreachable epoch after epoch, and the inactivity leak (§8's fifth mechanism, gradually reducing offline validators' effective balance until the online minority alone regains 2/3 of the shrinking total) began grinding down every offline validator's balance for days. Nobody's funds were stolen and no invalid block was ever finalized — the system did exactly what it was designed to do under mass unavailability — but it was the first large-scale, real demonstration that client diversity is not a footnote in proof-of-stake design, it's a load-bearing security assumption. Client teams responded by actively campaigning against any single client exceeding a "danger zone" market share ever since.

Incident 2: mainnet finality delay, May 11–12, 2023

A bug in a subset of Prysm and Teku nodes (the two together represented a large share of mainnet validators) caused affected nodes to briefly disagree on the correct head, delaying finalization by roughly 25 minutes before self-resolving as the affected validators recovered. No funds were at risk and no fork was finalized incorrectly, but the incident is a clean, small-scale echo of Medalla: even years after the Merge, a client-level bug — not an attacker — is still the most realistic near-term threat to Ethereum's finality guarantee, precisely because finality's 2/3 threshold has no tolerance for "most nodes agree" the way probabilistic finality does.

Incident 3: the Steem hostile takeover, February 2020

Justin Sun's Tron Foundation acquired Steemit Inc., which held a large pre-mined stake, and combined that stake with tokens borrowed from exchanges (Binance, Huobi, Poloniex) that custodied user deposits — voting all of it in a single coordinated block to replace Steem's elected witnesses with Sun-aligned candidates. This is not a bug; it is delegated proof-of-stake's centralization risk executing exactly as its critics described: a small, electable validator set means a plurality of votable stake — not necessarily honestly-held stake — can capture governance outright, and using exchange-custodied user funds to do it sidesteps the usual assumption that large holders act in their own long-term interest. The community's response was social, not cryptographic: a hard fork to Hive that simply left the captured chain (and Sun's acquired stake) behind — a real-world instance of the same "social backstop" this chapter invokes for long-range and finality attacks, deployed here against a governance attack instead.

def process_justification_and_finalization(state):
    # One FFG vote per attester per epoch; tally the total effective
    # balance that voted for the link (previous_checkpoint -> this_checkpoint).
    total_balance = get_total_active_balance(state)
    prev_epoch_voted = get_attesting_balance(state, state.previous_epoch_attestations)
    curr_epoch_voted = get_attesting_balance(state, state.current_epoch_attestations)

    # A link is justified once >= 2/3 of ALL active stake voted for it --
    # not 2/3 of participants, 2/3 of BALANCE. Whales and small holders
    # count by capital, exactly like the fork-choice weight in get_head().
    if prev_epoch_voted * 3 >= total_balance * 2:
        state.previous_justified_checkpoint = state.current_justified_checkpoint
        # two DIRECTLY consecutive justified checkpoints -> the earlier one finalizes
        if is_consecutive(state.previous_justified_checkpoint, state.current_justified_checkpoint):
            state.finalized_checkpoint = state.previous_justified_checkpoint
    if curr_epoch_voted * 3 >= total_balance * 2:
        state.current_justified_checkpoint = get_checkpoint(state, get_current_epoch(state))
Pythonfork_choice.py — get_head(), the LMD-GHOST walk
def get_head(store):
    head = store.finalized_checkpoint.root      # never walk below finality
    while True:
        children = get_children(store, head)
        if not children:
            return head                      # leaf reached -- this is the canonical head
        # weight = sum of effective balances of every validator whose LATEST
        # attestation votes for a block inside this child's subtree
        head = max(children, key=lambda c: get_weight(store, c))
Pythonslashing.py — is_slashable_attestation_data()
def is_slashable_attestation_data(a, b):
    # same validator, same target epoch, different data -- a double vote
    double_vote = (a != b) and (a.target.epoch == b.target.epoch)
    # one attestation's (source, target) range fully contains the other's
    surround = (a.source.epoch < b.source.epoch and b.target.epoch < a.target.epoch) \
            or (b.source.epoch < a.source.epoch and a.target.epoch < b.target.epoch)
    return double_vote or surround
Pythonconsensus_state.py — a Tendermint round, from Kwon & Buchman
def run_round(height, round_num, validators):
    proposer = validators.proposer_for(height, round_num)   # round-robin, weighted by stake
    block = proposer.propose() if proposer.is_me() else wait_for_proposal(timeout=PROPOSE_TIMEOUT)

    if block and is_valid(block):
        broadcast(Prevote(height, round_num, block.hash))
    else:
        broadcast(Prevote(height, round_num, None))               # nil prevote

    # a "polka": >= 2/3 of voting power prevoted the SAME block
    polka = wait_for_supermajority(Prevote, height, round_num, timeout=PREVOTE_TIMEOUT)
    if polka.block:
        lock(polka.block); broadcast(Precommit(height, round_num, polka.block.hash))
    else:
        broadcast(Precommit(height, round_num, None))

    commit = wait_for_supermajority(Precommit, height, round_num, timeout=PRECOMMIT_TIMEOUT)
    if commit.block:
        return finalize(commit.block)                    # DONE. absolute. no reorg, ever.
    return run_round(height, round_num + 1, validators)   # no quorum in time -> new round, same height
WHY IT WAS BUILT THIS WAY — THE ONE LESSON TO STEAL

Ethereum's split between get_head() (liveness — always produces some answer, even mid- partition) and process_justification_and_finalization() (safety — only ever advances on real supermajority proof) is a general pattern: don't conflate "what should I provisionally act on" with "what can never be undone." Tendermint deliberately collapses that distinction — its fork choice is its finality rule, because it refuses to act at all without a supermajority first — which buys enormous conceptual simplicity and forces the halt-under-partition tradeoff as a direct consequence. Neither choice is wrong; they are the same lesson from chapter 6's CAP discussion, applied to two different points on the consistency/ availability line.
