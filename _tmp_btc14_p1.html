ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

A settlement layer cannot also be a payment network

Take the number from chapter 7 seriously: a 4,000,000 weight-unit block every ~10 minutes, with a typical SegWit transaction costing roughly 140–250 weight units, caps the base chain at on the order of 7 transactions per second, averaged — the figure the Blockchain Fundamentals course's scaling chapter (chapter 12 there) derives from the same block-weight arithmetic applied to any UTXO chain with a fixed block budget. That is not a bug to be patched; it is the direct, arithmetic consequence of the design chapter 6 committed to: every full node validates every transaction, so the network's throughput is bounded by what the median validating node can absorb, not by what a datacenter could process. Raise the limit and you raise the hardware bar to run a node — chapter 12's trilemma again — you do not escape the tradeoff, you relocate it.

Compare that ceiling to what "being a payment network" actually requires. Visa's public figures describe sustained processing in the low thousands of transactions per second with burst capacity far higher; a single large payment processor's Tuesday afternoon exceeds Bitcoin's entire weekly capacity at 7 tx/s. The gap is not 10%, it is several orders of magnitude, and no plausible block-size increase closes it without concentrating validation in a handful of well-provisioned datacenters — which is a different, centralized system wearing Bitcoin's name.

So the requirement has to change shape. You cannot make the settlement layer faster without abandoning the property (independent validation by ordinary hardware) that makes it worth using. What you can do is reduce how often two parties need to touch it. Formally: find a construction where the chain records O(1) transactions per relationship — one to open, one to close — no matter how many times the parties pay each other in between, while every intermediate state remains as enforceable as if it had been written to the chain directly. That is the exact engineering target this chapter derives a solution for, and "as enforceable as if written to the chain" is doing all the work: it is the difference between an IOU and a payment.

THE SHAPE OF THE ANSWER, STATED EARLY

Every off-chain scaling construction in Bitcoin — payment channels, channel factories, statechains — follows the same pattern: put funds under a control condition on-chain once, then let the controlling parties agree to update who could claim those funds if the agreement were enforced right now, without touching the chain per update. The entire engineering difficulty is making "if it were enforced right now" airtight against a party who later prefers an older agreement. Sections 3–6 derive exactly why that is hard and what it costs to fix.

DEEP CONTEXT · WHY MALLEABILITY WAS THE BLOCKER, NOT THE MOTIVATION

Chapter 5 derived transaction malleability: before BIP143, a third party (or a careless wallet) could alter a transaction's witness data and change its txid without invalidating its signature. A channel construction that pre-signs transactions spending a not-yet-confirmed funding output is fatally exposed to this — if the funding transaction's txid changes after you've built refund/commitment transactions against it, those transactions now reference a nonexistent outpoint. This is the real reason Lightning stayed a research idea for two years after Satoshi's original sketch: the cryptography of channels was tractable in 2013, but the base layer's own transaction format had a bug that made building on it unsafe. SegWit (chapter 12) fixed the base layer; only then could the channel layer ship.
