Advanced Networking:
Transit Gateway, Direct Connect & Hybrid Connectivity

Every SAP-C02 candidate eventually meets a scenario with more than a handful of VPCs and a data center that refuses to disappear. This chapter is about the exam's favorite networking trap — VPC peering that quietly stops scaling — and its two real fixes: Transit Gateway for the VPC-to-VPC mesh problem, and the Direct Connect vs Site-to-Site VPN decision for everything that has to leave AWS and touch somewhere else.

ARC 01 · EXAM CONTEXT

Where this sits on the SAP-C02 blueprint

This chapter is Professional-level content, and it maps to two of the four SAP-C02 domains at once — which is unusual and worth noticing: Design for New Solutions (29% of the exam, the single largest domain) and Design Solutions for Organizational Complexity (26%). Networking-at-scale questions live at the intersection of both: a "new solution" domain question might hand you a greenfield multi-VPC design and ask how the VPCs should talk to each other; an "organizational complexity" question hands you an existing sprawl of accounts and asks you to fix it without a rebuild. Transit Gateway and Direct Connect show up in both flavors.

Quick exam-logistics refresher, stated as fact because it's stable: SAP-C02 is 75 questions (65 scored + 10 unscored), 180 minutes, multiple-choice and multiple-response, pass mark 750/1000, exam fee $300 at the time of writing (AWS does revise fees — treat that number as a snapshot, not a guarantee). You need 2+ years of hands-on AWS design/implementation experience to be well-prepared, and no prerequisite certification is formally required, though passing SAA-C03 first (chapters 1–15 of this course) is the practical on-ramp almost everyone takes.

One more framing point before the mechanics: VPC peering itself is Associate-level content (you met the basics back in chapter 3's VPC fundamentals). What makes this chapter Professional-level is what happens after peering — the organizational-scale failure mode, and the hybrid-connectivity decision layered on top of it. If peering felt simple at Associate level, that's correct; it's the scaling behavior that's the trap.

ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"Just peer it" and "Direct Connect is private, so it's secure"

Two instincts get candidates into trouble here, and both are reasonable-sounding extrapolations from something that is true at small scale.

Instinct #1 — keep adding VPC peering connections as the org grows. Peering worked fine for VPC A talking to VPC B. When VPC C shows up, you peer C to A and C to B. When D, E, and F show up, you peer each new VPC to every VPC it needs to reach. It's the natural move because each individual peering connection is simple, free of extra hops, and easy to reason about in isolation. The instinct fails to ask a scaling question: what does this look like at 20 VPCs, or 50, across a few dozen AWS accounts in an Organization?

Instinct #2 — "the scenario says encrypted, and Direct Connect is a private dedicated connection, so Direct Connect satisfies it." This one is seductive because "private" and "encrypted" feel like synonyms in everyday language — a private road and an encrypted tunnel both sound like "not for the public internet." The exam relies on candidates not distinguishing between who can physically access the medium (Direct Connect: nobody but you and your carrier/AWS, over a dedicated circuit) and whether the bits on that medium are cryptographically protected (Direct Connect: no, not unless you add something). Both instincts are explored — and broken — in the next section.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

The mesh math, the transitivity trap, and the encryption gap

The full-mesh math

VPC peering connections are pairwise. If you want every VPC in a group to be able to reach every other VPC directly, you need a peering connection for every pair — and the number of pairs among N items is the classic combinatorics identity N(N − 1) / 2. That is quadratic growth against a linear input, and it gets ugly fast:

Every one of those connections is a separate resource to create, a separate pair of route table entries to maintain on both sides, and a separate thing to audit when someone asks "can VPC 14 reach VPC 37." At 50 VPCs you are not managing a network anymore — you're doing configuration-management archaeology. Section 10 turns this into a live, slider-driven chart so you can feel the curve, not just read it.

The transitivity trap

Here is the fact the exam tests hardest: VPC peering is genuinely non-transitive. If VPC A peers with VPC B, and VPC B peers with VPC C, A cannot reach C through B — full stop, no configuration option turns this on. Peering connections are a direct routing relationship between exactly two VPCs; VPC B's route table can send B's own traffic to C, but it will not forward A's traffic through itself to reach C, even though B has routes to both. If a scenario needs A to reach C, A and C need their own peering connection — which is exactly why the full-mesh math above is unavoidable as long as you're using peering alone. This isn't a limitation someone forgot to lift; it's a deliberate simplicity/security tradeoff in how peering was designed, and it's precisely the thing Transit Gateway exists to fix (section 4).

THE TRAP, STATED THE WAY THE EXAM STATES IT

"VPC A is peered with VPC B. VPC B is peered with VPC C. A user in VPC A reports they cannot reach a resource in VPC C. What is the cause?" The tempting wrong answers involve route tables, security groups, or NACLs. The right answer is structural: no peering relationship connects A and C, and none of A's or C's configuration can create one — you'd need to either add a direct A↔C peering connection, or replace the whole design with a Transit Gateway hub that both A and C attach to.

Direct Connect's encryption gap

Direct Connect gives you a dedicated, private physical or logical connection from your premises into AWS — typically via a colocation facility or an AWS Direct Connect Partner, terminating on Amazon's network edge. That connection is private in the access-control sense: it's not routed over the public internet, and nobody outside your dedicated circuit (or VLAN, for a hosted connection) can put traffic on it. None of that means the traffic on the wire is encrypted. Direct Connect is a Layer 2/3 private link with BGP peering — by default it carries your packets in the clear, the same way a private leased line has always worked. If a scenario states encryption-in-transit as a requirement and you're using Direct Connect, the Direct Connect connection alone does not satisfy that requirement. The fix — covered in depth in section 4 and the simulator in section 15 — is to run a VPN (IPsec) session over the Direct Connect private virtual interface, or use MACsec on the subset of Direct Connect locations/ports that support it. Site-to-Site VPN, by contrast, is encrypted by construction: it's an IPsec tunnel, so "VPN" and "encrypted" really are close to synonyms.

ARC 04 · THE CORRECT APPROACH

Building the hub: from a mesh to a star

The fix for the mesh problem is to stop connecting VPCs to each other and start connecting them all to one thing: a Transit Gateway — a regional, managed router that VPCs, VPNs, and Direct Connect connections all attach to as spokes. Instead of N(N−1)/2 relationships, you get N attachments. Step through how a real hub-and-spoke design gets built, in the order you'd actually do it.

WHAT YOU JUST BOUGHT

Every spoke now has exactly one relationship to manage — its attachment to the hub — instead of one relationship per peer. Adding VPC #51 to a 50-VPC design is one new attachment and a route table association, not 50 new peering connections. The tradeoff, covered honestly in section 6: the Transit Gateway itself becomes a critical shared resource, its route tables become the new thing you must get right, and — the trap many candidates miss — peering's non-transitivity doesn't magically vanish just because a Transit Gateway exists somewhere in the account. Two VPCs that are only peered to each other (not attached to the TGW) still can't reach a third VPC through that peering, TGW or no TGW.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

What the correct approach still has to respect

A Transit Gateway fixes the mesh-math problem. It does not repeal the rest of AWS networking's physics. Keep these edge cases in your head — the exam loves testing whether you know the fix has limits too:

  • Non-transitivity survives, TGW or no TGW. If two VPCs are connected only through a plain VPC peering connection (not through Transit Gateway attachments), that pair is exactly as non-transitive as it always was. Adding a Transit Gateway elsewhere in the account changes nothing about that specific pair unless you deliberately re-point both VPCs at the TGW as spokes instead.
  • Overlapping CIDR blocks block the whole idea. Two VPCs with overlapping IP ranges cannot be peered, and cannot both be usefully attached to the same Transit Gateway route table (the TGW can't tell which VPC an overlapping destination belongs to). This is a design-time constraint, not something you patch later — plan non-overlapping CIDRs across every VPC that might ever need to talk to another one, including ones that don't exist yet.
  • Direct Connect has real lead time. Ordering a dedicated connection involves a Letter of Authorization and Connecting Facility Assignment (LOA-CFA), physical cross-connect scheduling at a colocation facility, and — for a brand-new physical presence — potentially construction. Total elapsed time is commonly measured in weeks, and can run longer if the facility or carrier has scheduling backlogs. Site-to-Site VPN, by contrast, is provisioned entirely in software in minutes to hours once your customer gateway is ready. This asymmetry is the single most exam-relevant fact in the whole Direct-Connect-vs-VPN comparison.
  • Transit Gateway attachment and route-table scaling is generous but not infinite. AWS documents per-account, per-region quotas for things like attachments per Transit Gateway and routes per route table; these numbers are the kind that AWS revises upward over time, so don't memorize a specific figure — the shape to remember is that they scale roughly linearly with attachment count (matching the hub-and-spoke's N growth), not quadratically the way a full mesh would. Check the current Service Quotas console for the exact current ceiling if a scenario is pushing hundreds of attachments.
  • Per-attachment and per-tunnel bandwidth are not unlimited either. A single VPN tunnel has a throughput ceiling that's commonly cited around the neighborhood of 1.25 Gbps (verify the exact current figure in AWS docs — this is exactly the kind of number that shifts). If a workload needs more than one tunnel's worth of throughput, Transit Gateway VPN attachments support ECMP (equal-cost multi-path) across multiple tunnels to add aggregate bandwidth — a detail worth knowing before you conclude "VPN can't handle this throughput, it must be Direct Connect."
ARC 07 · REFERENCE ARCHITECTURE

A realistic multi-region hub-and-spoke

This is the shape a SAP-C02 "design it" question is actually drawing toward: two regional hubs, each fronted by its own Transit Gateway, spoke VPCs attached to each hub (including a shared-services VPC that every other spoke needs — think directory services, package mirrors, central logging), Direct Connect as the primary hybrid link with Site-to-Site VPN standing by as an automatic backup, and the two regional hubs peered to each other so a workload in Region A's spokes can reach Region B's spokes without needing its own hybrid link. WHY "SHARED-SERVICES VPC" KEEPS SHOWING UP ON THE EXAM

Centralizing anything every spoke needs — a directory service, an NTP/package mirror, a central logging or inspection VPC — into one spoke that every other spoke can route to via the hub is the hub-and-spoke pattern's real payoff beyond just "fewer connections." It's also why Transit Gateway route tables (not just attachments) matter: you typically want every spoke to reach shared-services, but you do not want every spoke to reach every other spoke by default — that's a routing-table design decision, not an attachment decision, and it's exactly the kind of nuance SAP-C02 tests.

WHAT THE COMMITMENT ACTUALLY BUYS AND COSTS

Never anchor on a specific dollar figure for Direct Connect — it has a genuinely different cost structure than VPN (typically port-hour charges plus data-transfer pricing, often tied to a commitment), and that structure changes over time. What's stable and worth stating: Direct Connect trades upfront provisioning time and a commitment-shaped cost model for consistent, predictable throughput and latency that doesn't compete with the rest of the internet's traffic. VPN trades that consistency for pay-as-you-go simplicity and minutes-not-weeks provisioning. Section 9's decision table makes this concrete.

THE PAIRING THE EXAM WANTS YOU TO REACH FOR

The single highest-value pattern to have memorized: Direct Connect as primary + Site-to-Site VPN as automatic backup for any business-critical hybrid workload. AWS explicitly supports this as a resilience pattern — the VPN tunnel rides the public internet and only carries traffic if the DX circuit is down, giving you a failover path that doesn't depend on the same physical infrastructure as the primary link.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three vignettes, exam-phrased (original, not from any real exam)

These are original scenario constructions written in the same style the real exam uses — a company profile, a constraint, and a "which approach" question. None of these are claimed to be actual exam questions.

Case 1 — the retailer with forty VPCs

Profile: A retail chain's cloud team let each business unit spin up its own VPC over three years. They're now at 40 VPCs across a dozen accounts, connected by roughly 90 hand-created peering connections, and nobody is fully confident which VPCs can reach which others. A new fraud-detection service needs to read from a dozen of those VPCs. Constraint: Minimize new peering connections; must be manageable by a small platform team going forward. Which approach? Migrate to a Transit Gateway hub: attach the VPCs that need cross-VPC reach as spokes, retire the peering connections they replace, and use TGW route tables to control exactly which spokes can see the fraud-detection VPC — one attachment per VPC instead of one connection per pair, going forward.

Case 2 — the bank with six weeks and a compliance mandate

Profile: A financial services firm needs hybrid connectivity from a new trading-adjacent workload to an on-premises risk system, live in six weeks for a regulatory deadline. Compliance requires encryption-in-transit on every link touching this workload, and the data volumes are moderate (not massive). Constraint: Six-week hard deadline; encryption is non-negotiable. Which approach? Site-to-Site VPN. Direct Connect's provisioning lead time (commonly weeks, sometimes longer) makes it a real risk against a six-week deadline even if ordered today, and VPN is encrypted by default with no extra overlay needed — it satisfies both constraints without betting the deadline on a physical circuit installation.

Case 3 — the media company moving large backup volumes continuously

Profile: A media company continuously ships large video-archive backups from an on-premises SAN to S3, sustained near-line-rate, with no hard deadline on when the link needs to be live — it's a planned infrastructure project, not an emergency. Encryption-in-transit is not a stated requirement for this particular archival workload. Constraint: Needs consistent high throughput that shouldn't compete with general internet traffic; deadline is flexible. Which approach? Direct Connect. The throughput/consistency requirement and the flexible timeline both point away from VPN's public-internet path and toward a dedicated circuit; because this is still a production-critical pipeline, pair it with a Site-to-Site VPN as an automatic backup path rather than leaving a single physical connection as the only route.

aws ec2 create-transit-gateway-route-table --transit-gateway-id tgw-0abc123

WHY THE EXAM CARES ABOUT THIS SYNTAX

SAP-C02 scenario questions frequently show you a fragment of what was configured — "a private VIF was created with these parameters" — and ask what's missing or wrong. Recognizing that create-private- virtual-interface has no encryption-related parameter at all is the whole question: there's nothing to misconfigure, because Direct Connect was never carrying encryption in the first place. Similarly, recognizing that create-transit-gateway-vpc-attachment is called once per VPC (not once per pair) is the fastest way to identify a Transit Gateway design versus a peering design in a CLI exhibit.

GATE · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

Before chapter 18: the five facts most likely to be tested

If you remember nothing else from this chapter, remember these. First, VPC peering is genuinely non-transitive — A peered with B and B peered with C never implies A can reach C, and no configuration changes that. Second, full-mesh peering needs N(N−1)/2 connections for N VPCs; Transit Gateway needs N attachments — that quadratic-versus-linear gap is the entire justification for the hub-and-spoke redesign. Third, Direct Connect does not encrypt traffic by default — it's a private dedicated connection, not an encrypted tunnel, and a stated encryption-in-transit requirement over DX means adding a VPN overlay or MACsec, not relying on DX alone. Fourth, Direct Connect and Site-to-Site VPN are the exam's classic hybrid-connectivity pair: DX for consistent high-throughput/low-latency workloads with weeks of lead time to spare, VPN for lower-throughput or urgent connectivity that needs to exist in minutes and comes encrypted by default. Fifth, Direct Connect Gateway lets one physical connection reach multiple VPCs and regions — it extends DX's reach, but it doesn't change DX's throughput ceiling or its lack of default encryption.
