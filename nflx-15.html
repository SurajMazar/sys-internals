NETFLIX STREAMING INTERNALS · CHAPTER 15

Chaos Engineering:
Chaos Monkey & the Simian Army

Chapter 14 built circuit breakers, bulkheads, and graceful degradation on the theory that they would keep playback alive when something failed. Theory is not proof. This chapter covers the real, famous practice Netflix originated to close that gap: deliberately, randomly killing production instances during business hours — on purpose, continuously — specifically so that "something failed" stops being a rare emergency and becomes a routine, well-rehearsed event. It is also a chapter about discipline: this only works, and is only safe, once chapter 14's real reliability patterns already exist to catch the failures you're about to cause.

ARC 15 · THE PROBLEM

A resilience pattern you haven't tested is a hypothesis, not a guarantee

Chapter 14 derived circuit breakers, bulkheads, and graceful degradation from first principles, and the arguments for why they should work were sound. But "should work, on paper, based on a design document" and "actually works, verified against real production conditions" are different claims — and the gap between them is exactly where confident engineering teams get surprised. A fallback path that has never actually been exercised in production, a bulkhead sized from a back-of-envelope estimate that never got load-tested against real traffic, a breaker threshold tuned against synthetic data — every one of chapter 14's patterns could be silently broken, and a design review will not catch that. Only running the actual failure will.

At Netflix's real scale — thousands of virtual machine instances running continuously on a cloud provider's infrastructure — hardware and infrastructure failures are not a hypothetical edge case scheduled for "someday." They are a routine, statistically certain, background condition of operating at that scale. The engineering question this chapter answers is not "how do we prevent instances from ever failing" (a losing battle against physics and probability) but "how do we find out, on our own terms, before a real customer-facing outage finds out for us, whether our systems actually tolerate the failures we already know are coming."

  • Postmortem-driven learning only teaches you about failures you've already survived. Waiting for real incidents to reveal resilience gaps means your hard-won lessons are always paid for with a real, uncontrolled outage first.
  • Staging environments don't have production's scale, traffic shape, or configuration drift. A fallback path that works perfectly against staging's clean test data can fail in ways that only appear under real production load and real, messy account data.
  • Confidence without verification is a liability, not an asset. A team that believes its system is resilient because the design document says so, and has never actually tested that belief, is arguably worse off than a team that knows it's fragile — at least the second team knows what to fix.
THE REFRAME THIS CHAPTER MAKES

Instead of treating instance failure as an exceptional event to be prevented, treat it as a routine, expected background condition to be tolerated — and then prove that tolerance continuously, in production, on purpose, rather than assuming it. That reframe, formalized and open-sourced, is exactly what Netflix calls Chaos Monkey.

DEEP CONTEXT · WHY "MONKEY"?

The name is deliberately vivid: the tool behaves like a wild animal let loose in a data center, randomly unplugging machines. That mental image was itself an engineering communication choice — a name boring enough to sound like ordinary infrastructure tooling would not have conveyed the same "we do this on purpose, all the time, and you should build for it" cultural message to every engineer at the company. The name became load-bearing for the discipline it named, spreading industry-wide well beyond Netflix.

ARC 15 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: test resilience in staging, then hope

The conventional approach to verifying resilience, before chaos engineering existed as a named discipline, looked like this:

pseudocoderesilience_verification.py — the strawman, annotated
def verify_resilience(service):
    staging_env = provision_staging_environment()      # smaller, cleaner, no real traffic
    simulate_dependency_failure(staging_env, service)   # a manually written, one-off test
    if check_fallback_worked(staging_env):
        return "Resilient — ship it."
    # ... and then, separately and reactively:
    def on_real_production_incident(incident):
        write_postmortem(incident)
        fix_the_specific_thing_that_broke(incident)      # learn only what broke, only after it broke

This is the honest, reasonable-sounding default — most engineering organizations before Netflix's public writing on this topic worked exactly this way, and many still do. It is not indefensible: staging tests catch real bugs, and postmortems produce real fixes. The question, as always in this course, is precisely where this design's assumptions break down at real scale.

ARC 15 · WHY IT FAILS

The arithmetic of failure at thousands of instances

Do the numbers honestly, the way this course always does. Suppose a single cloud virtual machine instance has a very good, very ordinary annual failure rate — hardware issues, host maintenance, transient cloud provider issues together giving, say, a 0.5% chance of an unplanned termination on any given day (a deliberately conservative, illustrative figure — real cloud failure rates are not published by providers with this precision, and the exact number is not the point).

THE NUMBERS

At a single instance, a 0.5%-per-day failure rate feels almost negligible — you might go months without seeing it. Now scale to a fleet of 10,000 instances, a realistic order of magnitude for a service operating at Netflix's scale: the expected number of instance failures per day is 10,000 × 0.5% = 50. Fifty individual instance failures, every single day, as a routine background condition — not a rare edge case a team can reasonably plan to "handle if it ever comes up." At this scale, "an instance might fail" is not a hypothetical risk to document in a design review; it is an hourly certainty the system must already be built to tolerate as normal operation.

Against that backdrop, the naive design's gaps become concrete: a staging environment tested once before launch tells you nothing about whether the system still tolerates failure correctly six months later, after a dozen unrelated changes to the exact code path the resilience test exercised. And a postmortem only teaches you about the specific failure that already happened to a specific customer — it does not proactively surface the twenty other latent resilience gaps sitting untested in the same system, waiting for their own unlucky day.

ARC 15 · BETTER SOLUTION

Chaos Monkey, derived from the arithmetic in §4

If instance failure is already happening constantly, at scale, as a statistical certainty — the logical move is not to try to prevent it, but to make it happen on your own schedule, deliberately, and safely, so your team learns about resilience gaps during business hours with everyone watching, instead of at 3am during a real incident.

From "failures happen" to "Chaos Monkey"STEP-BY-STEP
THE CLINCHING ARGUMENT: DURING BUSINESS HOURS, ON PURPOSE, IS THE SAFE CHOICE

The detail that makes Chaos Monkey specifically well-designed, not merely aggressive, is exactly when it runs: deliberately during business hours, with engineers awake, watching dashboards, and able to respond immediately — not at 3am, and not silently in the background. A resilience gap surfaced at 2pm on a Tuesday with the responsible team already at their desks is a minor, well-supported inconvenience. The identical gap surfacing on its own, unannounced, at 3am during real peak load is an incident. Chaos Monkey's real contribution isn't just "causing failures" — it's causing them at the best possible time to learn from them cheaply.

ARC 15 · WHY THAT STILL HURTS

The precondition this chapter keeps insisting on

Randomly terminating production instances is only a safe, useful practice under a specific precondition that is easy to state and easy to skip: the system being tested must already have chapter 14's real reliability patterns in place before you start. Run this on a system that doesn't, and you are not testing resilience — you are just causing an outage, on purpose, with extra steps.

  • Without bulkheads and breakers, a killed instance's in-flight requests have nowhere safe to go. A load balancer routing to a now-dead instance, with no health-check-driven failover and no caller-side timeout, produces exactly the same request pile-up chapter 14's §4 derived — except now it's self-inflicted rather than caused by an external dependency degrading.
  • Blast radius must be deliberately bounded. Killing instances across an entire fleet with no limit on how many, how often, or which specific services are in scope, risks turning a controlled experiment into an uncontrolled one — the opposite of the goal.
  • You need a way to know, fast, if the experiment itself is causing real customer harm. Without a steady-state health metric being monitored during the experiment, and an automatic abort if that metric degrades, a chaos experiment has no circuit breaker of its own — ironic, given the previous chapter.
  • Organizational buy-in is a real, non-technical cost. Deliberately breaking production requires trust from leadership and comfort from every team whose service might be targeted — a genuine cultural shift, not just a tool you install.
STATED PLAINLY, PER THIS CHAPTER'S BRIEF

This is not a hypothetical caveat added for balance — it is the central operational discipline of this whole chapter. Chaos engineering is a verification tool for reliability patterns that already exist, not a substitute for building them. A team without chapter 14's circuit breakers, bulkheads, and graceful degradation should build those first; running Chaos Monkey before that point doesn't teach you anything you couldn't have predicted from first principles, and it does real, avoidable customer harm in the process.

So the final architecture needs graduated, controlled deployment: a scheduler with real guardrails, blast- radius controls, and an automated steady-state monitor that can abort an experiment before it becomes a real incident. Let's build it.

ARC 15 · FINAL ARCHITECTURE

The Simian Army — and what came after it

Chaos Monkey was the first of a real, publicly documented family of tools Netflix built around the same philosophy, each aimed at a different reliability question, plus a later platform (ChAP) that automated the whole discipline with the guardrails §6 demands. Click any member below.

FROM MANUAL TOOL TO AUTOMATED PLATFORM

Netflix later built and publicly wrote about the Chaos Automation Platform (ChAP), which formalizes exactly the discipline §6 demands: it defines a steady-state hypothesis for a service before an experiment starts, runs the failure injection against a small, carefully scoped fraction of traffic, monitors the steady-state metric continuously during the experiment, and automatically aborts if that metric degrades beyond a safe threshold — turning what began as a standalone tool (Chaos Monkey) into a governed, repeatable scientific process. §8 implements a simplified version of exactly this loop.

ARC 15 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

The scheduler, and the steady-state guardrail that makes it safe

Two pieces: (a) the scheduler that decides which instance to terminate and when, respecting the blast-radius controls §6 requires, and (b) the steady-state monitor that can abort an experiment automatically — the mechanism that turns "randomly break things" into "safely verify resilience."

(a) The termination scheduler

pseudocodechaos_monkey.py — scheduled, bounded, opt-in-aware
def run_chaos_monkey_cycle(fleet_registry, config):
    if not is_business_hours(config.timezone):
        return                                      # by design — see §5's callout

    for service in fleet_registry.opted_in_services():   # opt-in, not opt-out — blast radius control
        if random() > service.termination_probability:
            continue                                  # most cycles, most services: nothing happens

        candidates = service.healthy_instances()
        if len(candidates) < service.min_healthy_instances:
            log(f"skipping {service.name} — too few healthy instances to safely test")
            continue                                  # never push a fragile fleet below its safety floor

        target = random_choice(candidates)
        log(f"terminating {target.id} in {service.name} — scheduled chaos experiment")
        terminate_instance(target.id)

(b) The steady-state guardrail — this is what makes it safe, not just bold

pseudocodesteady_state_monitor.py — automatic abort, ChAP-style
def run_guarded_experiment(service, hypothesis_metric, tolerance, terminate_fn):
    baseline = measure(hypothesis_metric, service)      # e.g. request success rate, right now
    terminate_fn()                                        # inject the real-world variable — kill an instance

    for _ in range(config.monitor_duration_s):
        current = measure(hypothesis_metric, service)
        if current < baseline * (1 - tolerance):
            abort_experiment(service)                  # steady state broke — stop immediately
            alert_oncall(service, hypothesis_metric, baseline, current)
            return "ABORTED — resilience gap found safely"
        sleep(1)
    return "COMPLETED — steady state held; hypothesis confirmed"

Notice the framing of the return values: an ABORT is not a failure of the experiment — it is exactly the useful outcome, a resilience gap found safely, during business hours, before a real customer noticed. This reframing (abort = success, in the sense that mattered) is the single most important cultural idea underneath the code.

ARC 15 · TRADEOFFS

How much chaos, and how automated

APPROACHWHAT IT VERIFIESWHAT IT BUYSWHAT IT COSTS
Manual "game day"A specific, pre-planned failure scenario, rehearsed by a team on a scheduled date deep, deliberate learning for one scenario good for onboarding a team to the practice infrequent — doesn't catch regressions between game days
Chaos Monkey (automated, single-instance)Whether a service tolerates the loss of any one of its own instances continuous — catches regressions immediately cheap, small blast radius doesn't test correlated, larger-scale failures
Chaos Gorilla / Chaos Kong (zone / region)Whether the whole system tolerates losing an entire availability zone or region tests the failure mode that actually causes the worst outages high blast radius — needs the most organizational maturity and the most careful guardrails
ChAP (automated, hypothesis-driven)A specific steady-state hypothesis, against a small, controlled slice of real traffic automatic abort bounds worst-case customer impact scientific — falsifiable hypothesis, not just "break stuff" most engineering investment to build and maintain
Staging-only fault injectionWhatever the staging environment happens to resemble about production zero real customer risk misses everything §4 identified — real traffic shape, real scale, real config drift

Read this the way every tradeoff table in this course reads: a mature organization doesn't pick one row — it graduates through them, starting with staging tests and manual game days to build organizational confidence, then automated single-instance Chaos Monkey, and only once that's routine and boring, the larger blast-radius tools like Chaos Kong.

ARC 15 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS / HISTORY

From one instance to an entire region

Chaos Kong: rehearsing the loss of an entire AWS region

Beyond individual instances, Netflix has publicly discussed running full-scale exercises evacuating an entire AWS region's traffic to other regions — the real-world application of Chaos Kong, the Simian Army member whose blast radius is the largest in the family (§7). These were genuinely large, organization-wide exercises, run repeatedly over time rather than as one-off stunts, specifically to build justified confidence that a real regional outage — a rare, but not impossible, category of cloud-provider failure — would not mean Netflix's service going down entirely for everyone. This is the practical, load-bearing purpose of running increasingly large blast-radius experiments: proving the system handles catastrophic scenarios before one happens for real.

A realistic, industry-standard pattern: the chaos experiment that finds a real gap

Stated as a general, honest pattern rather than one specific public postmortem: a routine Chaos Monkey run terminates an instance behind a load balancer, and the steady-state monitor from §8b immediately catches a measurable drop in request success rate — revealing that a health check was misconfigured to consider the dead instance "healthy" for several seconds after it actually stopped responding. The experiment aborts automatically, the on-call engineer gets paged during business hours, and the health-check bug gets fixed that afternoon. This is chaos engineering working exactly as intended: a real resilience gap found cheaply, on purpose, instead of expensively, by accident, during a real incident.

STATED HONESTLY

Netflix's exact current chaos engineering tooling, the precise scope and frequency of live Chaos Kong- style exercises today, or a full list of every currently active Simian Army member, are not fully public in granular detail. What is public and well documented: Chaos Monkey's 2010–2012 origin and open-sourcing, the Simian Army's named members (§7), the formal "Principles of Chaos Engineering" published by Netflix engineers around 2015, the Chaos Automation Platform described in Netflix's own engineering writing, and the real practice of running region-evacuation exercises. This chapter derives the underlying engineering logic from those public facts rather than inventing current, non-public specifics.

pseudocodehypothesis.py — a falsifiable claim, not just "break stuff and see"
class SteadyStateHypothesis:
    def __init__(self, service, metric="request_success_rate", tolerance=0.02, min_healthy_instances=3):
        self.service = service
        self.metric = metric
        self.tolerance = tolerance                  # e.g. 2% relative drop is the abort trigger
        self.min_healthy_instances = min_healthy_instances

    def is_eligible(self):
        # the blast-radius / precondition check from §6 — codified, not just a policy doc
        return (self.service.opted_in
                and self.service.has_circuit_breakers()      # chapter 14's precondition, enforced in code
                and self.service.has_bulkheads()
                and len(self.service.healthy_instances()) > self.min_healthy_instances)
WHY IT WAS BUILT THIS WAY

1. is_eligible() checks for chapter 14's patterns before allowing any experiment to run at all. This turns §6's "only do this once real reliability patterns are in place" from a policy recommendation a team could forget into a structural precondition the platform itself enforces — the exact kind of automated guardrail chapter 14's own SRM-style gate philosophy (chapter 13) would recognize. 2. Every experiment starts from an explicit, falsifiable hypothesis, not an open-ended "let's see what breaks." This is what separates disciplined chaos engineering from recklessness — the platform has a precise, pre-defined definition of "this went wrong," rather than relying on a human noticing something felt off. 3. An ABORTED outcome is recorded and reported exactly like a bug found by any other testing method. Treating it as a normal, expected, even successful outcome — not an embarrassing failure of the experiment — is a cultural artifact of the code's own design, not just a talking point in a slide deck.
