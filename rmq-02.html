RABBITMQ INTERNALS · CHAPTER 02

Connections, Channels &
the Frame Protocol

Chapter 1 established that AMQP is a wire protocol, not an API. This chapter opens the wire. Every RabbitMQ interaction — declaring a queue, publishing a message, acknowledging a delivery — reduces to a sequence of small, self-describing frames flowing across a single TCP socket, multiplexed across lightweight logical channels. We derive the frame envelope from first principles, walk a real Basic.Publish byte by byte, and trace the exact chain of Erlang processes — rabbit_reader, rabbit_channel, rabbit_writer — that turns those bytes into routed messages without one slow queue ever blocking the socket.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

A socket is just a byte stream. AMQP needs concurrent, structured conversations.

A TCP socket gives you exactly one guarantee: bytes written on one end arrive at the other end, in order, eventually. It gives you zero notion of "where one message ends." The kernel is free to deliver your 4,000-byte write as one recv(), or as seventeen small ones, or coalesced with the next write you make a millisecond later — from the receiver's point of view it is one undifferentiated stream of octets. Any protocol built on top of TCP has to invent its own way of saying "this is where a logical unit stops," because TCP itself doesn't have that concept.

Layer a second requirement on top of that, one chapter 1 already forced into existence: a real client is never doing exactly one thing. The same process might be publishing trade events, waiting on a reply to an RPC-shaped request, and acknowledging deliveries from a background consumer callback — all "at once" from the application's point of view. Chapter 1 already ruled out one raw TCP connection per concurrent conversation on cost grounds (§4 of that chapter did the N×M arithmetic). So whatever this chapter's protocol design turns out to be, it has to solve two problems with one mechanism:

  • Framing — draw an unambiguous boundary around a variable-length, structured unit of data (a protocol command, a set of message properties, an arbitrarily large message body) inside an otherwise undifferentiated byte stream.
  • Multiplexing — let many independent logical conversations share one socket without one of them corrupting, or indefinitely delaying, any of the others.

It's worth naming two wrong mental models directly, because both are common and both are corrected by this chapter. First: channel.basic_publish(...) is not a single synchronous "send the message" operation — it is your client library assembling and queuing one or more discrete frames for rabbit_writer to place on the wire, in its own time, interleaved with whatever else is happening on that connection. Second: a published message is not one frame. §4 shows exactly why not, with the numbers to back it up, and §8 walks a real publish byte by byte so there's no ambiguity left about what actually crosses the socket.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE REST OF THIS CHAPTER

Every mechanism this chapter covers — the frame envelope, the four frame types, channels, the connection handshake, heartbeats, and the rabbit_reader/rabbit_channel/rabbit_writer process split — is a direct consequence of solving framing and multiplexing simultaneously, with a mechanism cheap enough to pay on every single message. Nothing here is protocol trivia; each piece exists because the alternative demonstrably breaks, and §3–§6 prove that by trying the alternatives first.

DEEP CONTEXT · THE PATTERN IS OLDER THAN AMQP, AND REAPPEARS EVERYWHERE

Length-prefixed, type-tagged framing over a byte stream is not an AMQP invention — X.25 packet framing and ASN.1 BER encoding solved essentially the same problem decades earlier, and the same shape reappears in every serious multiplexed wire protocol built since. HTTP/2 multiplexes many logical streams over one TCP connection using framed HEADERS/DATA frames tagged with a stream id — structurally the same trick AMQP uses with a channel number. Kafka's own wire protocol frames each request with a length prefix and a correlation id instead of a channel number, trading AMQP's persistent per-channel state for simpler, more stateless request/response pipelining — a deliberate design difference chapter 9 material and §9 below return to with numbers. The lesson: once you need to multiplex independent logical work over one socket, some variant of "tag every frame with an id, make every frame self-delimiting" is close to inevitable.

ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: a raw connection per concurrent operation, one giant blob per message

Derive it the way a client library author would, before thinking hard about framing. Two instincts arrive together, and both feel reasonable in isolation: (a) whenever the application needs to do two things "at once" — publish from one thread, ack from a consumer callback on another — just open another raw TCP connection for it, since sockets are cheap to open; and (b) serialize an entire logical message — its routing information, its properties, and its full body — into one blob, prefix it with a length so the receiver at least knows where it ends, and write the whole thing in a single send() call.

pseudocodenaive_publish.py — the strawman, annotated
class NaiveClient:
    def __init__(self):
        # one socket per "role" this process plays — publisher, consumer,
        # RPC-reply-waiter — because nobody has designed a way to share one yet
        self.publish_sock = connect("broker-host", 5672)
        self.consume_sock  = connect("broker-host", 5672)
        self.rpc_sock      = connect("broker-host", 5672)

    def publish(self, exchange, routing_key, properties, body):
        # everything — metadata AND the full body — serialized as one blob
        blob = json_dumps({
            "exchange": exchange, "routing_key": routing_key,
            "properties": properties, "body": body,   # could be 500 MB
        }).encode()
        self.publish_sock.send(pack(">I", len(blob)) + blob)
        # the receiver cannot know a single field of "properties" until it has
        # buffered and parsed the ENTIRE blob — body included

This is not a strawman built to be knocked down for sport — every client library author who hasn't yet needed multiplexing reaches here first, and for a single-threaded toy script talking to a broker it works fine. The stepper below plays it forward as the application grows into something that actually needs concurrency.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

The bill: file descriptors, kernel buffers, TLS handshakes, and forced head-of-line blocking

Formalize what the deck just showed. Every one of these costs is real, measurable, and scales with connection count or blob size — not with useful work done.

  • File descriptor exhaustion. Every TCP connection is one file descriptor. A default per-process ulimit -n on many Linux distributions is 1024; a service juggling a publisher socket, a consumer socket, and a per-outstanding-RPC socket for even a modest 300 concurrent request/response operations is most of the way to that ceiling before touching a single ordinary application file handle — and a process restart re-establishes every one of them simultaneously.
  • Kernel socket buffer cost, paid per connection, not per byte of useful traffic. Each socket carries its own send and receive buffers — commonly tens to a couple hundred KB per side depending on OS autotuning (SO_SNDBUF/SO_RCVBUF). 300 raw connections at even a conservative 64 KB combined is ~19 MB of kernel memory spent purely on connection overhead, before a single application byte is queued.
  • TLS handshake cost paid on every operation instead of once. Production brokers terminate TLS. A full handshake is multiple round trips plus real asymmetric-crypto CPU work; opening a fresh raw connection per logical operation means paying that cost every time, instead of amortizing it over one long-lived connection's entire lifetime.
  • No framing means no boundary detection and no safe multiplexing. Because the naive blob splits nothing — properties and body arrive as one undifferentiated unit — the receiver must buffer the entire blob before it can read even the routing key. A 500 MB export message forces a 500 MB allocation on the receiving side before any routing decision is possible. And because there is no generic, per-message frame boundary, safely interleaving two different logical operations on one socket isn't possible without inventing exactly the mechanism §5 derives next — the naive design simply doesn't have the vocabulary to attempt it.
THE NUMBERS, STATED PLAINLY

None of these costs come from "too much traffic." They come from connection count and blob size alone — a completely idle naive client holding open 300 sockets still pays the descriptor and kernel-buffer bill in full. And the framing failure isn't a performance nuance: a 500 MB message published on the naive design forces a 500 MB receive-side allocation before routing can even begin, on a design that was supposed to make routing decisions cheap. Both failures point at the same missing piece: a small, generic, self-delimiting envelope that any receiver can parse without understanding the payload — §5 derives exactly that, and shows why it kills both problems with one mechanism.

ARC 05 · THE FRAME ENVELOPE, DERIVED

Not a bigger blob, not more sockets — a small, generic, self-delimiting frame

Candidate A: keep separate sockets, just length-prefix the blob

Fixes the boundary-detection half of §4's failure — a receiver can now at least tell where one blob ends — but does nothing about connection count. Every concurrent conversation still needs its own raw socket, with the exact fd/kernel-buffer/TLS bill §4 already quantified. Rejected: still N sockets.

Candidate B: one socket, but take strict turns

Put everything through a single connection, but require that a complete logical blob finish writing before the next one starts — no interleaving at all. This "solves" multiplexing by refusing to actually multiplex: a 500 MB blob for operation A must finish writing in full before operation B's tiny acknowledgment gets even one byte onto the wire. Rejected — this reintroduces exactly the head-of-line blocking §4 identified, just moved from "which socket" to "whose turn," and makes it worse: now every conversation shares the one queue behind the giant blob, not just the two that happened to pick the same socket.

Candidate C ✓: a generic, self-delimiting frame envelope

Give every unit of data crossing the socket a small, fixed-shape envelope: a type, a channel number, a byte length, the payload itself, and a fixed sentinel byte marking exactly where the frame is supposed to end.

layoutthe AMQP 0-9-1 frame — every frame on the wire, no exceptions
[ type : 1 byte ][ channel : 2 bytes ][ size : 4 bytes ][ payload : size bytes ][ frame-end : 1 byte = 0xCE ]
    ^type: 1=METHOD 2=HEADER 3=BODY 8=HEARTBEAT      ^size: length of payload ONLY,
   ^channel: which logical channel this frame belongs to (0 = connection-level)   not including this 7-byte header or the 1-byte trailer

Because every frame — however small — is a complete, independently flushable unit with its own declared length, two things fall out immediately: (1) any receiver can find the next frame boundary in constant time without parsing a single byte of payload semantics, and (2) a writer can freely interleave complete frames belonging to different channels on one socket, because interleaving whole frames never corrupts anything — there's no partial frame sitting mid-stream for another channel's frame to land inside of.

Deriving the frame envelope from the two requirements in §1STEP-BY-STEP

One more design question the envelope alone doesn't answer: why is 0xCE there at all, when size already tells the receiver exactly how many payload bytes to read? Because size is only trustworthy if nothing upstream has gone wrong. If a bug — on either side, or corruption on the wire — has desynchronized the parser, the frame-end sentinel is a nearly-free sanity check: if the byte immediately following the declared payload isn't 0xCE, the receiver knows definitively that something is wrong right now, and can close the connection instead of silently misinterpreting arbitrary subsequent bytes as frame headers and corrupting every channel sharing that connection. One byte, cheap insurance against an entire connection quietly going insane. §15's channel-sharing failure injection triggers exactly this check.

FOUR FRAME TYPES, NAMED HERE, DERIVED IN FULL IN §6 AND §8

METHOD (type 1) — a protocol command: class-id + method-id + that method's arguments (e.g. Basic.Publish, Queue.Declare). CONTENT HEADER (type 2) — announces a message's total body size and its properties, before any body bytes exist on the wire. CONTENT BODY (type 3) — raw, opaque message bytes, chunked across multiple frames when large. HEARTBEAT (type 8) — an empty, connection-level frame whose only job is proving the socket is still alive. §6 derives exactly why content needs two frame types instead of one, and §8 opens every one of these to the byte.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL COSTS

One "content" frame still blocks. Splitting header from body is not free either.

The envelope from §5 solves generic framing and multiplexing — but apply it naively to a published message and a new failure mode appears immediately. Take the obvious next design: one CONTENT frame type carrying class-id, properties, and the entire body together, wrapped once in the §5 envelope.

That single frame is, by the envelope's own rules, an indivisible unit: once its header has declared "this frame's payload is 500,000,000 bytes," nothing else can appear on that channel until all 500 million bytes have been transmitted — and depending on how the writer shares the underlying TCP send buffer, a giant frame on one channel can still starve other channels' frames from reaching the socket at all while it's mid-flight. The generic envelope fixed corruption from interleaving; it did nothing about a single frame that is, by design, enormous.

The fix has to attack the content frame specifically. Properties are small and needed immediately — a receiver wants to know the total body size and delivery-mode before deciding how to allocate, and a channel wants to be able to start a routing decision without waiting on a single body byte. The body, by contrast, can be arbitrarily large and is opaque to the broker. So: split them. A small CONTENT HEADER frame carries the properties and the total body size; the body itself is chunked into one or more CONTENT BODY frames, each individually bounded to at most frame_max bytes — small enough that the writer can always interleave another channel's frame between any two body chunks of even a gigantic message. This is the actual, mechanical reason content needs two frame types, not an arbitrary protocol design choice.

  • Envelope overhead is now paid on every single frame, including every body chunk. 8 bytes (7-byte header + 1-byte frame-end) per frame means a message chunked into 100 body frames pays 800 bytes of pure overhead a single unbounded frame wouldn't — §10 turns this into a precise overhead percentage as a function of frame_max and message size.
  • rabbit_writer now does real scheduling work. Instead of "write whatever's queued next," it must fairly interleave frames belonging to different channels — deciding how many of channel A's queued body chunks to flush before giving channel B's frame a turn, so one channel's giant message can't monopolize the socket even though no single frame is unbounded anymore.
  • The receiver must reassemble a multi-frame body before anything downstream can use it as one unit — a modest, one-time-per-message cost: accumulate body frames on a channel until the running byte count matches the content header's declared body-size, then hand the complete message onward.
  • frame_max becomes a genuine tuning knob with a real tradeoff. Too large, and one channel's giant message can still hold the writer for a proportionally long — though now strictly bounded — interval before yielding to other channels. Too small, and per-frame envelope overhead and writer-scheduling overhead both rise, for every message, regardless of size. §8's slider widget and §15's simulator both make this tradeoff tangible.
THE HONEST TRADE

None of this is an argument against framing content into header + body — it's the actual price of bounding per-frame memory and preserving fairness across channels sharing one socket. The final architecture, then, is: one TCP connection, N lightweight channels multiplexed over it via the §5 envelope, four frame types serving four distinct jobs, and a set of negotiated ceilings (channel_max, frame_max, heartbeat) agreed once at connection setup so every later frame can be produced and consumed without re-litigating limits per message. §7 puts all of it on one map you can click through.

DEEP CONTEXT · WHY THE FRAME TYPES DON'T MULTIPLY AS FEATURES GROW

Notice what's absent from the frame-type list: there is no "Queue.Declare frame" or "Basic.Ack frame." Every protocol command — Queue.Declare, Basic.Publish, Basic.Ack, Channel.Open, all ~60 of AMQP 0-9-1's methods — travels inside the same generic METHOD frame type, distinguished only by the class-id/method-id pair in its payload. Adding an entire new protocol operation to AMQP never requires a new frame type; it requires a new class-id/method-id pair and an argument encoding, both of which live inside the payload the envelope treats as opaque. This is precisely the separation of concerns §5 bought: the envelope's job is framing and multiplexing; the payload's job is everything else.

ARC 08 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

A complete publish, byte by byte — then the handshake, then the process chain

(a) The frame anatomy, exactly

Every frame on an AMQP 0-9-1 connection, after the initial protocol header, has this exact shape — no variants, no optional fields in the envelope itself:

layoutframe envelope — 7 bytes of header, 1 byte of trailer, always
type     1 byte    // 1=METHOD  2=HEADER  3=BODY  8=HEARTBEAT
channel  2 bytes   // big-endian unsigned short; 0 = connection-level (Tune, heartbeats)
size     4 bytes   // big-endian unsigned long; length of payload ONLY
payload  size bytes // meaning depends entirely on type
frame-end 1 byte   // always literal 0xCE — a desync/corruption sentinel, not data

Three frames for one logical "publish this message" — never one. Two structural reasons, both provable from the bytes above: (1) the header lets the receiving rabbit_channel begin work before the body finishes arriving. Frame 2 alone tells the channel the complete body size (21, here — but the same field holds true for a 2 GB body) and every declared property, which is enough to enforce a max_message_size limit, pre-size a buffer, or begin an exchange routing lookup using only the method frame's routing key — all without a single body byte having crossed the wire yet. (2) chunking bounds per-frame memory and preserves fairness. A body frame can never exceed frame_max − 8 bytes (§6's fix), so rabbit_writer can always interleave a different channel's frame between any two chunks of an enormous body — the three-frame minimum isn't overhead for its own sake, it's the mechanism that makes both early routing and bounded-memory chunking possible at the same time.

(c) The connection handshake: seven required round trips before one application frame is possible

Before any METHOD/HEADER/BODY frame can be exchanged, both peers must agree on protocol version, on authentication, and on three numeric ceilings. The very first exchange is deliberately not a frame at all:

The negotiation rule for Tune/Tune-Ok is asymmetric and worth stating precisely: the server proposes ceilings; the client may only accept them or lower them, never raise them (a value of 0 from the server means "no specific limit," in which case the client's proposal wins outright for that one parameter). RabbitMQ's own defaults are concrete, real numbers you should know: channel_max = 2047, frame_max = 131072 bytes (128 KiB), heartbeat = 60 seconds.

(d) Channels: why lightweight multiplexed sessions, not more TCP connections

§4 already quantified the cost of one raw connection per concurrent conversation — fds, kernel buffers, TLS handshakes, all paid per connection regardless of traffic. A channel reuses none of that machinery per logical conversation: it is a number in the frame header (§5) plus one Erlang process (rabbit_channel, §8f) holding that conversation's protocol state — no new socket, no new kernel buffers, no new handshake. Opening a channel is a single METHOD frame (Channel.Open) and its reply, on an already-open connection; closing one is the same, in reverse. This is precisely why "one connection, many channels" is cheap where "one connection per conversation" was expensive.

THE RULE: A CHANNEL IS NOT THREAD-SAFE — HERE'S EXACTLY WHAT BREAKS

The broker side is perfectly safe — a rabbit_channel process serializes whatever arrives in its mailbox, one message at a time, regardless of who sent it. The danger is entirely client-side. Suppose thread A calls channel.basic_publish(msg_1) and thread B calls channel.basic_publish(msg_2) on the same channel object at the same instant. Each publish is a METHOD frame followed by a HEADER frame followed by one or more BODY frames — and if the client library does not serialize access to that channel's underlying socket write, thread A's method frame can go out, then thread B's method frame, then thread A's header frame, then thread B's body frame — all on the same channel number. The receiving rabbit_reader has no way to know two logical messages got interleaved; it will either misassemble a corrupted composite message or trip the frame-end sentinel from §5 and close the connection outright. §15's simulator lets you trigger this exact corruption on demand. Every major client library (Pika, the Java client, amqplib for Node) documents this rule explicitly and provides a connection-level or channel-pool abstraction specifically so application code doesn't have to hand-roll the locking.

(e) The per-connection process chain: rabbit_reader → rabbit_channel → rabbit_writer

One TCP connection maps to exactly one rabbit_reader process (owns the socket, parses the generic frame envelope, applies connection-level credit-based flow control), N rabbit_channel processes (one per open channel, running that channel's protocol method state machine), and one rabbit_writer process (serializes and fairly interleaves every outbound frame from every channel back onto the one socket). This is not an accidental decomposition — it exists specifically so a slow downstream stage can never stall the socket read loop.

(f) Heartbeats: why TCP keepalive is not enough

TCP keepalive exists, but it answers the wrong question at the wrong timescale. Linux's default tcp_keepalive_time is 7200 seconds (2 hours) before the kernel even sends its first probe — designed to reap long-dead idle sockets eventually, not to detect an application hang within seconds. Worse, keepalive only proves the kernel on the other end is reachable; it says nothing about whether the application — the BEAM VM, the client library's event loop — is still making progress. A peer whose process is deadlocked, or whose event loop is blocked, keeps ACKing keepalive probes at the TCP layer while being completely unresponsive at the AMQP layer.

AMQP's heartbeat is application-level by design: at the negotiated interval (default 60s, from Tune/Tune-Ok), each peer must see some traffic — a real frame or an explicit empty HEARTBEAT frame if nothing else was due — within 2× the negotiated interval, or it considers the other side dead and closes the connection. Two intervals, not one, specifically to absorb ordinary jitter (a slightly delayed scheduler tick, a brief GC pause) without false-triggering on every minor hiccup.

THE CLASSIC FALSE POSITIVE

The 2× grace period absorbs ordinary jitter, but a sufficiently long stall still trips it on a perfectly healthy connection. If the BEAM scheduler thread responsible for sending a node's heartbeats is itself blocked — a long garbage collection of an unusually large process, a NIF or dirty-scheduler call that runs long, or the node under heavy disk-I/O pressure from an unrelated queue's page-cache activity — the heartbeat-sending process simply doesn't get scheduled in time. The peer sees no traffic for 2× the interval, concludes the connection is dead, and disconnects a connection whose network path was never actually the problem. §11 covers a real, publicly documented version of exactly this failure mode; §15's simulator lets you trigger the same timer yourself.

ARC 10 · PERFORMANCE CHARACTERISTICS

Numbers you can predict before benchmarking

From the frame model and process split alone, several performance properties follow directly:

  • Frame parsing is O(1) per frame, not O(connection size). rabbit_reader's receive loop matches the fixed 7-byte header, reads exactly size more bytes, checks for 0xCE, and dispatches — no scanning, no backtracking, regardless of how many frames have already crossed the connection.
  • Method dispatch is a direct pattern match, effectively O(1). The generated rabbit_framing_amqp_0_9_1 module pattern-matches class-id/method-id pairs directly to argument decoders — there is no runtime lookup table walked linearly; the BEAM compiles pattern-match clauses into efficient jump-table-like code.
  • Per-channel mailbox depth is the real variable state. A channel's Erlang mailbox grows by however many decoded methods/frames rabbit_reader has forwarded but the channel hasn't processed yet — bounded not by any protocol-level cap but by the credit-flow mechanism in §8f, which is precisely why credit flow exists rather than an arbitrary fixed queue-depth limit.
  • Per-frame envelope overhead is a fixed 8 bytes, so its relative cost is a function of payload size. overhead % = 8 × frame_count / total_wire_bytes. For a tiny 50-byte body (3 frames total: method + header + one body frame), 24 bytes of pure envelope against roughly 90–120 total bytes is a meaningful ~20–25% overhead; for a 10 MB body at the default 128 KiB frame_max, the ~640 body frames' overhead (~5 KB) is well under 0.1% of the total. Small, frequent messages pay proportionally far more for framing than large ones — a real argument for publisher-side batching where message rate, not message size, is the bottleneck.

WHAT THE BEAM SCHEDULER ADDS ON TOP

Chapter 1 established that BEAM processes are reduction-counted and preempted fairly (~2,000 reductions per slice). That guarantee applies directly here: a connection with one channel parsing a storm of tiny frames cannot monopolize a scheduler thread and starve a different connection's rabbit_reader on the same core — each reader, channel, and writer is its own independently scheduled process, so per-connection frame-parsing load is fair across connections by the same mechanism that made per-queue load fair across queues in chapter 1.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD PRODUCTION

Three shapes of incident this chapter's mechanics explain completely

Story 1: connection-per-request as a documented anti-pattern

A recurring, widely reported shape: a web application opens a fresh AMQP connection on every incoming HTTP request to publish one event, instead of reusing a long-lived pooled connection. Under moderate load this reproduces §4's fd-exhaustion and TLS-handshake-per-operation math directly — CPU spent on repeated handshakes and accept-path work dominates the actual publish cost, and the service hits connection-count ceilings well before it hits any genuine throughput limit. RabbitMQ's own client guidance and hosting providers' public documentation (CloudAMQP among others) call this out explicitly by name, recommending one pooled connection per process (or a small pool) holding many long-lived channels — exactly the shape §8d derives from cost, not merely as a style preference.

Story 2: heartbeat "flapping" under BEAM scheduler pressure

Operators have documented (in RabbitMQ community discussions and issue trackers) connections dropping with missed-heartbeat errors during periods of heavy node load — large garbage collections, disk-pressure spikes from an unrelated queue's activity, or bursts of expensive management-API calls — with no actual network problem. This is precisely the §8f false-positive mechanism: the heartbeat-sending process was scheduled late enough to blow through the 2×-interval deadline on an otherwise perfectly healthy connection. The practical fixes mirror the mechanism exactly — raise the heartbeat interval to add slack, and address the underlying resource pressure so the scheduler isn't starved in the first place; simply disabling heartbeats trades this false-positive risk for the much worse failure mode chapter 1 already ruled out (a truly hung peer going undetected indefinitely).

Story 3: the leaking client that exhausts channel_max

A commonly reported client-library misuse bug, independently reproduced across ecosystems (Java, Python, and Node clients all have public issue-tracker reports of this exact shape): a service opens a new channel per message instead of reusing a small per-worker pool, and never closes the old ones. Given RabbitMQ's default channel_max of 2047, sustained traffic eventually exhausts the ceiling, and the connection starts rejecting further Channel.Open attempts with CHANNEL_ERROR — number of channels opened exceeds the negotiated channel-max value. The fix is always the same: pool a fixed, small number of long-lived channels per connection and reuse them, which is simultaneously the cheapest design (§8d) and the one immune to this failure mode by construction.

First, the frame-parsing loop itself — this is what §8a's byte layout looks like as Erlang binary pattern matching:

Erlangrabbit_reader.erl — frame parsing, simplified
parse_frame(<<Type:8, Channel:16, Size:32, Payload:Size/binary,
             16#CE:8, Rest/binary>>, State) ->
    %% Size bytes read, and the very next byte IS 0xCE — the stream is
    %% still synchronized. Dispatch by type and keep going with Rest.
    NewState = dispatch_frame(Type, Channel, Payload, State),
    parse_frame(Rest, NewState);

parse_frame(<<Type:8, Channel:16, Size:32, Payload:Size/binary,
             Bad:8, _Rest/binary>>, State) when Bad =/= 16#CE ->
    %% Declared size was honored, but the sentinel byte doesn't match —
    %% something upstream desynced. Don't guess: close the connection.
    connection_error(frame_error, "expected frame-end 0xCE", State);

parse_frame(Incomplete, State) ->
    %% not enough bytes yet for a full frame — buffer and wait for more
    %% from the socket; this is what makes TCP's arbitrary chunking a
    %% non-issue for the protocol layer above it.
    State#reader{partial = Incomplete}.

Second, rabbit_command_assembler — the state machine that turns a method+header+body sequence into one complete command before rabbit_channel ever sees it:

Erlangrabbit_command_assembler.erl — states, simplified
%% States: expecting_method -> expecting_header -> expecting_body -> done
process({method, Method, ClassId}, expecting_method) when has_content(Method) ->
    {expecting_header, #partial{method = Method, class = ClassId}};
process({method, Method, _}, expecting_method) ->
    %% content-free methods (Queue.Declare, Basic.Ack, ...) need no header/body
    {done, #command{method = Method}};

process({header, BodySize, Props}, expecting_header, Partial) ->
    case BodySize of
        0 -> {done, #command{method = Partial#partial.method, props = Props, body = <<>>}};
        _ -> {expecting_body, Partial#partial{remaining = BodySize, props = Props, acc = []}}
    end;

process({body, Chunk}, expecting_body, Partial) ->
    Remaining = Partial#partial.remaining - byte_size(Chunk),
    Acc = [Chunk | Partial#partial.acc],
    case Remaining of
        0 -> {done, #command{method = Partial#partial.method, props = Partial#partial.props,
                             body = iolist_to_binary(lists:reverse(Acc))}};
        _ -> {expecting_body, Partial#partial{remaining = Remaining, acc = Acc}}
    end.

Third, the credit-flow sketch that stops a slow channel from turning into an unbounded reader backlog:

Erlangrabbit_reader.erl / rabbit_channel.erl — credit flow, simplified
%% Reader side: only forward another frame to a channel if that channel
%% has credit left. No lock, no shared counter — just a message.
maybe_forward(Channel, Frame, State = #reader{credits = Credits}) ->
    case maps:get(Channel, Credits, State#reader.default_credit) of
        C when C > 0 ->
            Channel ! {frame, Frame},
            State#reader{credits = maps:put(Channel, C - 1, Credits)};
        0 ->
            %% out of credit for THIS channel only — reader keeps parsing and
            %% forwarding frames for every OTHER channel on the connection
            State#reader{blocked = [Channel | State#reader.blocked]}
    end.

%% Channel side: once it has drained its backlog, it replenishes credit —
%% an ordinary async message, not a shared mutable counter.
handle_info(drain_complete, State) ->
    State#ch.reader_pid ! {credit, State#ch.number, 100},
    {noreply, State}.
WHY THE DEVELOPERS WROTE IT THIS WAY — THREE CHOICES TO STEAL

1. Make protocol-level corruption detectable, not just theoretically preventable. The frame-end sentinel costs one byte per frame and turns "silently misinterpret the stream" into "detect and close immediately" — cheap insurance is still insurance. 2. Never let a slow consumer of decoded frames block the producer of decoded frames. Credit flow is deliberately an asynchronous message, not a blocking call or a shared counter guarded by a lock — the reader can always keep parsing frames for channels that aren't backed up, which is the same "don't let one slow entity stall an unrelated one" instinct chapter 1's supervision trees embody at the process-crash level. 3. Push reassembly complexity into one well-tested state machine, not into every message handler. rabbit_command_assembler is the single place that knows how to turn method+header+body into one command; rabbit_channel's actual method handlers never see partial frames at all, which keeps the code that implements AMQP semantics free of framing concerns entirely.
