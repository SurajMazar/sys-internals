Multi-Account Strategy:
Organizations, Control Tower & SCPs

A single sentence carries most of this chapter's exam weight: a Service Control Policy can only ever restrict permissions — it can never grant them — and it is evaluated alongside IAM, never instead of it. Everything else — Organizations, OUs, consolidated billing, Control Tower's landing zones — exists to give that one restriction mechanism somewhere sensible to attach. Get comfortable with the intersection logic in this chapter and a large share of SAP-C02's Organizational Complexity domain falls into place.

You will be able toState — and defend against a trick question — exactly why an SCP can never grant a permission.
You will understandWhy a single AWS account, no matter how carefully its IAM is written, cannot create an org-wide guardrail.
You will predictWhether a scenario is really asking for Organizations, SCPs, or Control Tower based on its constraints.
You will simulateAttaching an SCP to an OU and watching IAM-permitted actions get blocked anyway.

Where this sits on the SAP-C02 blueprint

This chapter maps to AWS Certified Solutions Architect – Professional (SAP-C02), domain Design Solutions for Organizational Complexity — 26% of the exam, the second-largest of the four Professional domains (Design for New Solutions 29%, Continuous Improvement for Existing Solutions 25%, Accelerate Workload Migration and Modernization 20%). Multi-account design is arguably the single most distinctive "Professional, not Associate" topic on this exam: SAA-C03 assumes you can secure one account well; SAP-C02 assumes you can govern a hundred accounts you don't personally administer, run by teams you don't personally trust to always do the right thing.

It matters beyond its own domain, too. ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"One account, careful IAM" and "an SCP Allow means they can do it"

Two wrong instincts show up constantly in this domain, and the exam is happy to bait both in the same question.

Wrong instinct #1 — the single-account instinct. A candidate sees "multiple teams, multiple environments, multiple cost centers" and reaches for what already works at small scale: one AWS account, IAM groups per team, tags per environment, cost allocation tags for billing. It feels efficient — one console, one set of service quotas to track, no cross-account role assumptions to configure. For a small company with three engineers, this is often the right call. The exam, however, is almost never describing a small company with three engineers; it's describing an enterprise, and at that scale the single-account instinct is the trap answer.

Wrong instinct #2 — the "SCP grants permissions" instinct. A candidate reads that an SCP attached to an OU includes an Allow statement for, say, s3:*, and concludes that every principal in every account under that OU can now use S3 freely — even ones with no IAM policy granting S3 at all. This is a natural but completely incorrect reading of what an SCP Allow statement means.

THE TRAP, STATED PLAINLY

An SCP is a filter, not a grant. It defines the outer boundary of what could ever be allowed in an account — the ceiling — never the floor. A principal still needs an actual IAM identity-based or resource-based policy that grants the action before the SCP's opinion is even relevant. Hold that sentence in your head through the rest of this chapter; nearly every SAP-C02 question on this topic is testing whether you actually believe it.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

The specific mechanism each instinct breaks

Why one account can't create an org-wide guardrail

IAM is a complete, self-contained permission system within a single account. That's precisely the problem for governance: whoever administers that account's IAM can grant themselves — or anyone else in that account — anything IAM is capable of expressing, including full administrator access. There is no layer above IAM, inside a single account, that can act as an independent guardrail, because the same humans who control the workloads also control the policies meant to restrain them. A "guardrail" enforced by the same people it's supposed to restrain isn't a guardrail; it's a suggestion.

Single-account architecture also concentrates blast radius in ways that don't show up until something goes wrong: one exposed credential, one over-permissive Lambda execution role, or one public S3 bucket now sits in the same resource graph, the same CloudTrail trail, the same service quotas, and the same monthly bill as every other team's production workload. Proving a clean compliance boundary — "this PCI-scoped workload cannot possibly touch that unrelated marketing workload" — is very hard to demonstrate convincingly when both live in the same account, no matter how carefully the IAM policies are scoped. An account boundary is a much stronger, more auditable line than any IAM policy boundary, because it's enforced by AWS's own account isolation rather than by policy correctness.

Why "SCP Allow" doesn't mean "principal can do it"

AWS evaluates a request against every applicable policy type at once, and the request is allowed only if none of them deny it and at least one identity-based or resource-based policy explicitly allows it. An SCP's job in that evaluation is narrower than people assume: it does not attach any permission to any principal. It only answers the question "is this action even in the realm of the possible for this account?" Concretely:

  • IAM allows X, SCP allows X → the action is permitted (assuming no explicit deny elsewhere).
  • IAM allows X, SCP denies X (or excludes it from an allow-list) → denied. The SCP's restriction wins.
  • IAM never mentions X, SCP allows X → still denied — an SCP allowing something does nothing if no identity-based or resource-based policy ever granted it in the first place.
  • IAM denies X → denied regardless of what any SCP says — you can't out-allow an explicit IAM deny either.

Effective permission is the intersection of what IAM allows and what the applicable SCPs allow — not the union, and not something either layer can decide alone. The default SCP that AWS attaches to every root, OU, and account when you enable SCPs — FullAWSAccess — is a single Allow: * statement for exactly this reason: SCPs start as a no-op ceiling set at "everything," and you carve it down from there. Nothing about attaching that default policy grants anyone anything; IAM was already doing 100% of the granting.

A NEWER ADDITION — HANDLE WITH CARE

Late 2024 onward, AWS introduced Resource Control Policies (RCPs) — a sibling to SCPs that restricts what resource-based policies can grant, regardless of the identity making the request (useful for things like enforcing that only your organization's principals can ever access an S3 bucket, even if a resource policy is misconfigured to allow more). Flagging this clearly: RCPs are new enough that they are unlikely to be core, heavily-tested SAP-C02 material at the time of writing. Know the name and the one-sentence idea — "SCPs restrict identities, RCPs restrict what resource policies can grant" — but don't over-invest study time here versus the well-established SCP mechanics this chapter is built around.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

The fine print the correct approach still has to respect

None of this works if you don't also know its edges. These are the specific facts SAP-C02 uses to build "gotcha" distractors:

  • SCP document size limit: 5,120 characters. This is the commonly-published figure for a single SCP's policy text. Large deny-lists (many actions, many conditions) can hit this surprisingly fast — §10 lets you watch it happen.
  • OUs can nest up to 5 levels deep, including the root. Also a commonly-published figure. Deep trees are rarely a good idea anyway: every level an account sits under is another SCP whose statements get intersected together, and a five-deep tree is five separate documents to reason about for every permission question.
  • The management (root/payer) account is not restricted by SCPs attached to it. This is one of the single most-tested facts in this domain. An SCP attached at the organization root, or directly to the management account, still applies to every member account under that root — but never to the management account itself. This is precisely why landing-zone guidance is unanimous: keep the management account nearly empty of workloads. It's a governance seat, not a place to run production, because it's the one place your guardrails can't reach.
  • Service-linked roles are exempt from SCP restrictions. SCPs are designed not to break the AWS-managed automation that services rely on internally (for example, the roles Auto Scaling or AWS Config use to do their job) — otherwise a broad deny statement could silently break unrelated managed features across every account it touches.
  • Every root, OU, and account gets the default FullAWSAccess policy when SCPs are enabled (technically, when you enable "all features" mode in Organizations, as opposed to the older consolidated-billing-only mode). It's a single Allow: * statement — a deliberate no-op — that you either leave in place alongside your own restrictive SCPs, or detach once you've replaced it with an explicit allow-list (at which point everything not listed becomes unreachable — §8 walks through exactly this).
  • Exact numeric quotas move. Maximum accounts per organization, maximum SCPs attached per entity, and similar hard numbers are the kind of detail AWS revises with a service update. If a scenario turns on an exact number you don't confidently remember, answer from the decision framework in §9 rather than a guessed quota — check current AWS documentation for the number that actually matters on exam day.

WHY THIS EXACT SHAPE KEEPS SHOWING UP

It isn't arbitrary. Security and Infrastructure are split from Workloads because they need different, stricter guardrails than a normal application team's account — the log archive account, for instance, should probably deny even its own administrators the ability to delete the bucket holding every account's CloudTrail logs. Prod and NonProd split inside Workloads because they warrant genuinely different SCPs (a NonProd OU can tolerate looser region restrictions than Prod, for example). Sandbox gets the tightest allow-list of all, because its entire purpose is letting people experiment without any chance of production blast radius or an unbounded bill.

ARC 09 · DECISION FRAMEWORK

  
SCP vs IAM permission boundary vs Control Tower guardrail

  
This table is the single most exam-relevant artifact in the chapter. Every one of these mechanisms can
  "restrict," but they restrict different things, at different scopes, enforced by different actors — and the
  exam tests exactly that distinction.

  

    
MECHANISMSCOPEWHO IT RESTRAINSUSE IT WHEN

    
Service Control Policy (SCP)An OU or account, org-wide reach
      Every principal in scope, including that account's own administrators and root user
      You need a guardrail the account's own admins cannot override from inside their account — region locks, "never leave the org," "never disable CloudTrail."

    
IAM permission boundaryOne IAM user or role, within one account
      That single principal — administrators in the same account can still edit or remove the boundary
      You need to cap what a specific role can do (e.g. a role that provisions other IAM roles must not be able to grant itself admin), inside one account, where the account owner is trusted to maintain the boundary.

    
Control Tower preventive guardrailAn OU (implemented as an SCP under the hood)
      Every principal in scope, same as a hand-written SCP
      You want the exact protection an SCP gives you, pre-written and consistently applied across every account Control Tower provisions — no custom JSON to author or maintain.

    
Control Tower detective guardrailAn OU (implemented as an AWS Config rule)
      Nothing in real time — it observes and reports (some can trigger remediation) after the fact
      The action can't reasonably be prevented upfront (or you want visibility without blocking anyone), but you need to know the moment an account drifts out of compliance — e.g. flagging an S3 bucket that became public.

  

  
Read "preventive vs detective" as the exam's favorite axis here: preventive stops the action before it
  happens (SCPs and permission boundaries both live in this category — they're both evaluated before AWS
  authorizes anything); detective finds out after the fact and either alerts or remediates. A scenario
  that says "we must guarantee this can never happen" wants a preventive control. One that says "we need to
  detect and respond when it happens" wants a detective control — and reaching for an SCP there is over-engineering
  a stop sign onto a webcam.

WHY THIS CURVE MATTERS OPERATIONALLY

    
Real-world SCPs hit this limit for a boring reason: region-restriction policies need a long
    NotAction exemption list (IAM, Organizations, Support, and a handful of other global services
    have to be excluded or you lock yourself out of the account entirely), and every additional exempted
    service adds real bytes. Teams that hit the ceiling typically respond by splitting one sprawling SCP into
    several smaller, single-purpose SCPs attached to the same OU — the effective restriction is identical
    (SCPs at the same level are combined), it's just expressed as multiple documents instead of one.

  







  
ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

  
Three vignettes, written the way the real exam phrases them

  
These are original constructions for this course, built to mirror how SAP-C02 actually writes scenario
  questions — a company profile, a constraint, and a "which approach" decision. They are not, and do not claim
  to be, real exam questions.


  
Case 1: The acquired subsidiary

  
A logistics company acquires a smaller competitor that already runs its own AWS workloads in its own,
  independently-created account. Finance wants consolidated billing across both companies within 30 days;
  Security wants the subsidiary's account to inherit the parent's region restrictions and "never disable
  CloudTrail" guardrail within the same window, without touching a single IAM policy inside the subsidiary's
  account (the subsidiary's engineering team keeps day-to-day IAM authority). The approach: invite the
  existing subsidiary account into the parent's AWS Organization (accounts can be invited, not just created
  fresh), place it under the appropriate Workloads OU, and let the OU's already-existing SCPs apply
  immediately on join — no IAM changes required, because SCPs are an org-level guardrail layered on top of
  whatever IAM the subsidiary already has.


  
Case 2: The regulator's boundary

  
A healthcare analytics company must prove to an auditor that its HIPAA-scoped workload cannot share
  infrastructure, IAM entities, or a blast radius with its unrelated internal-tools workload, which is managed
  by a different, less security-conscious team. The approach: separate AWS accounts, not separate VPCs
  or separate IAM roles in one account — the audit boundary the regulator wants is exactly the boundary an
  account provides natively (distinct resource namespace, distinct CloudTrail trail, distinct service quotas),
  and it can't be argued away by a misconfigured policy the way an in-account boundary can. An SCP on the
  HIPAA-scoped account's OU can additionally enforce guardrails like disallowing resources outside an approved
  region, but the account boundary itself is what makes the compliance argument clean.


  
Case 3: Scaling account creation faster than the platform team

  
A fast-growing SaaS company's platform team currently creates each new AWS account by hand — copying an
  IAM baseline, re-attaching the standard SCPs, manually wiring up centralized logging — and it takes roughly a
  day per account, with the occasional forgotten step. The company plans to onboard 40 new product teams into
  their own accounts over the next two quarters. The approach: AWS Control Tower's Account Factory. This
  is the textbook "standing up new accounts at scale with consistent guardrails" scenario, and it's precisely
  what Control Tower automates: every new account gets the same baseline OU placement, the same preventive and
  detective guardrails, and the same centralized logging wiring, without a human re-typing the same setup 40
  times and eventually missing a step on account 27.

jsonregion-restriction.json — example SCP document

    
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "DenyOutsideApprovedRegions",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "NotAction": [
        "iam:*", "organizations:*", "support:*",
        "sts:*", "cloudfront:*", "route53:*"
      ],
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringNotEquals": {
          "aws:RequestedRegion": ["us-east-1", "us-west-2"]
        }
      }
    }
  ]
}

  


  

    
bashattach-policies.sh — example syntax

    
aws organizations create-policy --name "RegionRestriction" \
  --type SERVICE_CONTROL_POLICY \
  --description "Deny all regional actions outside us-east-1/us-west-2" \
  --content file://region-restriction.json

# create-policy returns a policy ID (p-xxxxxxxx); attach it to a target
# — an OU, an account, or the root — with attach-policy:
aws organizations attach-policy --policy-id p-xxxxxxxx --target-id ou-abcd-workld1

  


  

    
WHY THE EXAM CARES ABOUT THIS EXACT SYNTAX

    
SAP-C02 scenario questions frequently describe the outcome of a CLI call or a policy document
    ("an administrator ran a command that..." / "given the following SCP...") and ask you to predict what
    happens, rather than asking you to write the command yourself. Recognizing create-policy vs
    attach-policy as two separate steps (authoring a policy document doesn't restrict anything
    until it's attached to a target) and recognizing the NotAction + region-condition pattern as
    the standard "region lock" SCP shape are both extremely common exam patterns.

GATE · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

  
The five facts most likely to appear on test day

  
One: an SCP can only restrict permissions, never grant them, and is evaluated alongside IAM, never
  instead of it — effective permission is the intersection of what IAM allows and what the applicable SCPs
  allow. Two: the management account is exempt from SCPs attached to it, which is exactly why it should
  stay nearly empty of real workloads. Three: a single AWS account cannot create an org-wide guardrail,
  because the same administrators who'd need to be restrained also control the restraint — multiple accounts
  give you blast-radius isolation, clean billing separation, and provable compliance boundaries that IAM policy
  alone cannot. Four: the SCP document size limit (5,120 characters, commonly published) and the OU
  nesting limit (5 levels including the root, commonly published) are real, exam-testable numbers — but treat
  any quota you're not sure of as "check current AWS docs," not as a guess. Five: whenever a scenario
  describes standing up many new accounts at scale with consistent guardrails, Control Tower's Account Factory
  and landing-zone automation is almost always the intended answer over hand-rolled Organizations scripting.
