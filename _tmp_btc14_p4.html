ARC 09 · TRADEOFFS

Scaling payments, five ways: honest comparison

Lightning is one point in a design space every serious scaling proposal occupies. None of these dominate the others — each trades away a property Bitcoin's base layer guarantees, in exchange for throughput or cost:

APPROACHWHAT IT BUYSWHAT IT COSTSTRUST MODEL
On-chain (base layer) no counterparty, no liveness requirement ~7 tx/s ceiling 10+ min settlement Trustless — full validation
Lightning channels instant, high-volume, low-fee payments O(1) on-chain footprint per relationship capital locked per channel must stay online or delegate (watchtower) routing can fail silently Trust-minimized — enforced by chain, needs liveness
Custodial exchange balances unlimited internal throughput, zero capital lockup for the user full counterparty trust exchange can freeze, lose, or steal funds Fully trusted third party
Optimistic / ZK rollups (chapter 12 of the Blockchain Fundamentals course) amortizes many transfers into one settlement no per-pair capital lockup withdrawal delay (optimistic) or proving cost (ZK) not native to Bitcoin's UTXO model without extra tooling Trust-minimized via fraud proofs or validity proofs
Statechains transfers coin ownership without an on-chain transaction relies on a semi-trusted, non-custodial-in-theory operator not colluding with a past owner Trust-minimized, but weaker than channel penalties

Read the table as confirmation of chapter 12's trilemma, applied one layer up: Lightning buys throughput and speed by requiring capital lockup and liveness — it does not buy anything for free. The honest pitch for Lightning specifically is narrower than "solves Bitcoin's scaling": it is the best-understood mechanism for repeated, bidirectional, small-value transfers between parties who can plan ahead to open a channel. It is a poor fit for a single infrequent large payment to a stranger with no existing route — exactly the case where paying on-chain, once, is still the simpler and often cheaper answer.

ARC 10 · PERFORMANCE & SECURITY CHARACTERISTICS

What you can predict before running a node

Cost and complexity, derived rather than benchmarked:

  • Per-hop cryptographic cost: one ECDH + two ChaCha20 stream operations (encrypt payload, generate padding) per onion layer — negligible versus network latency, which is why Lightning payments settle in low hundreds of milliseconds across several hops rather than the block-interval timescale of on-chain confirmation.
  • Routing search cost: a sender runs shortest-path search (Dijkstra or a variant) over the locally cached channel graph — tens of thousands of channels network-wide as of the mid-2020s, so a full-graph Dijkstra run is milliseconds on ordinary hardware. The expensive part is not computing a route; it's that the computed route might not actually have the liquidity to carry the payment (below).
  • Force-close cost: a unilateral close publishes the broadcaster's current commitment transaction plus, for every in-flight HTLC, a separate claim transaction — one on-chain transaction per pending HTLC, at whatever the prevailing feerate is at the moment of the dispute, which is usually the worst possible moment (§11 covers exactly this failure).
  • Penalty economics: broadcasting a revoked state is rational only if the expected gain (the balance difference versus the current state) exceeds the near-certain loss of the entire channel to a watching counterparty, plus the fee to claim it. For any channel where the honest party (or their watchtower) checks the chain within the to_self_delay window, expected value of cheating is strongly negative — which is the point.
  • Channel reserve: BOLT #2 requires each party to keep a minimum reserve (commonly ~1% of channel capacity) that can never be moved to the other side. This isn't arbitrary — it guarantees both parties always have some balance to justify a force-close's fee and keeps at least one output above the dust limit chapter 6 defined, so a commitment transaction is never left unable to pay for its own eventual settlement.

ROUTING FAILURE: THE DOMINANT REAL-WORLD UX PROBLEM

The network deliberately does not gossip each channel's directional balance — only its total capacity, and its fee/CLTV policy, are announced (BOLT #7 channel_update). Advertising live balances would leak exactly the transaction-graph information channels exist to hide, and balances change every payment, which would flood the gossip network with updates. The direct consequence: a sender can compute a route that is well-formed and cheap on paper, attempt it, and have it fail at hop 3 of 5 because that specific channel happens to be nearly drained in the needed direction — information no one could have told them in advance. Real implementations compensate with pathfinding heuristics (probabilistic liquidity models updated from past successes/failures), multi-part payments (splitting one payment across several smaller HTLCs over different routes, BOLT #4's MPP), and retry-on-failure — but "will this route work" remains, structurally, a question you answer by trying, not by asking.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS

Three failures that changed how channels are built and operated

Incident 1: fixed-fee commitments and the stuck force-close (→ Anchor Outputs, 2020)

Original commitment transactions had their fee baked in and signed at commitment-update time, based on whatever feerate looked reasonable then. Bitcoin's fee market (chapter 11) is not stable minute to minute; during the fee spikes of 2017–2018, some channels' pre-signed commitment transactions carried feerates that were reasonable when signed and far too low by the time a force-close actually needed to confirm — and because the transaction was already fully signed by both parties, there was no way to bump its fee without a fresh signature from a counterparty who, in a dispute, might be exactly the party with no incentive to cooperate. Root cause: coupling fee selection to signing time in a construction whose entire premise is that broadcast might happen much later, adversarially, when neither party can be assumed to cooperate. Fix: BOLT #3's anchor outputs update added two small, always-present "anchor" outputs to every commitment transaction, spendable by anyone after a delay, specifically so either party can attach a child transaction and use Child-Pays-For-Parent (chapter 11) to bump the commitment's effective feerate at broadcast time, unilaterally, using their own separate funds — decoupling "the fee that was fair when we signed" from "the fee needed to actually confirm."

Incident 2: Flood & Loot (Harris & Zohar, 2020)

Academic disclosure, not a live theft, but a precise demonstration of a real structural weakness. The paper showed that an attacker controlling both ends of several routed HTLCs could deliberately let many timeouts converge on the same block height, then flood the mempool with their own competing, low-value transactions right before that height — congesting it enough that honest nodes' timeout-claim transactions (needed to reclaim funds from expiring HTLCs) miss their deadline. Once a timeout is missed, the attacker's own preimage-based claim on the other side of the same HTLC succeeds instead, so the attacker recovers funds that were never economically theirs. Root cause: a fixed, public block-height deadline creates a predictable congestion target, and Bitcoin's fee-priority mempool means congestion is a purchasable resource. Mitigation, and its limit: wider safety margins (larger cltv_expiry_delta) and fee-bumping via anchor outputs both help, but neither eliminates the underlying tension — chapter 11's pinning discussion is the general form of this same problem, and no complete fix exists that doesn't cost either capital efficiency (wider margins tie up funds longer) or added on-chain fee spend.

Incident 3: the replacement cycling attack (Antoine Riard, disclosed 2022–2023)

A more targeted descendant of the same family. Bitcoin Core's replace-by-fee rules (BIP125, chapter 11) let a transaction be replaced by a conflicting one paying a higher fee. Riard's disclosure showed that an attacker could construct their own low-value transaction that conflicts with (double-spends an input needed by) a victim's justice or HTLC-claim transaction, then repeatedly replace their own conflicting transaction with a slightly different version of itself — a "replacement cycle" — each cycle evicting the victim's pending transaction from mempools it had just re-entered, without the attacker's transaction ever actually confirming. Cycled for long enough, this pushes the victim's confirmation past their HTLC timeout, letting the attacker claim funds on the other side that should have reverted. Root cause: RBF's replacement rules were designed around single, independent transactions; they did not anticipate an adversary using the ability to replace their own transaction, repeatedly and cheaply, purely to deny mempool space to someone else's time-sensitive transaction. This directly informed the 2023 mempool-policy discussions on the bitcoin-dev mailing list about full-RBF and pinning more broadly — an area chapter 11 flags as still actively evolving.

STILL UNRESOLVED: CHANNEL JAMMING

As of this writing, channel jamming has no complete deployed fix. An attacker who controls both ends of a route (or just wants to grief) can send HTLCs that they never intend to settle, holding routing nodes' liquidity locked for up to the maximum timeout (which can be on the order of two weeks for a long route) at negligible cost to themselves — a denial-of-service against routing capacity rather than against funds. Proposed fixes (unconditional forwarding fees charged regardless of success, reputation/stake-based admission, HODL-invoice restrictions) each trade away something — usually fee simplicity or permissionless routing — and none has reached the kind of universal deployment BOLT-level standardization implies. This is a legitimately open problem, not a solved one being explained after the fact.
