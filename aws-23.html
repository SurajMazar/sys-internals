ARC 01 · EXAM CONTEXT

The blueprint this whole chapter is organized around

This chapter maps to every domain of the AWS Certified Solutions Architect – Professional (SAP-C02) exam simultaneously, because that is exactly what a full-length practice run has to do. Before you touch a single practice question, the four numbers below need to be load-bearing knowledge, not trivia:

  • 75 questions in 180 minutes — multiple-choice and multiple-response, mixed without warning.
  • 65 scored + 10 unscored questions, and you cannot tell which is which during the exam — the only sane strategy is to treat every question as if it counts, because functionally it might.
  • Pass score: 750 out of 1000 — a scaled score, not "you need 75% raw." Different exam forms weight questions slightly differently to stay fair across candidates, so don't try to reverse-engineer "how many can I get wrong" from the 750 number; it doesn't map to a fixed raw count.
  • Exam fee: $300 at the time of writing — AWS certification pricing changes periodically, so treat this as a planning estimate and confirm the current fee before you register.

And the spine of the whole exam — the four domains and their real weights, which is also exactly the proportional distribution this chapter's practice questions and §15's timed set are built against:

WHERE THIS EXAM CAME FROM

The current version of this exam is SAP-C02. Its predecessor, SAP-C01, was retired in 2022 — if you find older study material online, check which version it targets, since domain names and weights shifted between versions. Everything in this chapter targets SAP-C02 as it exists today.

Why a full timed run matters specifically now, this late in prep: reading about a decision framework and executing it correctly under a ticking clock, after 74 other questions have already tired out your judgment, are different skills. §15's simulator is built to train the second one — not to teach you anything new about S3 or DynamoDB, but to pressure-test whether you can still apply chapters 16–22's frameworks when it's minute 140 and you're behind pace.

ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"Just pick the cheapest one"

Ask a candidate who has passed the Associate exam but never sat Professional what their instinct is on a long scenario question, and most describe the same shortcut: skim the stem for the company's situation, skim the four answers, and pick whichever one is obviously the least expensive or least operationally heavy — because that's usually what "the right AWS answer" felt like on SAA-C03.

That instinct is not irrational. It is even usually correct on the Associate exam, where most scenario questions test one decision framework at a time (which storage class, which load balancer type, which DR tier) and the cheapest option that technically works genuinely is the intended answer more often than not.

The Professional exam is designed to break that exact habit. A SAP-C02 stem routinely states three or four independent constraints in the same paragraph — a migration deadline, a compliance/data-residency rule, an RTO/RPO target, a budget cap — and buries one candidate answer that is cheapest and violates one of the other stated constraints. Candidates who anchor on cost first pick that answer, feel confident about it, and are wrong — not because they misunderstood any AWS service, but because they optimized the wrong variable.

THE TRAP, NAMED PRECISELY

It is not "candidates don't know AWS services." It is anchoring on a single axis — almost always cost — and treating every other explicit sentence in the question as flavor text instead of as a hard constraint that eliminates answers. The fix, developed in §4, is mechanical: constraints get listed and checked before cost ever enters the decision.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

The exam scores constraint satisfaction, not single-axis optimization

Here is the mechanism, stated precisely: a SAP-C02 scenario question is graded as right or wrong against a design that must satisfy every explicit requirement in the stem simultaneously. An answer that is excellent on three axes and fails one stated, non-negotiable constraint is not "80% right" — it is wrong, full stop, with the same weight as an answer that fails on every axis.

Make it concrete with numbers. Suppose a stem states: "the company's compliance team requires all customer data to remain within the EU," "the CFO has capped this project's first-year spend at $50,000," and "the current data-center contract ends in 60 days." Now compare two candidate answers:

  • Answer A: Rehost into eu-west-1 using AWS Application Migration Service, completed well inside the 60-day window, first-year cost ≈ $38,000. Satisfies all three constraints.
  • Answer B: Refactor into a fully serverless architecture in us-east-1 (cheaper region, first-year cost ≈ $22,000) with a promise to migrate the data to the EU "in a later phase." Cheaper by $16,000 — and wrong, because it violates the residency constraint today, not eventually.

Answer B is not "the more cost-optimized choice with a minor caveat." It is a design that fails a hard requirement stated in plain English in the question. The exam does not average across axes; a violated explicit constraint fails the whole answer regardless of how well it performs everywhere else. This is the precise sense in which "pick the cheapest" is not merely a suboptimal heuristic on this exam — it is a category error about what is being measured.

THE QUANTIFIED TAKEAWAY

A $16,000 saving does not offset a data-residency violation, an RTO miss, or a blown migration deadline — because those are pass/fail gates in the grading logic, not inputs to a weighted average. Every stated constraint in a stem is a filter to run before cost, not a tiebreaker to run after.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

Real exam mechanics you're graded under whether you know them or not

These aren't AWS service limits — they're the rules of the exam itself, and misunderstanding any of them costs time or points for reasons that have nothing to do with your architecture knowledge.

  • 75 questions, 65 scored + 10 unscored — indistinguishable. There is no visual marker, no pattern, no "these are always at the end." Treat every single question as scored, because you cannot know otherwise.
  • 750/1000 is a scaled score, not a raw percentage. AWS calibrates scoring across exam forms of slightly different difficulty; do not try to compute "I can afford to miss N questions" from 750 — it isn't a fixed count, and chasing that number is wasted mental energy better spent on the next question.
  • You can mark questions for review and revisit them. The exam UI lets you flag a question and jump back to it before final submission — this is precisely why §8's triage protocol says "never leave a question blank," because a flagged guess still has a chance; a blank answer never does.
  • There is no built-in calculator. You are expected to reason about relative cost magnitudes ("Spot is meaningfully cheaper than On-Demand," "cross-region data transfer costs more than same-region") rather than compute exact dollar figures — the exam does not test arithmetic, it tests whether you know the right order of magnitude and direction of a tradeoff.
  • Retake policy: a minimum 14-day wait between attempts on the same exam, and you pay the fee again each attempt. This is a real, if unwelcome, part of pacing your prep — it argues for not scheduling the exam until a full practice run under real time pressure feels comfortable.
  • The certification NDA prohibits sharing real exam content — which is exactly why every question in this chapter, and every other chapter in this course, is an original construction modeled on real domain weights and real service behavior, never a claim to reproduce actual exam material.

READING THE SHAPE

Even pace is a straight line from 180 to 0 — simple, but leaves zero slack for a flagged question you want to revisit. Front-loaded pacing (spending disproportionate time on the first third of the exam, which is common when early questions feel unfamiliar) forces a steep, risky sprint through the back third. The reserved-buffer strategy deliberately answers at a slightly faster pace than "even" so it reaches question 75 with real minutes left over — exactly the minutes you'll want for every question you flagged in §8's triage protocol.

ARC 11 · SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three original, layered scenarios — worked with the elimination method

DISCLAIMER Every scenario below is an original construction written for this course. None of them is, or is claimed to be, a real AWS certification exam question.

Case study 1 — the migration deadline that hides a compliance trap

Scenario: MedRecords Inc. must migrate 200 on-premises application servers before its data-center lease ends in 60 days. Patient data must remain within the same AWS Region for HIPAA-adjacent contractual reasons. Budget is "reasonable but not unlimited" — no hard number given.

Constraints listed: (1) 60-day hard deadline, (2) single-region data residency, (3) unstated but real cost sensitivity. Elimination: A full refactor to microservices is eliminated by constraint 1 — no team refactors 200 applications in 60 days safely. A "migrate to a new region with better pricing" answer is eliminated by constraint 2. Surviving: rehost via AWS Application Migration Service (MGN) into the same or a compliant region, which is fast, minimal-change, and keeps data in place. Sanity check: this is a Migration & Modernization-domain question, and "fast rehost under deadline pressure" is exactly its characteristic shape — confirms the survivor.

Case study 2 — the cost-optimization answer that breaks resilience

Scenario: An existing order-processing fleet runs 40 On-Demand EC2 instances at a steady, predictable load, 24/7, with a contractual 99.95% uptime SLA to a downstream customer. Finance wants the compute bill cut significantly this quarter.

Constraints listed: (1) steady, predictable, always-on load — no interruption tolerance stated, (2) a contractual uptime SLA, (3) a real cost-reduction mandate. Elimination: Moving the fleet to Spot Instances is eliminated — Spot's 2-minute interruption notices are fundamentally incompatible with an uptime-SLA-bound, steady, non-fault-tolerant workload. Doing nothing is eliminated — it ignores the stated cost mandate. Surviving: a Compute Savings Plan or Reserved Instances sized to the steady baseline, which cuts cost deeply without introducing interruption risk. Sanity check: Continuous Improvement domain, "reduce cost on an existing, stable workload without touching its risk profile" is its textbook shape.

Case study 3 — three constraints, one deciding factor

Scenario: A logistics company is standing up a brand-new cross-border tracking platform. It must (1) keep shipment data within the country of origin for several different countries simultaneously due to differing local data-sovereignty laws, (2) support near-real-time updates visible to customers worldwide, (3) launch within one fiscal quarter, and (4) minimize new operational headcount.

Elimination: A single-region global database is eliminated by constraint 1 outright — data sovereignty per country is non-negotiable, and no single-region design can satisfy "data stays in-country" for multiple countries. A fully custom multi-region active-active build from scratch on raw EC2 and self-managed databases is eliminated by constraints 3 and 4 — too slow to build, too much new operational burden. Surviving: a design using per-country regional deployments (data residing in-region) with a managed, near-real-time cross-region data service such as DynamoDB Global Tables scoped so that each country's authoritative data stays local, or an equivalent per-region deployment pattern with asynchronous cross-region status updates only (not raw customer data) flowing globally. The deciding constraint here is data sovereignty — it is the one constraint that, if violated, makes every other quality of the design irrelevant, which is exactly the kind of "find the deciding constraint" judgment §4's step 4 trains.

JSONexample.scp.json — Service Control Policy denying non-approved regions
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "DenyOutsideApprovedRegions",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": "*",
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringNotEquals": {
          "aws:RequestedRegion": ["eu-west-1", "eu-central-1"]
        }
      }
    }
  ]
}

An Effect: Deny SCP with a Condition block is a recurring shape in Organizational Complexity questions — the exam expects you to recognize that this denies the action account-wide, regardless of that account's own IAM permissions.

bashillustrative AWS CLI — kick off a DMS replication task
# Example syntax only — replace ARNs/identifiers with real values
aws dms create-replication-task \
  --replication-task-identifier "oracle-to-aurora-cutover" \
  --source-endpoint-arn "arn:aws:dms:eu-west-1:111122223333:endpoint:SRC" \
  --target-endpoint-arn "arn:aws:dms:eu-west-1:111122223333:endpoint:TGT" \
  --replication-instance-arn "arn:aws:dms:eu-west-1:111122223333:rep:INST" \
  --migration-type full-load-and-cdc \
  --table-mappings file://table-mappings.json

full-load-and-cdc is the pairing the exam expects you to recognize for "migrate the data and keep replicating changes until cutover" — full-load alone leaves a gap between the initial copy and the cutover moment.

yamlillustrative CloudFormation — a StackSet deployed across an OU
Resources:
  GuardrailStackSet:
    Type: AWS::CloudFormation::StackSet
    Properties:
      StackSetName: org-wide-baseline
      PermissionModel: SERVICE_MANAGED
      AutoDeployment:
        Enabled: true
      OperationPreferences:
        MaxConcurrentPercentage: 25
        FailureTolerancePercentage: 10
      StackInstancesGroup:
        - DeploymentTargets:
            OrganizationalUnitIds: ["ou-abcd-11112222"]
          Regions: ["eu-west-1", "eu-central-1"]
bashillustrative AWS CLI — share a Transit Gateway via RAM
aws ram create-resource-share \
  --name "shared-tgw-networking" \
  --resource-arns "arn:aws:ec2:eu-west-1:111122223333:transit-gateway/tgw-0123456789abcdef0" \
  --principals "arn:aws:organizations::111122223333:ou/o-abc123/ou-abcd-11112222"
WHY THE EXAM CARES ABOUT THIS SYNTAX

You will not be asked to write any of this from memory. You will be asked to read a short snippet or CLI fragment embedded in a question or answer option and correctly infer what it does — which SCP effect wins, whether a DMS task keeps replicating after the initial copy, which accounts a StackSet or RAM share actually reaches. Recognition, not recall, is the tested skill.

GATE · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

Before chapter 24: what actually matters from this chapter

Five things worth carrying into the real exam room, in order of how often they change an outcome:

  • List constraints before you look at cost. Every stated number or requirement in a stem is a filter to apply before optimizing anything.
  • An explicit constraint violation fails an answer regardless of its other merits. There is no partial credit for "cheapest, but non-compliant."
  • Treat every question as scored — you cannot tell the 10 unscored ones apart from the 65 that count.
  • Never leave a question blank. Flag and move on; a guess has a chance, a blank answer never does.
  • 750/1000 is scaled, not raw — don't spend exam-room mental energy reverse-engineering "how many can I miss."

Chapter 24 is this course's capstone: instead of one more isolated topic, you'll assemble a full multi-tier, multi-account architecture end to end against a stated set of real constraints, and get computed feedback on whether each piece actually satisfies its requirement — the constraint-elimination discipline from this chapter, applied at the scale of an entire design instead of one question.
