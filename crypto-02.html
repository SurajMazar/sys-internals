APPLIED CRYPTOGRAPHY · CHAPTER 02

Symmetric Ciphers I: Block Ciphers
& the Feistel Network

Chapter 1 ended with a bind: the one-time pad is the only cipher ever proven unbreakable, and it is unusable — its key must be as long as every message you will ever send, used exactly once, and shared in advance over some other, already-secure channel. Everything since 1970 in symmetric cryptography is an attempt to get most of the OTP's guarantees back using a short, reusable key. This chapter derives the first — and still most influential — engineering answer: the Feistel network, and the cipher built from it that the U.S. government ran the entire financial system on for over two decades, DES.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

You need a permutation with a trapdoor, and you need it cheap

Strip away the history and the standardization politics, and a block cipher is answering one precise engineering question: can you build a keyed family of functions Ek over some fixed-size block — say n bits — such that for every key k, Ek is a bijection on those n-bit blocks — a permutation — and yet an adversary who doesn't know k cannot distinguish Ek from a uniformly random permutation chosen from the 2n! that exist?

That property has a name — a pseudorandom permutation (PRP) — and it decomposes into three separate requirements that pull in different directions:

  • Bijective, for every key. The receiver must be able to recover a unique plaintext. There is zero tolerance here: even one colliding pair of inputs anywhere in the domain, for any key, is a correctness bug, not a security nuance — §3 and §4 make this concrete with a real collision you can compute by hand.
  • Cheap to invert, given the key. Not just theoretically invertible — invertible fast enough that the legitimate receiver can decrypt every packet, on commodity hardware, in real time. A permutation that takes a supercomputer-year to invert even with the key is useless.
  • Indistinguishable from random, without the key. The strongest thing you can honestly claim about a 65-bit-or-larger block cipher today is not "unbreakable" — chapter 1 already showed you what an information-theoretically unbreakable cipher costs. It's "no attack known that does meaningfully better than brute-force key search," a claim that is falsifiable, gets re-evaluated every year, and is precisely what cryptanalysis (§6, §10, §11) spends its time trying to break.

The naive way to satisfy all three is to make key material choose one specific permutation out of the 2n! possible ones and just store it — a giant lookup table mapping every input block to its output. §3 tries exactly that, and §4 shows why it is not merely inefficient but physically impossible past trivial block sizes. The tension between "must be a true bijection" and "must be buildable from a short key using cheap arithmetic" is what this whole chapter resolves.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE NEXT 14 CHAPTERS

Every symmetric primitive from here on — AES (ch.3), the stream ciphers built on top of block-cipher-like primitives (ch.4), the modes that turn a single-block permutation into something that encrypts gigabytes (ch.5), and the MACs and AEAD constructions built on the same substrate (ch.6–7) — inherits its properties from how well its underlying permutation approximates "random." The Feistel network is the first practical answer to that question that shipped at scale, and every later design (including AES's very different one) is, in part, a reaction to what this chapter's cipher got right and wrong.

ARC 02 · HISTORICAL CONTEXT

1973–1999: from a classified IBM project to a public execution

Unlike chapter 1's ciphers, block ciphers were never a solitary insight — they were shaped by an unusual three-way tension between an industrial research lab (IBM), a standards body (the National Bureau of Standards, NBS — now NIST), and a signals-intelligence agency with its own, initially undisclosed, agenda (the NSA). DEEP CONTEXT · SHANNON'S 1949 BLUEPRINT, TWENTY-FIVE YEARS EARLY

Claude Shannon's 1949 paper "Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems" (the same paper chapter 1 used for perfect secrecy) also named the two properties every practical cipher since has chased: confusion — make the relationship between key and ciphertext as complex as possible, ideally so each ciphertext bit depends on the key in a way no simple equation captures — and diffusion — spread each plaintext bit's influence over many ciphertext bits, so statistical structure in the plaintext (the very thing that broke Vigenère in chapter 1) gets smeared out. Horst Feistel's contribution, a quarter-century later, was an architecture that lets you build both properties out of small, cheap, repeatable pieces instead of one enormous, hand-designed function.

ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: make the round function do everything, and make it invertible by hand

If a cipher just needs to be a keyed bijection, the most obvious design is: define one function that mixes the key into the block thoroughly, and make sure that function itself is invertible, so decryption just runs the inverse. No swapping, no halves, no rounds-within-rounds — one function, one inverse.

pseudocodenaive_cipher.c — the strawman, annotated
/* "Mix the whole byte with the key, then scramble it." Looks like a
 * round function. The question §4 asks: is scramble_byte() actually
 * a bijection on 0..255, for every key? */
uint8_t scramble_byte(uint8_t x, uint8_t key) {
    uint8_t mixed = x ^ key;         /* XOR: bijective on its own — fine so far */
    uint8_t out   = (mixed * 2) & 0xFF;  /* "extra confusion": multiply by 2, truncate to a byte */
    return out;
}

/* Decryption must invert scramble_byte() exactly. To undo the XOR you
 * XOR again with the same key — trivial. To undo the multiply-by-2,
 * you need to divide by 2. On an 8-bit register, can you? */
uint8_t unscramble_byte(uint8_t out, uint8_t key) {
    uint8_t mixed = out / 2;             /* ??? — see §4 */
    return mixed ^ key;
}

This looks reasonable, and it is exactly the kind of function a competent engineer reaches for first: XOR the key in (cheap, and definitely reversible), then apply some nonlinear-looking arithmetic for "extra security." The bug is not in the code's style — it's in a property the author never checked: is (x*2) mod 256 a bijection? §4 answers that with an arithmetic proof you can verify on a phone calculator, and it is fatal, not fixable by adding more rounds of the same operation.

The second naive idea, once you notice the first one's flaw, is to route around it entirely: stop trying to invert an arithmetic expression, and instead let the key select an explicit, guaranteed-bijective lookup table — a full permutation of every possible block, stored as data rather than computed. Decryption just looks up the inverse table. No arithmetic to get wrong, ever.

NAIVE ATTEMPT #2 — SKIP THE MATH, JUST STORE THE PERMUTATION

For an n-bit block, precompute a table T of 2n entries where T[i] is the ciphertext for plaintext i under the current key, guaranteed to be a bijection because you build it by construction (e.g. a keyed shuffle of 0..2n-1). Encryption is T[plaintext]; decryption is a lookup in the precomputed inverse table. Correctness is now trivial to guarantee. §4 shows exactly where this idea breaks — and it isn't correctness.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

One fails on correctness, the other on physics — and both point at the same fix

Attempt #1 is not a bijection, and you can prove it in one line. (x*2) mod 256 maps every input x and x+128 to the identical output, because 2·(x+128) = 2x + 256 ≡ 2x (mod 256). Concretely: scramble_byte(10, 0) = 20 and scramble_byte(138, 0) = 20 — two different plaintext bytes, same ciphertext byte, same key. unscramble_byte(20, 0) cannot know which one you meant. This isn't a rare edge case: doubling mod 256 maps all 256 inputs onto only 128 distinct outputs, so every single ciphertext byte is two-way ambiguous, always, for every key. No amount of extra rounds recovers information a non-bijective step already destroyed — once two inputs collide, they stay collided forever, downstream of that point.

THE GENERAL LESSON

If a cipher's encryption function is built by directly composing arithmetic/logical operations end to end, every single operation in that chain must itself be a bijection on its full output width — not "usually invertible," not "invertible unless the input is unlucky." That rules out plain addition with carry truncation, AND, OR, any modular multiplication by a non-unit, and any operation that compresses width (e.g. hashing two bytes into one). Design freedom shrinks drastically, and worse: the compiler and the CPU will happily let you write a non-bijective "cipher" that looks fine in every test until the one input that collides walks in.

Attempt #2 is correct — and unbuildable. A table-based cipher needs one full permutation table per key, with 2n entries. For a modest 64-bit block (DES's actual size, chosen in §7):

THE ARITHMETIC

264 ≈ 1.8447 × 1019 entries. Even packing each entry as a raw 8-byte output (ignoring the index, which you'd also need to store or imply), that table is 1.8447 × 1019 × 8 ≈ 1.4757 × 1020 bytes — roughly 147.6 exabytes, per key, regenerated every time the key changes. Global data-center storage today is measured in low zettabytes; one 64-bit permutation table alone would consume a meaningful fraction of it, and you would need a fresh one for every key a single server ever uses. AES's 128-bit block (ch.3) makes this 2128 — a number larger than a rough estimate of the atoms in the observable universe. The table approach isn't slow. It's impossible, full stop, past a trivially small block.

Put the two failures side by side and the real requirement snaps into focus: you need a construction where the pieces you compose don't each have to be bijections (fixing attempt #1's problem) and you never have to materialize a whole-block permutation table (fixing attempt #2's problem). Section 5 is that construction.

ARC 05 · THE FEISTEL INSIGHT

Split the block. Only require the combination step to be invertible.

Horst Feistel's structure, refined at IBM through the Lucifer project (§2), makes one substitution that dissolves both failures at once: split the n-bit block into two halves, L and R, and define one round as

THE FEISTEL ROUND

Li = Ri-1
Ri = Li-1 ⊕ F(Ri-1, Ki)

where F — the round function — can be any function whatsoever. It can lose information. It can be exactly attempt #1's broken (x*2) mod 256. It does not need to be a bijection, and it never gets inverted directly. Why is the whole round still perfectly invertible? Because F's output only ever gets XORed into Li-1 — and XOR is its own inverse: (a ⊕ b) ⊕ b = a, always, regardless of what b is or how it was computed. Given (Li, Ri) and the same key, you recover Ri-1 = Li directly (it's just sitting there from the swap), recompute F(Ri-1, Ki) yourself — you have everything needed to compute it — and XOR it against Ri to recover Li-1.

Stack many rounds of this and every round function can be a cheap, small, deliberately lossy mess of table lookups and rotations — exactly the kind of thing attempt #1 tried to use directly and failed with — and the overall cipher stays exactly, provably invertible. No whole-block permutation table is ever built (attempt #2's problem gone too): each round only needs a small function on a half-block.

A NOTE ON THE FINAL SWAP

Textbook Feistel diagrams — and DES itself, via its final permutation (§7) — add one more half-swap after the very last round, purely so ciphertext bytes come out in the same left/right order plaintext went in. Our toy cipher skips that cosmetic step: whatever (L,R) the last round produces is the ciphertext. Decryption compensates by doing the swap explicitly at both ends — swap the ciphertext halves, run the identical round function with the key schedule reversed, then swap back. §8 and §15 make this concrete end to end.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL COSTS

The Feistel network is invertible for free. Security and speed are not.

Solving invertibility doesn't solve security or performance, and the bill for both is precise and payable in full knowledge of the mechanism above:

  • Only half the block changes meaningful content per round. Look at the round equation again: Li is literally just the previous R, unchanged. A single round leaves half the state a verbatim copy of input — terrible diffusion on its own. Reaching the point where every output bit has been influenced by every input bit and every key bit (full avalanche) takes several rounds, not one — §8's slider widget lets you watch this happen, or fail to, bit by bit.
  • DES's actual key is 56 bits, not 64. The stored key is 64 bits, but 8 of those are parity-check bits left over from an era of unreliable key transmission (§7's key schedule drops them via PC-1). Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman publicly objected in 1976 that 56 bits was already borderline brute-forceable by a well-funded adversary — a claim that looked alarmist in 1976 and looked exactly correct twenty-two years later (§11).
  • DES's block is 64 bits — small enough to collide. A 64-bit space sounds enormous until you apply the birthday bound: encrypt about 232 blocks (≈4.3 billion, ≈32 GB of traffic) under one key in a chaining mode, and you have better-than-even odds of two ciphertext blocks colliding — a collision that leaks the XOR of the two corresponding plaintext blocks. This sounds academic until §11's Sweet32.
  • Software hates bit-level permutations. DES's expansion and permutation steps (§7) move individual bits to arbitrary positions — cheap as fixed wiring in a 1970s integrated circuit, but expensive on a modern CPU that wants to operate on whole bytes and words at once. Software DES implementations are notoriously slow relative to their key size; AES (ch.3) was designed from the start around byte- and word-aligned operations, and eventually got dedicated CPU instructions (AES-NI) that DES never received.
  • Doubling the rounds doesn't double the security. The obvious patch for a 56-bit key — encrypt twice with two independent keys ("2DES," 112 key bits) — is broken down to roughly 257 effort by a meet-in-the-middle attack, barely more than single DES. The real fix, triple-DES (§9), needed a specific three-key encrypt-decrypt-encrypt structure and still only reaches about 112-bit effective security at three times the computation.
ARC 07 · FINAL ARCHITECTURE

DES: the Feistel idea, wired into a 1975 standard

DES (FIPS 46, 1977) is the Feistel round from §5, run 16 times over a 64-bit block split into two 32-bit halves, wrapped in a keyless bit-shuffle at each end and driven by subkeys derived from a 56-bit key through a fixed rotation schedule. THE TRICK QUESTION: WHAT DOES IP ACTUALLY BUY YOU?

Nothing cryptographic. IP and FP are exact inverses of each other with 16 full Feistel rounds sandwiched between them — remove the rounds and they cancel completely. They exist because 1970s hardware engineers found that particular bit-wiring convenient to lay out in silicon, not because it adds confusion or diffusion. It's a genuinely popular interview trap for exactly this reason (§12).

ARC 08 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

A real toy Feistel cipher, computed live

DES's real round function operates on 32-bit halves with 48-bit subkeys and eight hand-built S-boxes (§13 has the actual constants). To make every value in this chapter checkable by you, in your head or on a calculator, this section instead builds and runs a genuine — if deliberately tiny — Feistel cipher: 8-bit halves (16-bit block), 16 rounds, a real substitution box built from actual GF(2⁸) field arithmetic (the same finite field AES's real S-box starts from, previewed here and finished properly in chapter 3), and a real key schedule. It is not secure — a 16-bit key is 65,536 guesses, crackable in microseconds — but every operation is the authentic mechanism, just scaled down enough to watch.

The round function, exactly

JavaScriptthis chapter's actual cipher core (see §15 for full source in the page)
function feistelF(R, K, round) {
  const mixed = SBOX[(R ^ K) & 0xFF];  // real GF(2⁸) inverse, see below
  const rot   = 1 + (round % 5);   // rotation varies by round, like DES's varying shifts
  return rotl8(mixed, rot);              // F need not be, and here is not, a bijection
}
function feistelRound(L, R, K, round) {
  return [R, L ^ feistelF(R, K, round)];   // used VERBATIM for encrypt AND decrypt
}

SBOX is built once, at load time, from real arithmetic: multiply bytes in GF(28) reduced modulo x8+x4+x3+x+1 (0x11B — AES's exact reduction polynomial), build exponent/logarithm tables over generator 3, and invert every nonzero byte as a-1 = 3255-log₃(a). DES's own S-boxes are not built this way — they are eight opaque, hand-picked 6-to-4-bit tables with design criteria the NSA didn't publish until 1994 (§13's callout); this chapter's algebraic version is used specifically so you can verify there's no hidden trapdoor in it.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR ROUND COUNTS

At 1–2 rounds you should see almost no spread — the flipped bit hasn't left its own half yet if it started in L, or has only gone through F once if it started in R. By round 5–6 (on this tiny 16-bit toy) it should look close to the ~8-of-16 bits an ideal random function would flip. DES's real 64-bit block needed all 16 rounds to reach that same saturation point at a much larger scale — which is exactly why "just use fewer rounds to go faster" was, and still is, a real attack surface (reduced-round DES variants are meaningfully weaker, and reduced-round cryptanalysis is a standard tool against every modern cipher too).

ARC 09 · TRADEOFFS

Feistel is one answer to §1's question. It's not the only one.

CONSTRUCTIONENCRYPT/DECRYPT CIRCUITDIFFUSION PER ROUNDREAL EXAMPLESPRIMARY WEAKNESS
Feistel network identical — reverse key order half the block only DES, 3DES, Blowfish, Twofish, Camellia needs many rounds; historically paired with small (64-bit) blocks
Substitution-Permutation Network (SPN) usually needs a separate inverse circuit whole block, every round AES (ch.3), Serpent, PRESENT every internal step must itself be invertible — doubles implementation surface (ch.3)

Read this as proof that "half-block diffusion needing many small rounds" versus "whole-block diffusion needing fully-invertible steps" is a real, unavoidable design fork, not a mistake either side made. Chapter 3 picks up the SPN branch and shows exactly what "every step must be invertible" costs and buys in AES's actual design.

ARC 10 · PERFORMANCE / SECURITY CHARACTERISTICS

Brute force, in real operations, over three decades

A 56-bit keyspace means 256 ≈ 7.2 × 1016 possible keys, and an average successful brute-force search checks half of them: 255. What that costs in wall-clock time is entirely a function of how many keys per second the attacker's hardware can test — and that number has moved by roughly seven orders of magnitude since DES shipped. READ THE 40-BIT BAR CAREFULLY

40-bit keys are not a hypothetical — 1990s U.S. export-control regulations legally capped many cipher products shipped internationally at 40 effective key bits, precisely because that was believed (correctly) to be within reach of a well-resourced attacker while 56+ bits was not, at the compute costs of that era. The gap between "legal to export" and "safe" is a policy decision layered on top of physics, not a cryptographic one — a theme that recurs with Logjam's export-grade Diffie-Hellman in chapter 11.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS

Three ways this exact design has failed in production

Incident 1: EFF's Deep Crack (1998) — the key length argument settled by hardware

Twenty-two years after Diffie and Hellman's public warning (§6), the Electronic Frontier Foundation built "Deep Crack": a purpose-designed machine of 1,856 custom search chips, reportedly built for around $250,000, able to test on the order of 90 billion DES keys per second. It found the correct key for RSA Security's "DES Challenge II-2" in about 56 hours. The lesson was not "DES is broken" in the cryptanalytic sense — no shortcut attack was used, only exhaustive search — it was that 56 bits had quietly become a hobbyist-adjacent budget item, not the province of nation-states. Four months later, Deep Crack combined with distributed.net's volunteer computing network to win DES Challenge III in 22 hours 15 minutes.

Incident 2: Sweet32 (CVE-2016-2183 / CVE-2016-6329) — the 64-bit block comes due

In 2016, researchers Karthikeyan Bhargavan and Gaëtan Leurent showed that DES's descendants didn't need their key broken at all: 3DES (still deployed in TLS cipher suites and Blowfish in some OpenVPN configurations) shares DES's 64-bit block, and CBC mode (chapter 5) leaks the XOR of two plaintext blocks whenever two ciphertext blocks collide. The birthday bound puts that collision within reach after only 232 blocks — about 32 GB — under one key, entirely realistic for a long-lived HTTPS or VPN session carrying repeated, partially predictable data like cookies. The fix wasn't a patch to 3DES; it was retiring 64-bit-block ciphers from TLS and VPN cipher suites entirely, which is exactly what §6 predicted purely from the block-size math, years before an exploit existed.

Incident 3: DES's weak and semi-weak keys — a key-schedule design gap

DES has exactly 4 "weak" keys (all-zero and all-one C/D register halves after PC-1, among a couple of others) for which every one of the 16 round subkeys turns out identical — making the cipher an involution: encrypting a block twice under a weak key returns the original plaintext. There are also 12 "semi-weak" keys that pair up so that encrypting under one key equals decrypting under its partner. Neither flaw is exploitable in normal use (an attacker who can already choose your key doesn't need this), but they are a textbook example of what a key schedule (§7, §13) must be checked against: not just "is the key long enough," but "does the schedule ever degenerate into something structurally weaker than a generic key would produce." Every later cipher's key schedule design explicitly checks for exactly this class of gap.

The round function's shape, in reference pseudocode — this is exactly §5's feistelRound, with F filled in for real:

pseudocodeone DES round, f(R, K) expanded
function des_round(L, R, K_i):
    expanded   = E(R)                 # 32 → 48 bits, duplicates 16 bits
    mixed      = expanded XOR K_i        # the ONLY place key material enters this round
    six_bit_groups = split_into_6(mixed)  # eight groups of 6 bits
    substituted = concat(S1(g[0]), S2(g[1]), ..., S8(g[7]))  # 48 → 32 bits
    f_out      = P(substituted)       # fixed 32-bit transposition
    return (R, L XOR f_out)              # identical shape to feistelRound() in §5/§8

And the key schedule that produces each round's K_i — 16 shifts, one per round, of two 28-bit registers seeded from the 56 non-parity key bits:

pseudocodeset_key.c — PC-1, the shift schedule, PC-2
(C0, D0) = PC1(key64)         # drop 8 parity bits, split 56 → two 28-bit halves
shifts = [1,1,2,2,2,2,2,2,1,2,2,2,2,2,2,1]  # fixed, not data-dependent
for i in 1..16:
    (C_i, D_i) = rotate_left(C_{i-1}, shifts[i-1]), rotate_left(D_{i-1}, shifts[i-1])
    K_i = PC2(C_i, D_i)        # select + permute 48 of the current 56 bits
WHY THE S-BOXES WERE BUILT THIS WAY — CRYPTANALYSIS THAT WAS CLASSIFIED FOR 20 YEARS

In 1994, Don Coppersmith (an IBM researcher on the original Lucifer/DES team) published "The Data Encryption Standard (DES) and its strength against attacks," revealing that IBM's team had discovered differential cryptanalysis internally around 1974 — sixteen years before Eli Biham and Adi Shamir published it openly in 1990 believing they had discovered something new. DES's S-boxes, and specifically the NSA's involvement in adjusting them before standardization, had been deliberately hardened against exactly this attack the whole time; the reduction from Lucifer's 128-bit key to 56 bits was the price of admission for that undisclosed hardening. The lesson for today: opaque, hand-tuned design criteria can be sound — but you cannot audit "trust us" until it's declassified, and DES's own history is the reason modern standardization (AES's open 1997–2000 NIST competition, ch.3) insists on public, adversarial review from day one instead.

You can now derive why a Feistel round's function never needs to be invertible, trace DES's real 16-round structure piece by piece, and quantify exactly what its 56-bit key and 64-bit block cost in brute-force time and birthday-bound risk. Chapter 3 stays with block ciphers but switches architecture entirely: AES is a Substitution-Permutation Network, transforming the whole block every round — which means every one of its steps must be invertible on its own, and buys full-block diffusion in far fewer rounds.
