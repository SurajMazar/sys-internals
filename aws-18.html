AWS CERTIFICATION PREP · CHAPTER 18

Migration Strategies:
The 7 Rs & Migration Tooling

SAP-C02 loves to describe a company with 400 servers, a deadline, and a wish list, then ask which migration strategy fits. The trap is always the same: the "best" answer on paper (rewrite everything as cloud-native) is rarely the right answer for the stated constraint (get out of the data center in 60 days). This chapter drills the real 7 Rs framework, the AWS services that execute each one, and the discipline of matching strategy to constraint instead of to architectural ambition.

You will be able toName and correctly apply all seven migration strategies to a scenario, not just recite them.
You will understandExactly which AWS service executes which R, and why Migration Hub isn't a migration tool at all.
You will predictWhen a stated deadline or budget eliminates Refactor as a valid answer, no matter how attractive it looks.
You will simulateFeeding real scenario constraints into a strategy picker and watching the correct R and tool get selected with a visible reasoning trace.
ARC 01 · EXAM CONTEXT

Where this sits in the blueprint

This chapter maps to the AWS Certified Solutions Architect – Professional (SAP-C02) domain "Accelerate Workload Migration and Modernization," which carries a real 20% weight — tied for the second-largest domain on the Professional exam. It is a domain almost entirely about judgment: given a company's stated constraints (timeline, budget, staff skill, licensing, compliance), pick the migration strategy and the AWS service that actually satisfies them. There is comparatively little raw service trivia here and a lot of "which of these four plausible answers is the one the scenario is actually asking for."

The Associate exam (SAA-C03) does not have a dedicated migration domain, but a basic version of this question — "should this get a lift-and-shift or a rewrite?" — can still appear inside Associate-level cost/resiliency scenarios, which is why one of this chapter's sample questions is labeled introductory rather than strictly Professional. On the Professional exam, expect this material to show up dressed as a company narrative: an acquisition, a data-center exit, a licensing renewal nobody wants to sign again.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE NEXT 6 CHAPTERS

Chapters 18–24 form the Professional-level home stretch of this course. ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"Obviously we should refactor — it's the best long-term outcome"

Here is the instinct almost every technically strong candidate walks in with: of the seven migration strategies, Refactor / re-architect is the one that produces the most cloud-native, most elastic, most cost-optimized end state. Serverless functions instead of always-on servers, managed databases instead of self-managed instances, event-driven microservices instead of a monolith. If you only cared about where the workload should end up, Refactor wins almost every comparison. So when a scenario describes a company migrating to AWS, the reflex is to reach for the fanciest transformation available and present it as the obviously correct answer.

A typical exam scenario reads something like this: "A company's data center lease expires in 90 days. They run a 400-server monolithic order-processing application on VMware. The company wants to modernize while migrating. Which approach should the Solutions Architect recommend?" The candidate who has internalized "cloud native is always better" reaches for microservices, managed databases, and event-driven decoupling — a full Refactor — and picks the answer option that describes exactly that. It reads like the most sophisticated, most senior-sounding choice on the page.

THE TELL

Notice what that scenario actually contains: a hard, specific, numeric deadline — 90 days — sitting right next to a request to "modernize." The exam put both of those facts in the same paragraph on purpose. Whenever a scenario pairs an aggressive timeline with a wish for modernization, that is the exam testing whether you'll notice the conflict, not whether you know that Refactor exists.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

Refactor is the slowest, highest-skill, highest-cost R by construction

Refactoring 400 servers' worth of monolithic order-processing logic into microservices and serverless components is not a lift; it is a redesign. It requires re-architecting data models, rewriting business logic around new service boundaries, building out CI/CD and observability for many independently deployable pieces instead of one, and running an extended period of dual operation while the new design is validated against production traffic. Realistically that is a multi-quarter-to-multi-year program even for a well-resourced team — and the scenario gave you 90 days. A design that cannot fit inside the stated constraint is not a "more ambitious correct answer"; on this exam it is a wrong answer, full stop, regardless of how technically superior the end state would be.

This is the single most important reframe in this chapter: the exam is not asking "what is the best architecture in the abstract?" It is asking "what satisfies every constraint actually stated in this scenario?" A deadline is a constraint. A budget ceiling is a constraint. "The team has never operated a managed database and has six weeks of runway" is a constraint. Refactor fails against tight-timeline and limited-skill constraints not because it's a bad strategy in general, but because it is the one strategy whose cost structure — time, expertise, risk of a stalled cutover — is largest by definition. It buys the most cloud-native benefit and it charges the most for it.

  • Time: Rehost can move hundreds of servers in weeks using continuous block-level replication. Refactor requires a redesign cycle measured in months to years.
  • Skill: Rehost requires installing an agent and validating a test launch. Refactor requires application architects, new operational tooling, and a team that can safely run the new design.
  • Risk: A stalled or partially-complete Refactor leaves you running two systems in parallel indefinitely — often worse than the original problem, and directly threatening a hard deadline like a lease expiration.

The correct move in the 90-day scenario is to separate the two goals in time: rehost now to hit the deadline safely, then refactor selected components later once the pressure is off. AWS's own migration guidance explicitly frames this as a normal, mature pattern — "migrate, then modernize" — not a compromise. §4 walks the whole framework in order so this decision rule becomes automatic.

LEGACY TOOL NAME THE EXAM MIGHT STILL MENTION

AWS Server Migration Service (SMS) was an earlier agent-based server-replication tool. If you see "SMS" on an older-styled question, treat it as the predecessor to MGN, not as the current recommended answer.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

Where each tool's clean story gets complicated

DMS: homogeneous is simple, heterogeneous needs SCT

When source and target run the same database engine — a self-managed SQL Server on EC2 moving to Amazon RDS for SQL Server, for example — this is a homogeneous migration. DMS alone handles it: schema is structurally the same, so DMS's job is purely moving and continuously replicating data. When source and target run different engines — Oracle moving to Amazon Aurora (MySQL-compatible), for example — this is heterogeneous, and DMS's job gets harder: DMS replicates data, but it does not rewrite schema, views, stored procedures, or functions written in the source engine's dialect. That conversion work is exactly what the Schema Conversion Tool (SCT) exists for — it runs before DMS starts replicating and converts what it can automatically, flagging anything (often complex stored procedures) that needs manual rework. A common exam wrong-answer pattern is "just use DMS" for a scenario that explicitly names two different engines — that is always missing SCT.

MGN: what it actually needs on the source side

MGN works by installing a lightweight replication agent on each source server (physical, virtual, or another cloud), which continuously replicates block-level changes to a staging area in your AWS account over a network path (internet, VPN, or Direct Connect — any of the three work, but obviously bandwidth and latency affect how fast the initial full sync completes). Because replication is continuous, most of the data is already synced well before cutover day — the actual cutover downtime window is short: it only needs to catch up on the final delta and switch traffic, not copy the whole server from scratch. MGN also supports launching fully isolated, non-disruptive test instances from the replicated data before you ever touch production traffic — the exam likes to test whether you know cutover can be rehearsed safely.

When the network itself becomes the constraint

Both MGN's initial replication and DMS's full-load phase move data over the network. If the workload involves a very large data volume and a comparatively thin or unreliable network path, network replication time can exceed the project's timeline entirely — no amount of retrying will make an insufficient pipe move a petabyte in a week. That's precisely the scenario the Snow Family of physical data-transfer devices addresses: ship encrypted physical storage instead of streaming bytes. §8 and §10 build the actual math behind "when does physical shipping win?" instead of asserting a number.

A SUBTLE ONE: RETAIN ISN'T FAILURE

Retain gets treated like a consolation prize in study guides, but the exam uses it seriously: a workload tightly coupled to specialized on-prem hardware, or one under a regulatory hold that legally prevents it from leaving a jurisdiction right now, correctly gets Retain — not "Rehost eventually anyway." Migration Hub is still useful here: you record the workload as retained-for-now so it stays visible in the overall wave plan and gets revisited on a schedule, instead of silently falling off the migration roadmap.

WHY MIGRATION HUB ISN'T "STEP 2"

Migration Hub is a tracking and aggregation layer, not an execution tool — it has no idea how to move a server or a database by itself. It pulls status from Application Discovery Service, MGN, and DMS and gives program leadership one dashboard across every wave. The exam's favorite wrong-answer pairing is "use Migration Hub to migrate the databases" — Migration Hub tracks that migration; DMS (plus SCT if heterogeneous) executes it.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three original vignettes (not real exam content — this chapter's own constructions)

Case 1 — Northfield Freight: the lease is the constraint

Northfield Freight runs 380 servers across two colocation racks supporting a monolithic dispatch and routing application. Their colocation contract expires in 100 days and will not be renewed at any price the business will accept. There is executive appetite for "modernizing while we're at it," but no appetite for missing the exit date and paying month-to-month colocation penalties. Correct call: Rehost via MGN. The replication agent goes on all 380 servers now, initial sync runs continuously in the background while the team keeps operating normally, test launches validate the cutover in an isolated environment, and the whole fleet cuts over inside the 100-day window. Refactor candidates get identified and scheduled after the exit, as a second, unhurried phase.

Case 2 — Meridian Claims: the license renewal nobody wants to sign

Meridian Claims runs a large self-managed commercial database on EC2 whose annual license renewal has become the single largest line item in their infrastructure budget. The team wants off that license and onto a managed engine, but the application's SQL and stored procedures are deeply tied to the current engine's dialect, and a full rewrite of the application isn't funded. Correct call: Replatform via DMS + SCT. SCT converts the schema and as much procedural logic as it safely can to the new managed target; DMS handles the ongoing data replication and cutover; the application's external interface changes minimally, but the license cost and operational burden of patching/backing up the database disappear.

Case 3 — Larkspur Outfitters: replacing a homegrown system with a mature product

Larkspur Outfitters built its own customer-relationship tracking tool a decade ago. It works, but every feature request competes with the core business for engineering time, and mature commercial CRM products now cover everything it does and more. There's no meaningful "migration architecture" question here — the decision is whether to keep paying to maintain a homegrown system or switch products. Correct call: Repurchase. Customer data gets exported and imported into the new SaaS product; the old system is retired once the cutover is validated. No AWS migration-execution service does the heavy lifting here — the "migration" is largely a data export/import and a change-management exercise.

WHY THE EXAM CARES ABOUT THIS SYNTAX SHAPE

You will rarely be asked to write CLI from scratch, but you are routinely asked to spot the wrong service in a plausible-looking command, or the missing prerequisite step (SCT before DMS on a heterogeneous pair; agent install before describe-source-servers will return anything for MGN; start-test before start-cutover as the safe order of operations). Recognizing the shape — which service owns which verb — is the actual skill being tested.

GATE · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

The five facts most likely to earn or lose you points

  • Know all seven Rs by name, exactly: Retire, Retain, Rehost, Replatform, Repurchase, Refactor (re-architect), Relocate. Distractor options love to invent an eighth-sounding term or blur two of these together.
  • Constraint → R → Tool, never the reverse. The instant a scenario states a hard deadline or a limited budget/skill set, Refactor is very likely eliminated regardless of how attractive it looks — that's this chapter's central trap, and it repeats across the whole Professional exam in other disguises.
  • MGN = rehost. DMS (+ SCT for heterogeneous engines) = replatform/database migration. Migration Hub tracks a multi-wave program — it does not execute a migration itself. Mixing these three up is the single most common wrong-answer pattern in this domain.
  • SMS is legacy — MGN is the current rehost answer. If a question's wording feels dated, that's a hint, not a reason to pick the deprecated tool.
  • When the network can't move the data in time, physical transfer devices win — compute it, don't guess it. Transfer time scales linearly with data size at fixed bandwidth; past a crossover volume, no amount of patience fixes an undersized link.

Think about these before moving on — no multiple choice, just formulate the answer in your head first.
