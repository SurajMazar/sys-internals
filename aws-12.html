Content Delivery & DNS: CloudFront
& Route 53

Two services that get bundled together in casual conversation and pulled apart mercilessly on the exam. This chapter draws the real line: CloudFront caches content for speed, Route 53 routes and health-checks for resilience and compliance, and the two only become a real DR story when you deliberately wire origin failover and DNS failover together rather than assuming either one covers the other.

ARC 01 · EXAM CONTEXT

Where this chapter sits on the real blueprint

This chapter maps to Design High-Performing Architectures on the Associate exam (SAA-C03, 24% of the exam) and Design Resilient Architectures on the same exam (26%) — an unusual chapter in that it sits meaningfully inside two domains at once, because CloudFront is fundamentally a performance tool and Route 53 is fundamentally both a performance and a resilience tool, depending on which routing policy a scenario reaches for.

The exam consistently probes whether you understand that CloudFront's caching and Route 53's routing/health checks are two independent mechanisms that happen to work well together — not one feature with two names. Conflating them is one of the most common associate-level mistakes this chapter exists to correct.

WHY THIS MATTERS ON TEST DAY

A scenario that mentions "global users" and "fast" is not automatically a CloudFront-only question, and a scenario that mentions "failover" is not automatically solved by caching. Learn which mechanism does which job — cache for speed, DNS routing and health checks for reaching the right healthy origin — and most of this chapter's traps disarm themselves.

Both services are also billed primarily on usage (requests, data transfer, and query volume) rather than a flat reservation, which is worth knowing conceptually for the Cost-Optimized Architectures domain elsewhere in this course, even though this chapter deliberately avoids quoting a specific current price, since exact rates change over time and this course prefers durable, verifiable facts over numbers that will go stale.

These two services also show up constantly as supporting infrastructure in questions that are nominally about something else entirely — a migration scenario cutting traffic over to a new environment, a cost scenario weighing regional data transfer, even a security scenario about restricting where content can be viewed. Treat this chapter's vocabulary as something you'll reuse across the rest of both exams, not a topic confined to one chapter.

ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"Put CloudFront in front of it and it's resilient now"

Here is the instinct: a scenario asks for a highly available global application, and the tempting design adds a CloudFront distribution in front of a single origin and calls the availability requirement satisfied — on the theory that CloudFront's globally distributed edge locations make the whole system more resilient.

A second, equally common wrong instinct: whenever a scenario mentions "route traffic based on where users are" or "gradually roll out a new version," candidates default to Simple routing in Route 53 because it's the first policy in the list and sounds like the safe, boring choice — without checking whether the scenario actually needs weighting, latency-awareness, or failover behavior that Simple routing structurally cannot provide.

WHY IT'S TEMPTING

CloudFront genuinely does make a global application faster and does reduce load on the origin — so it feels like it's making things more resilient too. But caching a response and surviving an origin outage are different problems, solved by different mechanisms, and the exam is specifically testing whether you can tell them apart.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

Caching is not failover, and Simple routing cannot fail over at all

A CloudFront distribution with a single origin and no origin failover configured will happily keep serving cached content for a while if that origin goes down — but the moment a cache entry expires (its TTL is reached) or a request needs to go through to origin at all (a cache miss, or content explicitly marked non-cacheable), CloudFront has nowhere else to go, and the request fails exactly as it would with no CDN in front of it at all. Caching buys you time and reduces average origin load; it does not substitute for a second, healthy origin.

THE FIX, STATED PRECISELY

Real origin-level resilience in CloudFront requires an explicit origin group with origin failover — a primary and secondary origin, where CloudFront automatically retries a failed request against the secondary when the primary returns a defined failure status. Nothing about adding CloudFront by itself creates this behavior; it has to be configured on purpose.

The second wrong instinct fails for a structural reason: Simple routing has no concept of a health check at all — it just returns the configured record(s) for a name, unconditionally. If the only server behind that record is down, Simple routing will keep confidently returning its IP address to every client, forever. A scenario mentioning failover, gradual rollout, or performance-aware routing is explicitly ruling Simple routing out — those behaviors require Failover, Weighted, or Latency-based routing respectively, covered in §5.

ARC 04 · THE CORRECT APPROACH

Deriving the right routing policy and cache design

Choosing a Route 53 routing policySTEP-BY-STEP
THE ONE-LINE VERSION

Need lowest latency across regions → Latency-based. Need a gradual, controllable traffic shift for a rollout → Weighted. Need automatic failover to a standby when a primary becomes unhealthy → Failover, which requires a health check. Need to serve or restrict content by the user's location for compliance or licensing → Geolocation. Need none of the above, just one static answer → Simple. Layer CloudFront on top of whichever origin(s) that routing resolves to, specifically for the parts of the response that are cacheable.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

What the correct answer still has to respect

  • DNS caching means failover is bounded by TTL, not instant. Resolvers and clients cache a DNS answer for the record's TTL; lowering TTL speeds up failover propagation but increases query volume against Route 53 — a real, honest tradeoff the exam expects you to reason about rather than treat TTL as a free dial.
  • Route 53 health checks monitor endpoint health; they know nothing about CloudFront's cache state. A health check can mark an origin unhealthy and trigger DNS failover, but it is checking the origin (or a calculated/CloudWatch-alarm-based composite health check) — not whether CloudFront has a fresh cached copy of anything. These are two independent monitoring surfaces.
  • CloudFront's geo-restriction and Route 53's geolocation routing are not the same feature — the exam's favorite conflation trap in this chapter. CloudFront geo-restriction is an allow/block list at the edge, entirely within one distribution, typically used for licensing or compliance ("don't serve this content in these countries"). Route 53 geolocation routing returns a different DNS answer (potentially a different regional endpoint entirely) based on the resolver's geographic location. A scenario needing both "restrict content by country" and "route to the nearest region" needs both features, deliberately combined.
  • OAC (Origin Access Control) is how a CloudFront distribution reaches a private S3 bucket correctly. Making the bucket public defeats the point and directly conflicts with the "least privilege" principle the Secure Architectures domain tests elsewhere — OAC lets CloudFront authenticate to S3 using a scoped bucket policy, keeping the bucket itself private.
  • Cache invalidation has a real operational cost and is not the default answer to staleness. Explicitly invalidating many paths is the expensive, blunt tool; versioned object keys or query-string cache-busting (e.g. app.js?v=42) is the standard, cheaper, more scalable pattern the exam rewards for content that changes on every deploy.
  • Signed URLs and signed cookies restrict access to cached content without touching S3 permissions at all. They're the standard answer whenever a scenario needs to grant time-limited or user-specific access to content served through CloudFront — distinct from, and complementary to, OAC's job of securing the origin itself.
THE CLASSIC COMBINED TRAP

A scenario needs EU users to see only EU-licensed content, and also wants those same EU users routed to the lowest-latency regional endpoint. The trap answer picks one feature and assumes it does both jobs. The correct read: Route 53 geolocation (or latency-based) routing decides which regional endpoint a user reaches; CloudFront geo-restriction independently decides which content is allowed to be served at all, regardless of which endpoint answered — both are configured, deliberately, because they answer different questions.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three original vignettes, written the way the real exam phrases them

These are original scenarios written for this course to illustrate the exam's phrasing style — they are not drawn from, and do not claim to reproduce, any real exam question.

Case 1 — the global media site with static assets and a personalized API

A media company serves mostly static video-thumbnail and script assets alongside a personalized recommendation API that must never serve stale, cross-user results. Correct approach: one CloudFront distribution with two distinct cache behaviors by path pattern — a long TTL for the static asset path, and a short-or-zero TTL (or fully bypassed caching) for the personalized API path — rather than one TTL setting applied uniformly across the whole distribution.

Case 2 — the company running a canary deployment

A company wants to send exactly 10% of production traffic to a new application version before a full rollout, with the ability to adjust that percentage without a deployment. Correct approach: Weighted routing in Route 53, with two records (old and new version) at a 90/10 weight split — no application-level routing logic required, and the split can be adjusted live by changing the weights.

Case 3 — the company that must serve EU users only EU-licensed content

A streaming company must ensure EU-based users see only EU-licensed content and must also route those same users to the lowest-latency regional endpoint available. Correct approach: both features, deliberately combined — Route 53 latency-based (or geolocation) routing decides which regional endpoint answers, while CloudFront geo-restriction independently decides which content that distribution is allowed to serve at all, regardless of which endpoint handled the request. Picking only one of the two leaves the other requirement unmet — precisely the trap in §6.

Case 4 — the company migrating traffic to a new environment gradually

A company is migrating from a legacy data center to AWS and wants to shift real production traffic over in small, reversible increments while watching error rates closely, rather than cutting over all at once. Correct approach: Weighted routing between the legacy environment's record and the new AWS environment's record, starting the AWS weight small (e.g. 5%) and increasing it over time — reversible instantly by setting the weight back to 0 if the new environment shows problems, with no code change on either side.

Each command maps to a decision tested as prose: "automatic failover" → a health check attached to a Failover-type record, not just adding a second record; "keep this bucket private" → OAC plus a scoped bucket policy, never a public bucket; "content changes on every deploy" → versioned keys over repeated invalidation. ARC 16 · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

What to be sure of before you move on

Four facts carry most of this chapter's exam weight. Caching is not failover — a CloudFront distribution needs an explicit origin group with origin failover to survive a primary origin outage; caching alone only delays the moment a dead origin becomes visible. Simple routing cannot fail over — it has no health-check concept at all, so any scenario requiring automatic failover is explicitly ruling it out in favor of Failover routing. CloudFront geo-restriction and Route 53 geolocation routing are different features answering different questions — one decides what content is allowed to be served, the other decides which endpoint answers a query — and a scenario needing both compliance and performance-aware routing needs both, deliberately combined. And Origin Access Control, not a public bucket, is the correct way for CloudFront to reach a private S3 origin.

One habit worth carrying forward: whenever a scenario elsewhere in this course mentions "global," "fast," or "users in a specific region," pause and ask which of this chapter's two jobs it's actually describing — caching for speed, or routing/health-checking for correctness and resilience — before reaching for either service by reflex — that one-sentence gut check resolves most of this chapter's exam traps on its own.
