BITCOIN INTERNALS · CHAPTER 01

Genesis: The Whitepaper,
Decoded

On October 31, 2008, a nine-page PDF appeared on a cryptography mailing list under the name Satoshi Nakamoto. It contained no marketing, no roadmap, and no promises — just a precise engineering claim: that a payment system could be Sybil-resistant and totally ordered without any trusted party, using nothing but hash functions, digital signatures, and spent CPU cycles. This chapter reads that document the way you'd read an RFC before implementing it — section by section, claim by claim — decodes the genesis block it produced byte by byte, and is honest about the places the paper's own analysis didn't hold up.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

Digital cash needs a trusted third party — until it doesn't

A bit string is free to copy. That single fact is the entire reason digital cash is hard: if a "coin" is just data, nothing stops its owner from sending the identical bytes to two different people at once. Physical cash doesn't have this problem — a paper bill can only be in one wallet — and every digital-cash design before 2008 solved it the same way physical banking does: put a trusted, central bookkeeper in the loop who checks a ledger before honoring any transfer.

Nakamoto's whitepaper opens by naming that dependency as the problem, not a fact of life: "commerce on the Internet has come to rely almost exclusively on financial institutions serving as trusted third parties to process electronic payments... What is needed is an electronic payment system based on cryptographic proof instead of trust." Unpack what "instead of trust" has to actually deliver, and you get a precise, falsifiable engineering requirement with four parts:

  • No double-spend — once a coin is spent, every participant must be able to tell that a second, conflicting spend of the same coin is invalid, without asking a bank.
  • No privileged party — no single node, company, or government can unilaterally freeze, reverse, or create funds. Anyone can leave and anyone can join.
  • Sybil resistance — in an open, permissionless network, anyone can claim to be a thousand different participants for free. Whatever mechanism decides "the truth" cannot be a majority vote by identity, because identities are free to manufacture.
  • Byzantine tolerance under partial trust — some participants may be actively malicious, and the system must still converge on one consistent history as long as enough of the network is honest.

Each of the whitepaper's twelve sections exists to satisfy one slice of this list, and by the end of this chapter you'll have all twelve mapped to a mechanism and a future chapter of this course. That map itself is this chapter's simulator (§15) — but you can't appreciate the destination without first walking, honestly, through the decade of failed and partial attempts that preceded it, which is where §2 begins.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE NEXT 15 CHAPTERS

Every subsystem in this course exists because of a decision made to satisfy one of these four requirements. Public-key cryptography and addresses (chapter 2) exist so ownership can be proven without identity. The UTXO model and transaction format (chapters 3, 6) exist so double-spending is locally checkable. Proof-of-work and the block chain (chapters 7–9) exist so an open, Sybil-able network can still agree on one order of events. The P2P protocol and mempool (chapters 10–11) exist so that agreement can be reached without anyone being in charge of relaying it. Nothing in this system is decorative — trace any component back far enough and you land on one of these four bullets.

ARC 02 · HISTORICAL CONTEXT

The cypherpunk decade: what Satoshi actually combined

None of Bitcoin's individual ingredients were new in 2008. Digital signatures, hash chains, proof-of-work, and even the idea of computationally-scarce digital money had each been published, in some form, years earlier — by people the whitepaper cites by name. Reading the genesis chapter honestly means giving each of them credit for exactly what they solved, and being precise about the one piece none of them had: a way for an open, anonymous, permissionless network to agree on a single history without a trusted party anywhere in the loop.

THE SPECIFIC INSIGHT — SAY IT PRECISELY

Every predecessor in that timeline solved half the problem. Chaum's ecash gave you unlinkable payments but needed a bank to stop double-spends. Back's Hashcash gave you provable computational cost but no ledger, no chain, no reuse. Dai's b-money proposed tying money creation to computation but never specified how a leaderless network of untrusted peers agrees on whose ledger is canonical. Szabo's bit gold chained proof-of-work solutions together — remarkably close — but likewise never nailed down the Byzantine-agreement step. Finney's RPOW made proof-of-work reusable, but only by routing every transfer through one trusted server. Nakamoto's contribution was not "hash chains" or "proof-of-work" — both existed. It was noticing that proof-of-work can be reused as a Sybil-resistant voting mechanism, and that the very same votes, tallied as cumulative work rather than block count, also produce a total order — solving Sybil resistance and consensus ordering with one mechanism, for the first time. Section 5 of this chapter re-derives exactly why that double duty works.

ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: a trusted mint that just checks a database

Derive the design space honestly, the way the whitepaper implicitly does before section 3. The simplest possible digital-cash system keeps a single, trusted server that owns the one ledger everyone defers to. Every "coin" is really just an entry in that server's database, and spending a coin means asking the server to update its row and sign off.

PSEUDOCODEnaive_mint.py — the strawman every pre-2008 design reduces to
class TrustedMint:
    def __init__(self):
        self.ledger = {}              # coin_id -> current_owner_pubkey
        self.spent  = set()          # coin_ids already transferred this round

    def transfer(self, coin_id, from_sig, to_pubkey):
        # The ONLY check that matters: has this coin moved already?
        if coin_id in self.spent:
            raise Rejected("double-spend")       # trivial to detect...
        if not verify(from_sig, self.ledger[coin_id]):
            raise Rejected("bad signature")
        self.ledger[coin_id] = to_pubkey
        self.spent.add(coin_id)
        return self.sign(coin_id, to_pubkey)  # ...IF you trust who's doing the checking

This is not a strawman to mock — it is the correct starting point, and it already gets the cryptography right. David Chaum's actual 1982–1990 ecash designs are a far more sophisticated version of exactly this: blind signatures let the mint sign a coin without seeing its serial number, which gives payers real unlinkability, a property Bitcoin itself does not fully match (§10 of the whitepaper is explicit about this). The double-spend check above is also correct and cheap — a hash-set lookup. The entire weakness is architectural, not cryptographic: it lives in the word self.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

Every version of "trust the operator" eventually collapses the same way

The naive mint's flaw isn't hypothetical — it played out twice, in public, in the decade before the whitepaper, against two different kinds of pressure:

  • DigiCash, 1990–1998. Chaum's company built the cryptography correctly and still went bankrupt in 1998. The failure mode wasn't a broken protocol — it was that a single company, as the mandatory counterparty to every transaction, had to individually convince banks and merchants to integrate, could be out-negotiated, and had exactly one point of failure for the whole business. No amount of blind-signature math protects you from your only issuer running out of money.
  • e-gold, 1996–2008. A centralized, gold-backed digital currency that scaled to millions of accounts — and was indicted by the US Department of Justice in 2007 for operating an unlicensed money-transmission business, with its founder pleading guilty in 2008. The system was popular precisely because it had a trusted, accountable operator; that same operator was a single legal entity a government could subpoena, indict, and shut down.
QUANTIFY THE SINGLE POINT OF FAILURE

Model it plainly: if a system's continued operation depends on one legal entity remaining solvent, uncompromised, and un-prosecuted, its effective reliability is that entity's reliability — no cryptographic property of the protocol running on top of it can exceed that ceiling. DigiCash needed one bankruptcy court filing. e-gold needed one federal indictment. A network with N independent operators, none of whom is required for the others to keep functioning, doesn't have this ceiling — it has whatever survives when a minority of them disappear. That single structural difference is the entire argument for decentralization, stated without hand-waving.

So the requirement sharpens: not just "cryptographically secure ledger," but a ledger with no single operator — which immediately raises the harder question the naive mint never had to answer at all: if anyone can run a copy of the ledger, and copies can disagree, who decides which copy is real? Section 5 builds the answer up in the same order the whitepaper does.

ARC 05 · BETTER SOLUTION, DERIVED

Chain the records, then make forgery cost something real

Step through the whitepaper's own reasoning, one substitution at a time. Each step fixes the previous step's specific flaw — by the end you'll have re-derived section 4 of the paper from first principles.

From hash chains to proof-of-workSTEP-BY-STEP
THE RULE, STATED PRECISELY

"Nodes always consider the longest chain to be the correct one" is the whitepaper's own shorthand (§5) for a more precise rule this course will keep sharpening: nodes extend and accept whichever valid chain represents the greatest total proof-of-work, not the greatest block count. The two coincide almost always — they can diverge right at a difficulty retarget boundary, a subtlety chapter 8 covers in a dedicated derivation. For this chapter, the load-bearing idea is the double duty: the exact same computation that makes it expensive to impersonate many voters also makes it expensive to rewrite history, because rewriting means redoing all the work a forged branch would need to catch up on.

ARC 06 · WHAT IT STILL COSTS

The bill for removing trust: energy, latency, and an incomplete proof

Proof-of-work does not make attacks impossible — it prices them. Understand the price precisely, because imprecise versions of this argument ("Bitcoin is unhackable") are exactly what this course refuses to say:

  • Real, ongoing energy expenditure. Unlike Chaum's or Finney's designs, which cost roughly nothing to run once built, proof-of-work's security is a recurring cost paid every ten minutes, forever, by design — the cost is the security property. Chapter 9 quantifies network hashrate and the hardware arms race this produces.
  • The 51% ceiling isn't zero — it's a price, not an impossibility. A participant (or coalition) with a majority of hashrate can, in principle, rewrite recent history and double-spend. Section 11 of the whitepaper computes exactly how the probability of a successful attack falls as confirmations accumulate — and §10 of this chapter reproduces that computation live and then stress-tests the assumptions underneath it.
  • Settlement is slow by construction. A ~10-minute expected interval between blocks, with several confirmations recommended for finality, is a latency the naive centralized mint never had to pay — Chaum's server could confirm a transfer in milliseconds because it didn't need the rest of the world to agree.
A PREVIEW OF WHAT THE MATH LEFT OUT

Section 11's attacker-race analysis is a clean, honest piece of applied probability — and also a simplification its own author never claimed was complete. It models two competing Poisson processes racing from a tie, assuming instantaneous, symmetric block propagation and a single unified attacker. Real networks have propagation delay that measurably favors well-connected miners, and rational miners can do better than "race honestly" by strategically withholding found blocks. Both effects were discovered, formalized, and published years after 2008. §10 walks through exactly what changes once you stop assuming them away.

What survives all of this is still the answer: a decentralized network, with no trusted party, converges on one history by paying real resources for the right to extend it, and the same payment mechanism enforces both Sybil resistance and total order. What that looks like concretely — the actual block Satoshi mined to bootstrap the whole system — is next.

WHY THE 50 BTC CAN NEVER BE SPENT

This is not a scripting bug — the output script is an ordinary, valid pay-to-pubkey script that would spend like any other. The reason is structural: every block after genesis is added to a node's database by connecting it — validating its transactions and inserting its outputs into the UTXO set. The genesis block is different. It is hardcoded into the client itself and its chain state is initialized directly from that constant, without running through the same connection path every subsequent block uses. Its coinbase output was therefore never written into any node's spendable UTXO set in the first place — there is nothing to reference, so a transaction trying to spend it is rejected as spending an output that does not exist. It is a permanent, load-bearing quirk of how the very first block had to be bootstrapped, not a rule anyone consciously designed.

THE SIX-DAY GAP NOBODY FULLY EXPLAINS

Genesis was mined January 3, 2009. Block 1 didn't arrive until January 9 — six days later, against a protocol whose entire design targets a block roughly every ten minutes. At genesis's difficulty (the protocol's floor, target = 0x1d00ffff), a single contemporary CPU should find a block in minutes, not almost a week. The most credible explanation, consistent with Satoshi's own later messages, is simply that the client software wasn't finished — Bitcoin v0.1 wasn't publicly released until January 9, the same day block 1 appears. The gap is a visible fingerprint of a single person building and testing alone, not a network event, and it's a detail the whitepaper itself has nothing to say about.

WHY GENESIS'S NONCE LANDED WHERE IT DID

Genesis's actual nonce, 2,083,236,893, is a little under half of the 232 search space — which is completely unremarkable. Finding a hit is a memoryless process (chapter 8 of the Blockchain Fundamentals course derives the exponential distribution behind this in full): the number of attempts before success has no "expected trajectory" to be ahead of or behind. A find at roughly the halfway point of the space is precisely as likely as a find at the one-hundredth attempt or the four-billionth — Satoshi's genesis nonce is an ordinary draw from that distribution, not a sign of anything unusual.

ARC 09 · TRADEOFFS

Same problem, six attempts: comparative anatomy

Every design in §2's timeline solved digital scarcity or decentralization — never both. Reading them side by side makes Nakamoto's specific contribution impossible to miss:

SYSTEMMECHANISMWHAT IT BOUGHTWHAT IT COULDN'T SOLVE
Chaum's DigiCash (1989–98)Blind-signature ecash issued by a bank strong payer anonymity cryptographically sound required one trusted issuer for every coin no protocol-level double-spend defense — the bank's database WAS the defense
Back's Hashcash (1997)Single-use proof-of-work postage stamp cheap, fully decentralized proof of cost trivial to verify not reusable or transferable no ledger, no chain — a toll booth, not money
Dai's b-money (1998)Proof-of-work-priced money creation among broadcast peers first to tie issuance to computational cost proposed peer-maintained ledgers never specified how untrusted peers agree on ONE ledger without a leader
Szabo's bit gold (1998/2005)Chained proof-of-work puzzle solutions, timestamped chained PoW timestamping remarkably close to a block chain no working Byzantine-quorum mechanism for who owns which solution
Finney's RPOW (2004)Hashcash tokens made transferable via one trusted server first reusable, transferable PoW value server's code was auditable one server = one point of failure and trust, again
Bitcoin (2008/2009)PoW-chained blocks, broadcast to an open P2P network, most-cumulative-work chain wins no trusted party anywhere Sybil resistance AND total order from one mechanism real, recurring energy cost ~10-minute settlement latency throughput capped by block interval/size (btc-11)

Read the table as a proof by construction, not exhaustion: every prior design correctly solved a real piece of the problem, and each piece survives inside Bitcoin — Chaum's anonymity goal reappears, weaker, in address pseudonymity (chapter 2); Back's proof-of-work is the literal mining primitive (chapter 9); Dai's and Szabo's ledger-and-chain intuitions are the block chain itself; Finney was Bitcoin's first transaction recipient for a reason — he understood exactly what problem it had just solved, having spent 2004 failing to solve the last piece of it himself.

ARC 10 · PERFORMANCE & SECURITY CHARACTERISTICS

Section 11's math, reproduced — and stress-tested

The whitepaper's final technical section models an attacker with hashrate share q racing the honest network (share p = 1-q) to build an alternate chain long enough to reverse an already-buried payment. Treating both as competing Poisson processes gives a closed form for the attacker's probability of ever catching up from z blocks behind — the same gambler's-ruin result used to justify "wait for six confirmations." What this honest, correct derivation leaves out

The whitepaper's own math is not wrong — it is a clean, closed-form idealization, and Nakamoto never claims otherwise. But treat it as the whole security story and you'll misjudge real systems. Four gaps, each later formalized in print:

  • Propagation timing isn't symmetric. The model assumes every block reaches every node instantly. Decker & Wattenhofer's 2013 measurement of real Bitcoin block propagation found multi-second delays across the network — long enough that a large, well-connected miner suffers fewer orphaned blocks than a small one, giving big miners an effective advantage above their raw hashrate share that section 11's clean q-vs-p race never represents.
  • "Race honestly" isn't the attacker's best strategy. Eyal & Sirer's 2013 "Majority Is Not Enough" showed that a rational pool can profit by selectively withholding found blocks rather than broadcasting immediately — selfish mining — and that this strategy becomes profitable with as little as roughly 25–33% of network hashrate, well under the 50% threshold section 11 treats as the safety line.
  • SPV's downgrade is bigger than it reads. Section 8 lets a lightweight client trust whichever chain has the most proof-of-work without checking transaction validity itself. That's a real, silent reduction in guarantee — from "I verified every rule myself" to "I trust that whoever built this chain enforced the rules" — and the paper doesn't examine how an eclipse attack (Heilman et al., 2015, isolating a node's peer connections to feed it a fabricated view) exploits exactly that trust for a client with few connections.
  • "One-CPU-one-vote" quietly assumes homogeneous hardware. The paper's egalitarian framing — anyone's CPU counts equally — held for about a year. GPU mining (2010), FPGAs (2011), and ASICs (2013 onward) each represented an order-of-magnitude efficiency jump that plain CPUs could never match, concentrating voting power in whoever could afford and access the newest silicon — a possibility the paper never entertains.
THE OMISSION THE PAPER COULDN'T HAVE PREDICTED

Section 11 also implicitly models miners as atomic, independent CPUs. It has no concept of a mining pool — a coordinator who aggregates thousands of independent miners' partial work ("shares") and pays them proportionally, letting small miners smooth their income variance. Pools didn't exist in 2009; by 2014 one pool operator, GHash.io, briefly exceeded 50% of total network hashrate — a governance and centralization risk with no analogue anywhere in the whitepaper's atomistic-miner model. Chapter 9 covers pools, shares, and Stratum in full; the point here is narrower: the paper's math is correct conditional on assumptions that fifteen years of production Bitcoin quietly stopped satisfying.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS

Six events that define this chapter's history

DigiCash's 1998 bankruptcy

Root cause: a cryptographically sound protocol tied to a single corporate issuer that needed bank and merchant adoption it never secured. The lesson that shaped everything after it: no amount of protocol-level cryptography compensates for a business-level single point of failure.

e-gold's 2007–2008 federal shutdown

Root cause: a centralized digital currency, however popular, is a legal entity with an address a government can serve papers to. Founder Douglas Jackson was indicted in 2007 and pleaded guilty in 2008. This is the concrete, contemporaneous backdrop the whitepaper was written against — published within months of the indictment becoming public.

The genesis block's permanently unspendable 50 BTC

Root cause: the bootstrap code path, detailed in §7 — genesis is never "connected" like every subsequent block, so its coinbase output was never inserted into any node's UTXO set. Not a bug anyone needed to fix; a permanent structural fact about how the first block in any hash-chained system has to be created.

The six-day gap before block 1

Genesis: January 3, 2009. Block 1: January 9, 2009 — the same day Bitcoin v0.1 was publicly released. Root cause, by inference: private development and testing between mining genesis and shipping working software, not any network-level event. A visible fingerprint of a single-author project before it had a network at all.

Block 170 — the first transaction

On January 12, 2009, Satoshi Nakamoto sent 10 BTC to Hal Finney in block 170 — the network's first peer-to-peer transaction, sending value from the person who designed the protocol to the person who had spent 2004 building the closest prior attempt at reusable proof-of-work. Finney later described running the client and corresponding with Satoshi directly in its earliest days, before almost anyone else knew it existed.

Bitcoin Pizza Day and Satoshi's 2011 disappearance

On May 22, 2010, Laszlo Hanyecz paid 10,000 BTC for two pizzas, arranged over the Bitcoin Talk forum — the first documented real-world purchase, and the reason May 22 is still marked every year. Seven months later, Satoshi's public presence started winding down: a final forum post in December 2010, and a final known email in April 2011 to Mike Hearn — "I've moved on to other things. It's in good hands with Gavin and everyone." — handing effective stewardship to Gavin Andresen. Root-cause read, architecturally rather than personally: an anonymous, untraceable founder cannot be subpoenaed, indicted, or pressured into inserting a backdoor — exactly the failure mode that ended e-gold. The disappearance didn't just remove a person; it removed the one thing every prior centralized design had that Bitcoin now structurally lacked — a leader to coerce.

ARC 13 · SOURCE CODE WALKTHROUGH

Reading the real thing: how Bitcoin Core hardcodes genesis

Every implementation that has ever spoken the Bitcoin protocol contains, verbatim, the same handful of constants — because the alternative is not being on the same network. Here is where they live in Bitcoin Core.

src/
├── kernel/
│ ├── ● chainparams.cpp — CMainParams: hardcodes the timestamp string, pubkey, nBits, nTime, nNonce, and the resulting asserts
│ └── chainparams.h — declares CreateGenesisBlock()
├── primitives/block.h — CBlockHeader: the six fields from §7, in serialization order
├── consensus/merkle.cpp — BlockMerkleRoot(): the general N-leaf algorithm; N=1 degenerates to the leaf itself
├── arith_uint256.h — the 256-bit target/work arithmetic behind nBits (chapter 7)
└── validation.cpp — chain-state initialization; genesis is indexed but never run through ConnectBlock() like every other block (§7's unspendability)

First, the generic transaction-and-block assembly every genesis block (mainnet, testnet, regtest — they all call this) shares:

C++chainparams.cpp — CreateGenesisBlock(), the generic form
static CBlock CreateGenesisBlock(const char* pszTimestamp,
                                const CScript& genesisOutputScript,
                                uint32_t nTime, uint32_t nNonce, uint32_t nBits,
                                int32_t nVersion, const CAmount& genesisReward)
{
    // The block's ONLY transaction. A coinbase: no real input to spend,
    // so vin[0] carries the height-486604799 marker and, in this one
    // case, an arbitrary ASCII string instead of a real scriptSig.
    CMutableTransaction txNew;
    txNew.nVersion = 1;
    txNew.vin.resize(1);
    txNew.vout.resize(1);
    txNew.vin[0].scriptSig = CScript() << 486604799 << CScriptNum(4)
        << std::vector<unsigned char>((const unsigned char*)pszTimestamp,
                                       (const unsigned char*)pszTimestamp + strlen(pszTimestamp));
    txNew.vout[0].nValue = genesisReward;              // 50 * COIN — see §7's unspendable output
    txNew.vout[0].scriptPubKey = genesisOutputScript;

    CBlock genesis;
    genesis.nTime    = nTime;
    genesis.nBits    = nBits;
    genesis.nNonce   = nNonce;
    genesis.nVersion = nVersion;
    genesis.vtx.push_back(MakeTransactionRef(std::move(txNew)));
    genesis.hashPrevBlock.SetNull();                    // no parent — the one field that breaks the chain rule
    genesis.hashMerkleRoot = BlockMerkleRoot(genesis);   // one leaf -> root == the coinbase's own txid
    return genesis;
}

        "The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks";

WHY IT WAS BUILT THIS WAY

Every constant here — the timestamp string, the pubkey, the nonce — is a compile-time literal, not configuration. That's deliberate: genesis has to be a shared axiom, identical in every implementation that claims to speak the protocol, and the cheapest possible way to guarantee that is to make disagreement a compile-time or startup-time assertion failure rather than a runtime negotiation. There is no message on the wire that transmits "here is the genesis block" — nodes never need to ask, because the question was answered before the binary was built. This is the same instinct chapter 13's validation rules generalize: consensus-critical facts are the ones you can least afford to leave negotiable.
