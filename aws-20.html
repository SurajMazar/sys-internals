Deployment & CI/CD at Scale:
Blue/Green, Canary & Rollback

The Professional exam does not ask "what does CodePipeline do." It hands you a company's risk tolerance, deployment frequency, and a bad-deploy incident, and asks which of three real deployment strategies — in-place, blue/green, or canary — actually matches the stated constraint, and how CodeDeploy's alarm-triggered rollback closes the loop. This chapter builds the decision framework and the exact mechanics behind each strategy's blast radius.

WHY THIS MATTERS ON TEST DAY

Nearly every deployment-strategy question is secretly a risk-tolerance question wearing a service-name costume. The exam tells you the company's tolerance for downtime, cost, and blast radius in the scenario text — "cannot afford any downtime," "wants to validate with real traffic before full rollout," "needs the fastest possible iteration for an internal tool" — and expects you to map that sentence directly onto in-place, blue/green, or canary. Learn to read the risk sentence, not just the service names.

ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"Blue/green is the safest, so it's always the right answer"

Here is the trap: blue/green deployment sounds like the strictly superior choice — full pre-validated environment, instant traffic cutover, instant rollback by cutting back — so candidates default to picking it for every scenario that mentions "safety" or "zero downtime," without checking the other constraints the question actually gave them. The mirror-image trap: assuming in-place deployment is simply "the cheap, risky option" that a well-run company would never choose — when a scenario describing a low-traffic internal tool with a generous maintenance window is explicitly telling you in-place is perfectly appropriate.

THE INSTINCT, STATED PLAINLY

"Blue/green has the best rollback story, so pick it whenever rollback matters." "Canary is just a slower, more complicated blue/green, so skip straight to blue/green." Both statements miss that these three strategies aren't ranked best-to-worst — they trade cost, validation depth, and blast radius against each other in different directions, and the exam's scenario details tell you which axis actually matters this time.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

Blue/green validates nothing before the cutover — canary validates with real traffic

Blue/green's real mechanism: stand up a complete parallel environment ("green") running the new version, run your own validation against it, then swap traffic over — often instantly, via a load balancer target group swap, a Route 53 weighted record flip, or (for Lambda) an alias pointing at a new version. The rollback story is genuinely excellent: flip traffic back to "blue," which never stopped running. But look at what that swap actually does to risk: the moment you cut over, 100% of production traffic hits the new version simultaneously. If your pre-cutover validation missed a bug that only manifests under real production load patterns — a subtle race condition, a cache-stampede, a downstream dependency that only production traffic triggers — blue/green finds out at 100% exposure, all at once. It also, for the duration both environments run, roughly doubles your compute cost — a real, immediate, and often underestimated tradeoff.

Canary's real mechanism is different in a way that directly answers blue/green's weakness: shift a small percentage of real production traffic to the new version first, watch a CloudWatch alarm you define (error rate, latency, custom business metric), and only then shift more — either in one more jump (canary) or in a sequence of smaller increments (linear). If the alarm breaches, CodeDeploy can automatically roll back before most users are ever exposed. The tradeoff is time and complexity: canary takes longer to reach 100% and requires you to have defined a meaningful alarm in advance — a canary with no alarm, or a badly chosen metric, buys you nothing over blue/green except delay.

THE MECHANISM, PRECISELY

In-place deployment updates instances in a fleet directly (in batches), meaning old and new code run side by side across your own fleet during the rollout — with no automatic traffic-based rollback trigger built in the way canary has; a bad deploy is caught by monitoring after the fact, and rolling back means redeploying the previous version, not flipping a switch. This is the real reason in-place is called "fastest, no rollback safety" — the rollback path exists, it's just slower and manual by default.

THE ONE-SENTENCE VERSION

In-place trades safety for speed and cost; blue/green trades cost for an instant, clean rollback with zero pre-cutover traffic validation; canary trades time for the ability to catch a bad deploy at small percentage exposure using real production traffic as the test. None of the three is "the best" — each is correct for a specific stated risk tolerance.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

Where the correct approach still has sharp edges

  • CodeDeploy's canary and linear configs are explicit percentages and time windows you choose — for example, "shift 10% of traffic, wait 5 minutes, then shift the remaining 90%" (canary), or "shift 10% every 3 minutes until 100%" (linear). These are configuration choices with real operational tradeoffs, not fixed platform behavior — a badly chosen bake time can let a slow-burning failure through anyway.
  • Blue/green needs your database layer to be compatible with both old and new code simultaneously during the cutover window — a breaking schema migration deployed at the same moment as blue/green app code can strand the "blue" environment mid-cutover. This is why the exam's blue/green scenarios rarely also involve an incompatible schema change in the same step; when they do, it's testing whether you catch the conflict.
  • Rollback on alarm requires the alarm to exist and be attached before the deployment starts — CodeDeploy can only automatically roll back a deployment against a CloudWatch alarm you've associated with that deployment group in advance. No alarm means no automatic rollback trigger, regardless of strategy.
  • In-place deployments still respect fleet health via deployment configuration (e.g., "at most half the fleet unavailable at once") — it is not literally "update everything at once with no batching," but the batching is about availability during rollout, not about validating correctness before wider exposure.
  • CodePipeline stages run sequentially by default, but actions within a stage can run in parallel — a scenario describing "run unit tests and a security scan simultaneously before deploying" is describing parallel actions within one stage, not separate sequential stages.
  • Manual approval actions pause the pipeline and are themselves a valid answer whenever a scenario requires a human sign-off gate before a specific stage (e.g., before production deployment) — don't reach for a custom Lambda approval workaround when CodePipeline has this natively.
EDGE CASE THE EXAM LOVES

A scenario describes a canary deployment where the CloudWatch alarm is scoped to the entire service (old + new versions combined) rather than specifically to the new version's traffic. The trap: that alarm might never breach even if the new version is badly broken, because the old version's healthy majority traffic dilutes the aggregate metric. The fix the exam wants you to recognize: alarms driving canary rollback must be scoped to the new version specifically wherever the platform allows it.

ARC 09 · DECISION FRAMEWORK

Use X when / use Y when — the single most exam-relevant table in this chapter

STRATEGYUSE WHENNOT WHEN
In-placeLow risk tolerance requirement, a maintenance window is acceptable, cost must stay minimal, and the workload is internal or non-critical. Zero downtime or automated rollback-on-metric is a hard requirement — in-place has neither built in by default.
Blue/greenNear-zero downtime and a fast, clean rollback are required, and the team can absorb roughly double infrastructure cost for the cutover window. Budget can't absorb a temporary doubled environment, or the team needs to validate against real production traffic before full exposure.
Canary / linearThe team wants to validate a change against real production traffic at small scale before wider exposure, with an automated rollback triggered by a defined CloudWatch alarm. The team needs the fastest possible full rollout and has no meaningful automated health signal to gate on — canary without a good alarm just adds delay for no safety benefit.
Manual approval gateA scenario requires a human sign-off before a specific stage (often before production) regardless of which deployment strategy is used underneath. The requirement is fully automated deployment with no human step — an approval action would violate that constraint outright.
CloudFormation change setsInfrastructure itself (not just application code) is changing, and you need to preview exactly what will be created/modified/replaced before committing. Only application code is deploying to already-provisioned infrastructure — that's CodeDeploy's job, not a change set's.
ARC 10 · LIMITS & SCALING NUMBERS

Blast radius over time, charted

This is the genuinely stable, derivable relationship this chapter's decision hinges on: how much of your production traffic is exposed to a bad deployment as a function of elapsed time, for each strategy. This is a design fact, not a service quota that changes — it follows directly from each strategy's mechanism.

% of traffic on new (possibly bad) code vs. timeCANVAS CHART
━ in-place (batched, no gate) ━ blue/green (instant 100% cutover) ━ canary (10% → wait → 100%) ━ linear (10% every interval)

Read the shapes, not the exact minutes: blue/ green is a vertical jump straight to 100% — there is no partial-exposure phase to catch a problem in before users see it. Canary sits at a small percentage for a deliberate bake window, then jumps. Linear climbs in even steps. In-place climbs in fleet-sized batches with no automated gate at all — it can reach high exposure before a human even notices, unless paired with vigilant manual monitoring.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three constructions in the exam's actual voice

Original scenario constructions written for this course — not real exam content, and not claimed to be.

Case 1: The Black Friday freeze that wasn't actually a freeze

An e-commerce company wants to keep shipping small fixes through its highest-traffic week without risking a repeat of last year's bad deploy that took down checkout for 20 minutes before anyone noticed. The correct answer: a canary deployment with a tight CloudWatch alarm on checkout-service error rate scoped to the new task set specifically, a small first shift (5–10%), and a meaningful bake time — not a deployment freeze (which blocks legitimate fixes too) and not blue/green alone (which would expose 100% of Black Friday traffic to an unvalidated change the instant it cuts over).

Case 2: The regulated health-records platform

A healthcare company's platform must guarantee zero downtime during business hours for compliance reasons, and any deployment must be instantly reversible with no risk of serving a mix of old/new API contract versions to the same client session. The correct answer: blue/green — the complete, isolated environment swap guarantees no mixed-version exposure within one client's session the way a gradual canary or in-place rollout could, and rollback is an instant traffic flip back to the untouched "blue" environment.

Case 3: The internal analytics dashboard

An internal-only analytics tool used by a 40-person team has a 10pm–6am low-usage window, tolerates brief interruptions, and the team wants to minimize both cost and pipeline complexity. The correct answer: in-place deployment during the low-usage window — there is no meaningful risk-tolerance requirement in the scenario that blue/green's doubled cost or canary's added complexity would actually be buying protection against.

ARC 13 · CLI / CODE REFERENCE

Illustrative CLI & config syntax

Realistic, illustrative AWS CLI v2 and pipeline configuration syntax — the shapes you should recognize on sight, not copy verbatim into production without review.

deploy-pipeline/
├── buildspec.yml — CodeBuild: install, build image, push to ECR
├── appspec.yaml — CodeDeploy: ECS blue/green task-set spec
├── deploy-create.sh — aws deploy create-deployment CLI invocation
└── rollback-alarm.json — CloudWatch alarm driving automatic rollback
YAMLbuildspec.yml — illustrative example syntax
version: 0.2
phases:
  install:
    runtime-versions: { docker: 20 }
  build:
    commands:
      - echo Building image...
      - docker build -t $ECR_REPO_URI:$CODEBUILD_RESOLVED_SOURCE_VERSION .
      - docker push $ECR_REPO_URI:$CODEBUILD_RESOLVED_SOURCE_VERSION
  post_build:
    commands:
      - # write the new image URI into the task definition for CodeDeploy to pick up
      - printf '{"ImageURI":"%s"}' $ECR_REPO_URI:$CODEBUILD_RESOLVED_SOURCE_VERSION > imageDetail.json
artifacts:
  files: [imageDetail.json, appspec.yaml, taskdef.json]
YAMLappspec.yaml — ECS blue/green (illustrative)
version: 0.0
Resources:
  - TargetService:
      Type: AWS::ECS::Service
      Properties:
        TaskDefinition: <TASK_DEFINITION>
        LoadBalancerInfo:
          ContainerName: web
          ContainerPort: 8080
# CodeDeploy substitutes TASK_DEFINITION at deploy time and manages the
# blue (current) and green (new) target groups behind the load balancer
bashdeploy-create.sh — canary deployment via CLI (illustrative)
# deployment config name here maps to a canary shift you defined,
# e.g. "CodeDeployDefault.ECSCanary10Percent5Minutes"
aws deploy create-deployment \
  --application-name webapp \
  --deployment-group-name webapp-prod \
  --deployment-config-name CodeDeployDefault.ECSCanary10Percent5Minutes \
  --revision revisionType=AppSpecContent,appSpecContent="{content=\$(cat appspec.yaml)}"
WHY THE EXAM CARES ABOUT THIS SYNTAX

You will not be asked to write these files from memory, but you will be shown a deployment configuration name like CodeDeployDefault.ECSCanary10Percent5Minutes or CodeDeployDefault.ECSLinear10PercentEvery3Minutes and asked to identify the strategy and its exposure profile from the name alone — that naming convention is the exam-relevant detail, not the surrounding YAML syntax.

GATE · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

Before chapter 21: what to actually remember

If you remember nothing else from this chapter: (1) in-place, blue/green, and canary trade cost, validation depth, and blast radius against each other — none is universally "the best"; (2) blue/green's rollback is instant but its cutover exposes 100% of traffic at once with zero real-traffic validation beforehand; (3) canary's safety comes from the CloudWatch alarm you wire to it, not from the gradual shift by itself — no alarm means no automatic protection; (4) CodePipeline orchestrates, CodeBuild builds, CodeDeploy executes the strategy — know which service owns which step; and (5) a manual approval action is the correct answer whenever a scenario demands a human sign-off, regardless of strategy underneath.
