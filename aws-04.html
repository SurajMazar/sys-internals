ARC 01 · EXAM CONTEXT

Where this chapter lands on the blueprint

Compute choice is one of the most densely tested topics on both exams because almost every architecture question needs a compute layer before it needs anything else. On the SAA-C03 (65 questions, 50 scored + 15 unscored, 130 minutes, pass score 720/1000, exam fee $150 at the time of writing), this chapter maps directly to two domains: Design High-Performing Architectures (24%) — picking a compute shape that actually fits the workload's performance profile — and Design Cost-Optimized Architectures (20%) — picking how you pay for it. ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"Just pick the biggest instance, or the newest service"

Three wrong instincts show up over and over in practice-exam post-mortems, and they all feel reasonable in the moment:

  • "Bigger instance = safer answer." Faced with a performance-sounding scenario, pick the largest or most expensive instance type offered in the options, reasoning that more vCPUs and memory can't hurt.
  • "Serverless is the modern answer, so default to Lambda." Any scenario that mentions "reduce operational overhead" or "no servers to manage" gets an automatic Lambda answer, regardless of what the workload actually does once it's running.
  • "Reserved Instances are the responsible, safe default" for any workload that "runs in production," because commitment sounds like discipline and Spot sounds risky.

All three instincts substitute a vague heuristic — bigger, newer, safer-sounding — for reading the workload's actual shape. The exam is built specifically to punish this: it always gives you the concrete facts (how long the job runs, whether it holds state, whether it can be interrupted, whether it needs root access) precisely so you can compute the right answer instead of guessing by vibe.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

The ceilings and locks you actually hit

Each wrong instinct from §2 fails against a specific, quantifiable mechanism — not a vague "best practice." Learn the mechanism and the trap stops working on you.

  • "Bigger instance" ignores instance family, not just size. A compute-bound batch job on a memory-optimized R-family instance pays for RAM it never touches; a memory-bound in-memory cache on a compute-optimized C-family instance starves on RAM it desperately needs. Scaling the wrong family up just buys a bigger version of the wrong shape — the fix is picking the right letter, not a bigger number after it.
  • "Default to Lambda" collides with a hard wall: 900 seconds. AWS Lambda's maximum function timeout is 900 seconds (15 minutes), full stop — there is no configuration that raises it. A scenario describing a 45-minute video-encoding job or a nightly batch reconciliation that runs for two hours cannot be Lambda, regardless of how "serverless-friendly" the rest of the description sounds. Statefulness is a second, independent wall: Lambda's execution environment is ephemeral and reused opportunistically between invocations, so a workload that needs durable local state across calls is fighting the platform, not using it.
  • "RIs are always safe" ignores commitment risk. A Reserved Instance or Savings Plan is a 1- or 3-year spend or capacity commitment in exchange for a discount deeper than On-Demand. That's a great trade for a workload whose size you're confident about — and a bad one for a workload that's still finding its steady state, because you're now paying for capacity you committed to whether or not you use it. Spot, meanwhile, is typically dramatically cheaper than On-Demand — but only because AWS can reclaim that capacity with a real, short two-minute interruption notice. Put a workload that can't tolerate being paused there, and the "safe-sounding" instinct that avoided Spot for everything just quietly overpaid on the workloads that could have tolerated it fine.
THE NUMBER THAT DECIDES MOST OF THIS CHAPTER'S QUESTIONS

If a scenario states or implies a runtime over 15 minutes, Lambda is eliminated before you read another word — no exception, no configuration flag, no "provisioned concurrency fixes it." This single fact resolves a surprising fraction of EC2-vs-Lambda questions on both exams the moment you notice it.

THEN, ONLY THEN: PICK HOW YOU PAY

Purchasing option is a second, independent decision layered on top of the service decision, not part of it. Once you know you need EC2 (or an EC2-backed Batch compute environment), ask: can this workload absorb a 2-minute interruption? If yes, Spot is usually the right default for cost. If no, and the size is predictable, Reserved Instances or a Savings Plan buy a deeper discount than On-Demand. If neither — new, unpredictable, or genuinely can't commit — On-Demand is correct, and that's fine; flexibility is what you're paying its premium for.

THE LETTER IS A RATIO, NOT A RANK

C, M, and R instance families in the same generation often use nearly identical underlying processors — what differs is the vCPU-to-memory ratio AWS built the box with. A "bigger" C-family instance still gives you a compute-heavy ratio, just more of it. If a scenario's bottleneck is memory, no amount of scaling up within the C family fixes it — you need to change the letter, not just the number after it.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

The specific numbers the exam expects you to know cold

  • Lambda's maximum timeout is 900 seconds (15 minutes) — a hard platform ceiling with no override. Any scenario describing a longer single execution has already eliminated Lambda before any other constraint is even considered.
  • A Spot Instance interruption comes with a real two-minute notice before AWS reclaims the capacity (via the instance's metadata and, if configured, an EventBridge notification) — enough time to checkpoint progress or drain a connection, never enough time to do anything AWS didn't warn you to prepare for in advance. Spot is therefore never the right answer for a workload that cannot tolerate a sudden, short-notice stop.
  • Cluster placement groups are confined to a single Availability Zone by design — that's what buys the low-latency, high-throughput network adjacency they exist for. A scenario that also asks for multi-AZ resilience cannot use one placement group to satisfy both goals at once; that's two separate requirements answered by two separate mechanisms.
  • Spread placement groups cap out at a small number of running instances per Availability Zone per group (commonly documented as seven) because the whole point is spreading each instance onto genuinely distinct underlying hardware — past that count within one AZ, spreading further would mean adding more AZs to the group, not more instances to the same AZ.
  • Partition placement groups divide instances into a bounded number of logical partitions per Availability Zone (commonly documented as up to seven), each on distinct underlying hardware — a shape built for workloads that already replicate data across independent partitions themselves and want the physical placement to mirror that logical structure. Treat the exact partition ceiling as "check current service quotas," not a magic number to bet a question on.
  • On-Demand capacity is still bounded by account-level vCPU quotas per instance-family group — a scenario describing a sudden large fleet that fails to launch with a limit-style error is describing a quota, not a service outage; the fix is a quota increase request, not a different service.
  • Reserved Instances and Savings Plans are commitments, not switches — standard RIs are tied to a specific instance family and Region for their term, which is exactly the flexibility they trade away for their discount; convertible RIs and Savings Plans buy back some of that flexibility at a shallower (but still real) discount than a standard RI.
THE TRAP HIDING IN "LAUNCH A CLUSTER PLACEMENT GROUP LATER"

Cluster placement groups work best when every instance in the group is launched in one request, at capacity, up front — adding instances to an already-running cluster group later can fail with a capacity error even when plenty of On-Demand capacity exists elsewhere in the AZ, because the group is chasing tightly adjacent hardware that may simply not be free next to what's already running. The exam's fix is always the same: launch the whole group together, or accept that a partially-filled cluster group may need to be relaunched.

WHY THE BASELINE IS ON-DEMAND, NOT SPOT

The On-Demand slice guarantees a floor of capacity that survives a Spot interruption wave untouched — if every Spot pool for the chosen instance types is reclaimed simultaneously (rare, but the exam likes to test the "worst case"), the On-Demand base keeps serving traffic while the Auto Scaling group requests replacement Spot capacity, possibly in different instance types or AZs. This is the practical answer to "how do you get Spot's savings without betting the whole service on it."

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

  
Three vignettes in the exam's own style

  
Original, clearly-not-real-exam scenarios, written the way the real exam actually phrases them: a company
  profile, a constraint, and a "which approach" question. Think before scrolling to the reasoning.


  
Case 1 — Meridian Labs, overnight video transcoding

  
Meridian Labs ingests raw video uploads throughout the day and needs to transcode them into several output
  formats. Jobs take 20 minutes to 4 hours depending on file size, can start any time overnight, and can be safely
  restarted from the last checkpoint if interrupted. Cost is the dominant concern; a finance stakeholder has asked
  specifically about Spot.

  
Reasoning: duration rules out Lambda outright (up to 4 hours, versus a 900-second ceiling). The jobs are
  queue-driven with flexible start time and explicitly checkpoint-restartable — a textbook fit for AWS Batch
  with an EC2-backed compute environment leaning on Spot for the deep discount the finance stakeholder is asking
  about, since the interruption tolerance is explicitly stated.


  
Case 2 — Fenwick Retail, flash-sale checkout API

  
Fenwick Retail runs a checkout API that handles ordinary traffic most of the year but spikes 40× during two
  scheduled flash sales. Each request completes in well under a second, holds no state between requests, and the
  team wants to avoid managing a fleet sized for a peak that only happens twice a year.

  
Reasoning: short, stateless, HTTP-shaped, bursty-by-nature-of-the-business — every signal points at
  AWS Lambda behind API Gateway. The 900-second ceiling is irrelevant here (requests are sub-second), and
  paying per-invocation rather than provisioning a peak-sized fleet that sits mostly idle directly answers the
  team's stated cost concern.


  
Case 3 — Harborview Analytics, licensed simulation software

  
Harborview Analytics runs a commercial simulation package that requires a specific kernel module and a
  license server reachable only from machines with a fixed, dedicated host identity. The workload runs continuously,
  24/7, at a steady, well-understood size that hasn't changed in a year.

  
Reasoning: the kernel-module and host-identity requirements need OS-level control, which eliminates both
  Lambda and Fargate immediately — this has to be EC2 (or ECS on EC2 if the software were containerized,
  which a required kernel module usually argues against). The workload is steady and its size is well-understood,
  so once the service question is settled, Reserved Instances or a Savings Plan are the right purchasing
  answer — the commitment risk that made them wrong in §3's example doesn't apply to a genuinely stable workload.

bashcreate-cluster-pg.sh — single-AZ, tightly-coupled placement

    
# Create the group, then launch every instance in ONE request —
# adding instances to a running cluster group later can fail on capacity.
aws ec2 create-placement-group \
  --group-name hpc-cluster-01 \
  --strategy cluster

aws ec2 run-instances \
  --image-id ami-0abcdef1234567890 \
  --instance-type c6i.4xlarge \
  --count 8 \
  --placement GroupName=hpc-cluster-01

  


  

    
bashrequest-spot-instance.sh — interruption-tolerant one-off

    
# --instance-market-options marks this a Spot request; on interruption
# the instance gets a real 2-minute notice before capacity is reclaimed.
aws ec2 run-instances \
  --image-id ami-0abcdef1234567890 \
  --instance-type m6i.large \
  --count 10 \
  --instance-market-options '{"MarketType":"spot","SpotOptions":{"SpotInstanceType":"one-time"}}'

  


  

    
bashmixed-instances-asg.sh — On-Demand base + Spot pool

    
# base_capacity On-Demand units survive a Spot-wide interruption wave;
# capacity-optimized picks Spot pools statistically least likely to be reclaimed soon.
aws autoscaling create-auto-scaling-group \
  --auto-scaling-group-name blended-fleet \
  --min-size 6 --max-size 40 --desired-capacity 20 \
  --vpc-zone-identifier "subnet-aaa,subnet-bbb,subnet-ccc" \
  --mixed-instances-policy file://mixed-instances-policy.json

  


  

    
bashsubmit-transcode-job.sh — queue-driven, flexible timing

    
# AWS Batch schedules this against a compute environment (EC2 or Fargate);
# an EC2-backed environment configured for Spot can absorb interruptions and re-queue.
aws batch submit-job \
  --job-name transcode-2026-08-18 \
  --job-queue overnight-transcode-queue \
  --job-definition video-transcode:4

  


  

    
WHY THE EXAM CARES ABOUT THIS SYNTAX SHAPE

    
You are never asked to write these commands from memory. You are asked to recognize, from a CLI
    fragment or a described configuration, which purchasing option or compute service it implements —
    --instance-market-options means Spot, --mixed-instances-policy means a blended fleet,
    a Batch job queue means flexible-timing scheduling. Pattern-matching the shape of the config to the concept
    behind it is the actual tested skill.

    GATE · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS
    
  
    Before chapter 5: the five things to have automatic
    
  
    If you remember nothing else from this chapter walking into the exam: (1) Lambda's maximum timeout is
  900 seconds (15 minutes) — a hard ceiling, no exceptions, and the fastest single elimination in any compute
  question. (2) Spot Instances carry a real two-minute interruption notice — perfect for interruption-tolerant,
  checkpoint-able, or fault-tolerant workloads, and wrong for anything that isn't. (3) Instance family letters
  (C/M/R/I/D/T/G) signal a resource ratio, not a size tier — match the letter to the bottleneck before you touch the
  size. (4) Cluster placement groups buy low-latency adjacency at the cost of being confined to one
  Availability Zone; spread and partition groups trade that adjacency for fault isolation instead. (5) The
  EC2-vs-Lambda-vs-Fargate-vs-Batch decision is answerable from exactly three facts a scenario always gives you:
  duration, statefulness, and how much control over the host it needs.
    
  
    Chapter 5 moves one layer down: once you've picked EC2 (or an EC2-backed compute environment), what storage do
  you attach to it? Think about these before you turn the page.
