BITCOIN INTERNALS · CHAPTER 14

Lightning: Payment
Channels & Routing

Chapter 6 gave every full node the same UTXO set; chapter 7 gave every block a hard weight limit. Multiply those together and you get a ceiling: the base layer can confirm a few transactions per second, globally, forever. This chapter derives — not describes — the construction that lets two people transact an unbounded number of times while touching the chain twice: a bilateral ledger enforced by the threat of losing everything, chained across strangers by a cryptographic trick that makes a multi-hop payment atomic without anyone trusting anyone.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

A settlement layer cannot also be a payment network

Take the number from chapter 7 seriously: a 4,000,000 weight-unit block every ~10 minutes, with a typical SegWit transaction costing roughly 140–250 weight units, caps the base chain at on the order of 7 transactions per second, averaged — the figure the Blockchain Fundamentals course's scaling chapter (chapter 12 there) derives from the same block-weight arithmetic applied to any UTXO chain with a fixed block budget. That is not a bug to be patched; it is the direct, arithmetic consequence of the design chapter 6 committed to: every full node validates every transaction, so the network's throughput is bounded by what the median validating node can absorb, not by what a datacenter could process. Raise the limit and you raise the hardware bar to run a node — chapter 12's trilemma again — you do not escape the tradeoff, you relocate it.

Compare that ceiling to what "being a payment network" actually requires. Visa's public figures describe sustained processing in the low thousands of transactions per second with burst capacity far higher; a single large payment processor's Tuesday afternoon exceeds Bitcoin's entire weekly capacity at 7 tx/s. The gap is not 10%, it is several orders of magnitude, and no plausible block-size increase closes it without concentrating validation in a handful of well-provisioned datacenters — which is a different, centralized system wearing Bitcoin's name.

So the requirement has to change shape. You cannot make the settlement layer faster without abandoning the property (independent validation by ordinary hardware) that makes it worth using. What you can do is reduce how often two parties need to touch it. Formally: find a construction where the chain records O(1) transactions per relationship — one to open, one to close — no matter how many times the parties pay each other in between, while every intermediate state remains as enforceable as if it had been written to the chain directly. That is the exact engineering target this chapter derives a solution for, and "as enforceable as if written to the chain" is doing all the work: it is the difference between an IOU and a payment.

THE SHAPE OF THE ANSWER, STATED EARLY

Every off-chain scaling construction in Bitcoin — payment channels, channel factories, statechains — follows the same pattern: put funds under a control condition on-chain once, then let the controlling parties agree to update who could claim those funds if the agreement were enforced right now, without touching the chain per update. The entire engineering difficulty is making "if it were enforced right now" airtight against a party who later prefers an older agreement. Sections 3–6 derive exactly why that is hard and what it costs to fix.

ARC 02 · HISTORICAL CONTEXT

From a Satoshi mailing-list aside to a routed network

DEEP CONTEXT · WHY MALLEABILITY WAS THE BLOCKER, NOT THE MOTIVATION

Chapter 5 derived transaction malleability: before BIP143, a third party (or a careless wallet) could alter a transaction's witness data and change its txid without invalidating its signature. A channel construction that pre-signs transactions spending a not-yet-confirmed funding output is fatally exposed to this — if the funding transaction's txid changes after you've built refund/commitment transactions against it, those transactions now reference a nonexistent outpoint. This is the real reason Lightning stayed a research idea for two years after Satoshi's original sketch: the cryptography of channels was tractable in 2013, but the base layer's own transaction format had a bug that made building on it unsafe. SegWit (chapter 12) fixed the base layer; only then could the channel layer ship.

ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: a one-way refund channel via nSequence (2013)

Derive it the way Satoshi's 2013 bitcointalk post and Mike Hearn's follow-up code did. Two parties, Alice (payer) and Bob (payee), want Alice to pay Bob in small increments without a transaction per increment. Start from primitives you already have from chapters 3 and 4: a 2-of-2 multisig output and the transaction's nSequence field, which pre-SegWit was documented (if unreliably implemented) as a hint that a later version of a transaction spending the same input could replace an earlier, not-yet-confirmed one in miners' mempools.

pseudocodespilman_channel.py — the 2013 construction, annotated
# 1. Alice funds a 2-of-2 multisig with 1.0 BTC. She does NOT
#    broadcast this until step 2 exists, to avoid being stuck.
funding_tx = multisig_output(alice_pk, bob_pk, amount=1.0)

# 2. Before broadcasting funding_tx, Alice pre-signs a REFUND that
#    returns everything to her, timelocked far in the future (T).
#    This bounds her downside if Bob disappears: worst case, she
#    waits until T and reclaims all 1.0 BTC.
refund_tx = spend(funding_tx, to=alice_pk, amount=1.0, locktime=T)

# 3. Now Alice broadcasts funding_tx. The channel is open.

# 4. Each time Alice wants to pay Bob some amount, she signs a NEW
#    commitment paying Bob more and refunding herself less, sets a
#    high nSequence, and hands the fully-signed tx to Bob directly
#    -- off the network, off-chain, no fee paid yet.
commit_1 = spend(funding_tx, outs=[(bob_pk, 0.1), (alice_pk, 0.9)], nSequence=HIGH)
commit_2 = spend(funding_tx, outs=[(bob_pk, 0.3), (alice_pk, 0.7)], nSequence=HIGH+1)
# ... commit_N, whenever Alice wants, no on-chain footprint yet ...

# 5. Bob holds only the LATEST commitment he's been given. When he
#    wants to be paid, or T approaches, he broadcasts it.

This is a genuine payment channel: after the single on-chain funding transaction, Alice and Bob can exchange an arbitrary number of increasingly generous payments with zero additional on-chain transactions, and Bob is protected because each new commitment he receives strictly increases his balance. It already demonstrates the core mechanism this whole chapter builds on: a signed-but-unbroadcast transaction is a valid, enforceable claim the moment its predecessor confirms — broadcasting it is optional and can be deferred indefinitely.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

One direction, one relationship, and a replacement rule nobody enforces

Three failures, in increasing order of fundamental-ness — the first two are engineering defects, the third is structural and survives even a perfect implementation:

  • nSequence replacement was never a consensus guarantee. "Higher sequence number replaces lower" was a mempool policy convention in reference software, not a rule enforced by proof of work. A miner under no obligation to run that policy — or one who simply already has an earlier commitment in a block template — can confirm commit_1 instead of commit_9, and once any valid spend of the funding output confirms, the UTXO is gone; every other pre-signed commitment is now spending an already-spent input and is permanently invalid. Bob has no recourse: the chain doesn't know he was owed more, because he was never given anything that says so except a transaction Alice can suppress by getting an earlier one mined first, which she is financially motivated to do near the end of the relationship.
  • It is strictly one-directional. Every commitment must pay Bob more than the last (monotonic by construction, or Bob would simply broadcast the more generous ancestor). There is no way to pay Alice back without a second, independently funded channel running in the opposite direction — doubling the capital two parties must lock up to transact bidirectionally, which for anything resembling a real commercial relationship (a merchant refunding a customer, a market maker) is disqualifying.
  • The channel has a hard expiry, T, baked in at funding time. Reaching T forces an on-chain close and reopen to continue transacting — for a long-lived relationship this reintroduces exactly the per-relationship on-chain cost the construction was built to amortize away, just at a lower frequency.
QUANTIFY IT

Let p be the probability that some miner within the confirmation window includes an old, superseded commitment instead of the newest one Bob holds — driven by mempool propagation gaps, miners running non-default policy, or Alice paying an out-of-band side fee to a friendly pool to jump the queue. Bob's expected loss per channel lifetime is p × (final_balance − Bob's_confirmed_balance_if_cheated), and critically p is not under Bob's control — he cannot inspect miners' mempool policies, only hope. A construction whose security depends on unenforced, unobservable mempool convention is not a security property; chapter 11 will formalize this same failure mode as pinning, and you'll see the identical shape reappear in §11 of this chapter against the fixed version.

The fix for direction and expiry is comparatively easy — sign commitments both ways and let either party extend the timeout. The fix for the replacement problem is where the real engineering is, and it requires rethinking what a "commitment" is, not just how it's replaced.

ARC 05 · BETTER SOLUTION, EXAMINED

Attempt #2: symmetric, cooperatively-replaced commitments — and the cheat it invites

Drop reliance on mempool policy entirely. Instead of many pre-signed transactions racing for a single spend of the funding output, keep exactly one live commitment transaction per party at any moment, and make updating the balance a cooperative act: to move from state N to state N+1, both parties sign a fresh commitment transaction reflecting the new split, and both agree — verbally, by protocol convention — to discard transaction N. Nothing about consensus enforces "discard"; it is a promise. Step through what that promise is worth.

Symmetric commitments: the promise and the exploitSTEP-BY-STEP

Notice what changed for the better: both directions are now representable (either party can be the one whose balance increases in the next state), there's no expiry forcing periodic on-chain churn, and there is no ambiguous mempool race — any of the fully-signed commitment transactions is unconditionally valid the instant it's broadcast, by construction (both signatures are already on it). That last property is also exactly the new problem: validity no longer depends on being first past the post, it depends on nothing at all except having a copy. Every old commitment stays a live, spendable, fully-authorized transaction forever.

THE INCENTIVE, MADE CONCRETE

Suppose Alice and Bob open a channel at 50/50, transact for months, and now sit at 10/90 in Bob's favor — 40 coins have net flowed to Bob. Alice still possesses a validly, mutually signed copy of the very first commitment transaction, splitting the funds 50/50. Nothing on-chain distinguishes "the first state" from "the current state" — both are equally well-formed, equally signed spends of the same funding outpoint. If Alice broadcasts the old one and Bob isn't watching the chain at that exact moment, Alice recovers 40 coins she no longer has any economic claim to, at the cost of one transaction fee. The cooperative-discard promise has exactly the enforcement strength of an honor system between two people who opened this channel because they didn't want to have to trust each other.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL FAILS

The requirement this forces: make the old state worse than broadcasting nothing

You cannot prevent Alice from holding the old commitment — it is a fully signed, valid Bitcoin transaction and nothing in cryptography lets you un-sign it. You also cannot prevent her from broadcasting it — nodes will relay and mine any valid transaction. The only lever left is economic: make it so that if the old state ever reaches the chain, the counterparty can respond within a window and take the entire channel balance, not just their fair share. If broadcasting a revoked state has strictly negative expected value — you might gain your old balance back for an instant, but you will almost certainly lose everything, including funds that were legitimately yours — no rational party ever does it. This is the same move chapter 8 studied in mining: raise the cost of the attack above any plausible gain, rather than trying to make the attack impossible.

Deriving the mechanism from that requirement, three things must be simultaneously true of every commitment transaction:

  • Asymmetry. The two parties cannot hold identical copies of "the current state," because a penalty needs a party to react to a broadcast — if Alice's and Bob's copies were byte-identical, Bob's copy is exactly what got broadcast and there is no separate "Bob's honest output" to protect. Each party must instead hold a different transaction that pays out to the same two people but delays the broadcaster's own payout, giving the other side a reaction window.
  • A revocable delay on your own output. Whoever broadcasts a commitment must wait — via a relative timelock, chapter 8's OP_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY, typically hundreds of blocks — before spending their own balance out of it. That delay is the reaction window.
  • A trapdoor that opens only for old states. When the parties move to a new state, they must exchange something — a secret — that specifically unlocks an alternate spending path on the state they just agreed to abandon, and only that state. Whoever receives the secret for a given old state can, if that state is ever broadcast, claim the entire balance immediately, bypassing the delay entirely.
THE COST OF THIS FIX, HONESTLY STATED

This is not free. Every commitment now needs a delayed output with a penalty branch (more script, more on-chain bytes if it's ever force-closed); every party must retain every revocation secret it's ever received, for the life of the channel, or it cannot punish a cheat on an old state — a channel that has updated 100,000 times naively needs 100,000 stored 32-byte secrets; and — the sharpest operational cost — you must be online and watching the chain to see a cheat and react within the delay window, or the penalty right expires unused and the broadcaster keeps their unearned balance. Sections 8 and 11 derive the exact data structure that fixes the storage cost (a shachain, O(log n) not O(n)) and the exact service (a watchtower) that fixes the online-availability cost. Neither cost disappears; each is engineered down to something tolerable.

WHAT'S DELIBERATELY NOT ON THIS DIAGRAM

There is no global channel-balance registry, no central router, and no on-chain record of who paid whom through a channel — only that a channel with some total capacity was opened and, eventually, closed with some final split. Every payment in between is known only to the parties directly involved in each hop. This is a privacy property paid for with a UX cost you will feel directly in §10 and §15: nobody, including the paying node, can be certain a route works until they try it, because the one thing the network deliberately does not gossip is each channel's directional available liquidity.

ARC 08 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

The bytes, the keys, and the state machine, in order

Four pieces, built in the order a real channel needs them: (a) the commitment transaction's exact output layout, (b) the revocation key/secret machinery — including the storage trick that makes it practical, (c) HTLCs as the primitive that makes a multi-hop payment atomic, (d) the onion packet that lets a route exist without any hop seeing it.

(a) The commitment transaction: two outputs become up to 2 + 2·N_htlc

Every commitment transaction spends the single funding outpoint and pays out according to BOLT #3. In its simplest form (no in-flight HTLCs) it has exactly two outputs, and they are not symmetric between the two versions Alice and Bob each hold:

  • to_local — pays the owner of this copy of the commitment, but only after a relative delay to_self_delay (typically hundreds of blocks, negotiated at open) enforced by OP_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY — unless the spend instead reveals the revocation key for this exact commitment, in which case the counterparty can take it immediately, no delay.
  • to_remote — pays the counterparty directly, immediately spendable (a plain P2WPKH output, one confirmation and it's theirs) — since it is not this party's own delayed output, there is nothing to punish there.

Because to_local in Alice's copy pays Alice-with-a-delay while to_remote in that same copy pays Bob-immediately, and Bob's copy is the mirror image, the two parties are holding different transactions that nonetheless encode the identical balance split. That asymmetry is precisely the requirement derived in §6: only the broadcaster's own funds are ever delayed, which is what gives the other party a window to react.

THE OBSCURED COMMITMENT NUMBER — A REAL BOLT #3 DETAIL WORTH KNOWING

A channel's commitment transactions must all spend the same funding outpoint, so they'd normally be trivially linkable and orderable by anyone who saw two of them. BOLT #3 hides the sequence number: the 48-bit commitment index is XORed with a value derived from both parties' payment_basepoints (SHA256(open_payment_basepoint || accept_payment_basepoint), lower 48 bits), and the result is split across the transaction's nLockTime (lower 24 bits, with the upper byte forced to 0x20 so parsers don't mistake it for a real locktime) and the input's nSequence (lower 24 bits, with the top bits forced to mark it non-final). An outside observer sees two numbers that look like protocol noise; only the two channel parties can recover which commitment number produced them — a small, characteristic Lightning move: hide metadata inside fields the base layer already validates for something else, rather than adding new fields that would need a soft fork.

(b) Revocation: one basepoint, one per-commitment secret, and a tree that stores neither in full

Deriving a fresh throwaway key pair for every single commitment and remembering each one individually is the §6 cost made concrete. BOLT #3 avoids it with two linked constructions.

First, the revocation key itself is not a random per-state secret — it's a combination. Each party publishes a long-term revocation_basepoint once, at channel open. For commitment number i, the actual key that can sweep a revoked to_local output is derived by tweaking that basepoint with the counterparty's per-commitment secret for state i — elliptic-curve point/scalar arithmetic from chapter 4, applied so that knowing the basepoint alone reveals nothing, but basepoint + the one secret for state i reveals exactly the key needed to punish state i, and nothing about any other state.

Second — the storage trick. Rather than remember every per-commitment secret handed over, BOLT #3 Appendix D specifies shachain: secrets are generated from one 32-byte seed by a deterministic bit-flip procedure, so a party who once held the seed can regenerate any past secret on demand, and a party who receives secrets (and must give up the seed-holder's ability to regenerate, since revealing a secret must not reveal the seed) needs to retain at most 47 secrets to derive all 2⁴⁸ possible ones below any given index. (c) HTLCs: the primitive that makes a stranger-to-stranger payment atomic

  
Revocable commitments solve cheating within one channel. They say nothing about paying someone you have
  no channel with at all — which, in a network where opening a direct channel with every counterparty is exactly
  the on-chain cost explosion §1 ruled out, is the common case. You need to route through intermediaries you do not
  trust, without ever letting an intermediary either steal the funds or take a fee without actually forwarding.

  
Derive the mechanism from the requirement. Carol (the final recipient) generates a random 32-byte secret
  R (the preimage) and gives Alice (the sender) only its hash H = SHA-256(R), packaged as a
  BOLT11 invoice. Alice cannot pay Carol directly — no channel — but she has a channel with Bob, who has a channel
  with Carol. Alice offers Bob a conditional payment: "this output is yours if you reveal a preimage of H within
  144 blocks; otherwise it reverts to me." This is a Hash Time-Locked Contract — a special HTLC output on
  the commitment transaction, not a separate transaction. Bob cannot claim it without knowing R, and only Carol
  knows R. So Bob extends the identical offer to Carol — same hash H, but a shorter timeout, say 100 blocks.
  Carol, who has R, claims Bob's HTLC immediately by revealing R on their shared commitment update. Bob now holds
  R. He turns around and uses that same R to claim Alice's HTLC before her 144-block timeout. Money moved
  Alice → Bob → Carol, Bob never had to be trusted with anything except forwarding correctly, and if he tries to
  claim from Alice without paying Carol, he cannot — he doesn't have R until Carol reveals it, and Carol has no
  reason to reveal it without being paid.

WHY THE TIMEOUT MUST STRICTLY DECREASE TOWARD THE DESTINATION

    
Bob only turns his knowledge of R into a claim against Alice after Carol reveals it to him. If Bob's
    outgoing timeout to Carol expired at the same block height as his incoming timeout from Alice, a slow or
    congested mempool could let Alice's HTLC time out and revert to her before Bob finishes claiming from
    Carol and turning around — leaving Bob having paid Carol out of his own pocket with no way to recover it from
    Alice. Each hop therefore subtracts a safety margin, the cltv_expiry_delta, from the timeout it
    received before offering it onward — commonly tens of blocks per hop in real deployments (implementation
    defaults vary; on the order of 40 blocks is typical, with the final recipient's own minimum, BOLT11's
    min_final_cltv_expiry_delta, commonly around 18). The direct cost: a route's total worst-case
    lock time is the sum of every hop's delta, so a 6-hop route can tie up Alice's outbound HTLC for several times
    longer than a 1-hop route, even though the payment itself settles in milliseconds — capital that is committed,
    not spent, for the entire timeout window if something upstream stalls.

  


  

    
THE PRIVACY LEAK HTLCs CANNOT FIX — AND WHAT PTLCs DO ABOUT IT

    
Every hop's HTLC uses the identical hash H — it has to, or Bob couldn't prove to Carol he's forwarding the
    same payment Alice is funding. That means any two nodes who both happen to be on the route (or who collude, or
    who simply watch enough of the network) can correlate "an HTLC with hash H passed through me and also through
    you" and infer they're part of the same payment, even without knowing the sender or recipient. Chapter 13's
    Taproot machinery offers a fix: replace the hash-lock with a point-time-locked contract (PTLC), where
    each hop's condition is "produce a valid Schnorr signature under this specific curve point," built with an
    adaptor signature. The point Carol reveals to complete the payment can be randomized independently at
    every hop via the same key-blinding math MuSig2 uses for aggregation, so no single correlating value crosses
    the whole route. PTLCs also compress naturally — an adaptor-signature spend looks identical to a normal
    single-sig spend on-chain, unlike an HTLC's distinctive hash-and-timelock script. As of this writing PTLC
    support is experimental in the major implementations, not yet a deployed default — an honest case of "the fix
    is designed and mostly prototyped, not yet how payments actually route."

  


  
(d) Sphinx onion routing: a fixed 1,300-byte envelope, no matter how many hops

  
Even with PTLCs fixing the hash-correlation leak, a route is still useless privacy-wise if each hop can see
  the entire path or count how many hops remain. BOLT #4 adapts the Sphinx packet format (Danezis &
  Goldberg, 2009) so that every intermediary sees only: the immediate next hop, the amount and timeout to forward,
  and cryptographic material it cannot use to learn anything else. The construction Alice builds is layered
  encryption — she encrypts Carol's instructions, then Bob's instructions wrapping that, using a fresh shared
  secret with each hop derived via Diffie–Hellman from a single ephemeral key she generates once. Each hop peels
  exactly one layer and cannot see what's inside the next.

  
The field that makes this actually hide the route length is fixed-size padding. Drag the hop count below — the
  total onion payload stays exactly 1,300 bytes regardless, because unused hop slots are filled with pseudorandom
  padding indistinguishable from real, still-encrypted hop data:

WHAT EACH HOP ACTUALLY LEARNS — AND CANNOT LEARN

    
Bob, forwarding for Alice, learns: the previous hop (Alice, since she's his direct peer), the next hop
    (Carol), the amount to forward, and the CLTV to set. He cannot learn: how many hops came before Alice, how many
    come after Carol, or the total route length — the padding is bitwise indistinguishable from a real, still-onion-
    wrapped next layer. A per-hop HMAC lets each node detect tampering (a modified packet fails the check and the
    payment fails cleanly) without revealing what was tampered with. This is why a failed payment in Lightning
    returns an encrypted error, onion-wrapped back through the same path Alice built — only Alice, holding
    the original shared secrets, can decrypt which hop reported the failure and why (§10 returns to this).

ARC 10 · PERFORMANCE & SECURITY CHARACTERISTICS

  
What you can predict before running a node

  
Cost and complexity, derived rather than benchmarked:

  
    
    
  • Per-hop cryptographic cost: one ECDH + two ChaCha20 stream operations (encrypt payload, generate
      padding) per onion layer — negligible versus network latency, which is why Lightning payments settle in
      low hundreds of milliseconds across several hops rather than the block-interval timescale of on-chain
      confirmation.
    • 
    
  • Routing search cost: a sender runs shortest-path search (Dijkstra or a variant) over the locally
      cached channel graph — tens of thousands of channels network-wide as of the mid-2020s, so a full-graph
      Dijkstra run is milliseconds on ordinary hardware. The expensive part is not computing a route; it's that
      the computed route might not actually have the liquidity to carry the payment (below).
    • 
    
  • Force-close cost: a unilateral close publishes the broadcaster's current commitment transaction plus,
      for every in-flight HTLC, a separate claim transaction — one on-chain transaction per pending HTLC, at
      whatever the prevailing feerate is at the moment of the dispute, which is usually the worst possible
      moment (§11 covers exactly this failure).
    • 
    
  • Penalty economics: broadcasting a revoked state is rational only if the expected gain (the balance
      difference versus the current state) exceeds the near-certain loss of the entire channel to a watching
      counterparty, plus the fee to claim it. For any channel where the honest party (or their watchtower) checks
      the chain within the to_self_delay window, expected value of cheating is strongly negative —
      which is the point.
    • 
    
  • Channel reserve: BOLT #2 requires each party to keep a minimum reserve (commonly ~1% of channel
      capacity) that can never be moved to the other side. This isn't arbitrary — it guarantees both parties always
      have some balance to justify a force-close's fee and keeps at least one output above the dust limit
      chapter 6 defined, so a commitment transaction is never left unable to pay for its own eventual settlement.

ROUTING FAILURE: THE DOMINANT REAL-WORLD UX PROBLEM

    
The network deliberately does not gossip each channel's directional balance — only its total
    capacity, and its fee/CLTV policy, are announced (BOLT #7 channel_update). Advertising live
    balances would leak exactly the transaction-graph information channels exist to hide, and balances change
    every payment, which would flood the gossip network with updates. The direct consequence: a sender can compute
    a route that is well-formed and cheap on paper, attempt it, and have it fail at hop 3 of 5 because that
    specific channel happens to be nearly drained in the needed direction — information no one could have told
    them in advance. Real implementations compensate with pathfinding heuristics (probabilistic liquidity models
    updated from past successes/failures), multi-part payments (splitting one payment across several smaller HTLCs
    over different routes, BOLT #4's MPP), and retry-on-failure — but "will this route work" remains, structurally,
    a question you answer by trying, not by asking.

  







  
ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS

  
Three failures that changed how channels are built and operated


  
Incident 1: fixed-fee commitments and the stuck force-close (→ Anchor Outputs, 2020)

  
Original commitment transactions had their fee baked in and signed at commitment-update time, based on
  whatever feerate looked reasonable then. Bitcoin's fee market (chapter 11) is not stable minute to minute; during
  the fee spikes of 2017–2018, some channels' pre-signed commitment transactions carried feerates that were
  reasonable when signed and far too low by the time a force-close actually needed to confirm — and because the
  transaction was already fully signed by both parties, there was no way to bump its fee without a fresh signature
  from a counterparty who, in a dispute, might be exactly the party with no incentive to cooperate. Root cause:
  coupling fee selection to signing time in a construction whose entire premise is that broadcast might happen
  much later, adversarially, when neither party can be assumed to cooperate. Fix: BOLT #3's anchor outputs
  update added two small, always-present "anchor" outputs to every commitment transaction, spendable by anyone
  after a delay, specifically so either party can attach a child transaction and use Child-Pays-For-Parent (chapter
  11) to bump the commitment's effective feerate at broadcast time, unilaterally, using their own separate funds —
  decoupling "the fee that was fair when we signed" from "the fee needed to actually confirm."


  
Incident 2: Flood & Loot (Harris & Zohar, 2020)

  
Academic disclosure, not a live theft, but a precise demonstration of a real structural weakness. The paper
  showed that an attacker controlling both ends of several routed HTLCs could deliberately let many timeouts
  converge on the same block height, then flood the mempool with their own competing, low-value transactions right
  before that height — congesting it enough that honest nodes' timeout-claim transactions (needed to reclaim funds
  from expiring HTLCs) miss their deadline. Once a timeout is missed, the attacker's own preimage-based claim on
  the other side of the same HTLC succeeds instead, so the attacker recovers funds that were never economically
  theirs. Root cause: a fixed, public block-height deadline creates a predictable congestion target, and
  Bitcoin's fee-priority mempool means congestion is a purchasable resource. Mitigation, and its limit:
  wider safety margins (larger cltv_expiry_delta) and fee-bumping via anchor outputs both help, but
  neither eliminates the underlying tension — chapter 11's pinning discussion is the general form of this same
  problem, and no complete fix exists that doesn't cost either capital efficiency (wider margins tie up funds
  longer) or added on-chain fee spend.


  
Incident 3: the replacement cycling attack (Antoine Riard, disclosed 2022–2023)

  
A more targeted descendant of the same family. Bitcoin Core's replace-by-fee rules (BIP125, chapter 11) let a
  transaction be replaced by a conflicting one paying a higher fee. Riard's disclosure showed that an attacker
  could construct their own low-value transaction that conflicts with (double-spends an input needed by) a
  victim's justice or HTLC-claim transaction, then repeatedly replace their own conflicting transaction with a
  slightly different version of itself — a "replacement cycle" — each cycle evicting the victim's pending
  transaction from mempools it had just re-entered, without the attacker's transaction ever actually confirming.
  Cycled for long enough, this pushes the victim's confirmation past their HTLC timeout, letting the attacker claim
  funds on the other side that should have reverted. Root cause: RBF's replacement rules were designed
  around single, independent transactions; they did not anticipate an adversary using the ability to replace
  their own transaction, repeatedly and cheaply, purely to deny mempool space to someone else's
  time-sensitive transaction. This directly informed the 2023 mempool-policy discussions on the bitcoin-dev mailing
  list about full-RBF and pinning more broadly — an area chapter 11 flags as still actively evolving.


  

    
STILL UNRESOLVED: CHANNEL JAMMING

    
As of this writing, channel jamming has no complete deployed fix. An attacker who controls both ends of a
    route (or just wants to grief) can send HTLCs that they never intend to settle, holding routing nodes' liquidity
    locked for up to the maximum timeout (which can be on the order of two weeks for a long route) at negligible
    cost to themselves — a denial-of-service against routing capacity rather than against funds. Proposed fixes
    (unconditional forwarding fees charged regardless of success, reputation/stake-based admission, HODL-invoice
    restrictions) each trade away something — usually fee simplicity or permissionless routing — and none has
    reached the kind of universal deployment BOLT-level standardization implies. This is a legitimately open
    problem, not a solved one being explained after the fact.

ARC 13 · CODE WALKTHROUGH

  
Where the enforcement actually lives

  
Lightning itself is not in Bitcoin Core — it is a separate peer-to-peer protocol (specified by the BOLTs and
  implemented by LND, Core Lightning, Eclair, and others) that only ever touches the base layer at open and close.
  What is in Core, and load-bearing for every claim this chapter makes, are the two opcodes that make
  timelocked, revocable outputs enforceable by consensus rather than by promise: OP_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY
  (the relative delay on to_local) and OP_CHECKLOCKTIMEVERIFY (the absolute HTLC timeout).
  Read those first, then the BOLT #3 script text they enforce.

First, the two consensus primitives. Both are soft-fork opcodes — before their respective BIPs they were
  OP_NOP3/OP_NOP2, no-ops that scripts could include harmlessly; the soft fork redefined
  them to fail the script unless a condition holds, which is why old, non-upgraded nodes still accept these
  transactions (they see a no-op) while upgraded nodes enforce the new rule:


  

    
C++script/interpreter.cpp — OP_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY (simplified, faithful to structure)

    
case OP_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY:
{
    // BIP112: the top stack item is the relative-locktime an input
    // MUST have waited before this script can succeed -- e.g. Lightning's
    // to_self_delay guarding a commitment's to_local output.
    if (!(flags & SCRIPT_VERIFY_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY)) {
        break; // pre-fork: behaves as OP_NOP3, always succeeds
    }
    if (stack.size() < 1)
        return set_error(serror, SCRIPT_ERR_INVALID_STACK_OPERATION);

    const CScriptNum nSequence(stacktop(-1), fRequireMinimal, 5);
    if (nSequence < 0)
        return set_error(serror, SCRIPT_ERR_NEGATIVE_LOCKTIME);
    if (nSequence.GetInt64() & CTxIn::SEQUENCE_LOCKTIME_DISABLE_FLAG)
        break; // script author explicitly opted out of the check

    // checker delegates to the actual spending input's nSequence field
    // and the UTXO's confirmation depth -- this is where "has enough
    // blocks actually passed since this output confirmed" gets decided.
    if (!checker.CheckSequence(nSequence))
        return set_error(serror, SCRIPT_ERR_UNSATISFIED_LOCKTIME);
    break;
}

  


  

    
C++script/interpreter.cpp — OP_CHECKLOCKTIMEVERIFY (simplified)

    
case OP_CHECKLOCKTIMEVERIFY:
{
    // BIP65: the top stack item is an ABSOLUTE block height or
    // timestamp before which this script cannot succeed -- Lightning's
    // HTLC timeout branch: "Bob can reclaim after block T if Carol
    // never revealed the preimage."
    if (!(flags & SCRIPT_VERIFY_CHECKLOCKTIMEVERIFY)) {
        break; // pre-fork: OP_NOP2, always succeeds
    }
    const CScriptNum nLockTime(stacktop(-1), fRequireMinimal, 5);
    if (nLockTime < 0)
        return set_error(serror, SCRIPT_ERR_NEGATIVE_LOCKTIME);
    if (!checker.CheckLockTime(nLockTime))
        return set_error(serror, SCRIPT_ERR_UNSATISFIED_LOCKTIME);
    break; // note: this only VERIFIES -- it does not consume the
                // stack item, so scripts follow it with OP_DROP
}

  


  
Now the scripts BOLT #3 actually builds from those two opcodes. This is the offered-HTLC witness script — the
  one Alice's commitment holds, giving Bob two ways in and giving Alice one, exactly matching §8(c)'s derivation:


  

    
Script (BOLT #3)offered_htlc_script — three spending branches, annotated

    
# Branch 1 (fastest path): remote node reveals the preimage and
# claims immediately -- this is Bob claiming from Alice's HTLC.
OP_DUP OP_HASH160 <RIPEMD160(revocationkey)> OP_EQUAL
OP_IF
    # Branch 0 (always available): counterparty proves it holds the
    # REVOCATION key for this exact commitment -- i.e. this state was
    # revoked and is being cheated with. Immediate, no delay, no HTLC
    # condition at all: revocation always wins.
    OP_CHECKSIG
OP_ELSE
    <remote_htlcpubkey> OP_SWAP OP_SIZE 32 OP_EQUAL
    OP_NOTIF
        # Branch 2 (timeout path): NOT a 32-byte preimage on the stack --
        # must be Alice reclaiming after CLTV expiry.
        OP_DROP 2 OP_SWAP <local_htlcpubkey> 2 OP_CHECKMULTISIG
    OP_ELSE
        # Branch 3 (success path): a real 32-byte value was given --
        # verify it actually hashes to H before allowing the claim.
        OP_HASH160 <RIPEMD160(SHA256(R))> OP_EQUALVERIFY
        OP_CHECKSIG
    OP_ENDIF
OP_ENDIF

  


  

    
WHY THIS WASN'T BUILT INTO BITCOIN CORE ITSELF

    
Three reasons, and they generalize to most Layer 2 design: (1) iteration speed — Lightning's protocol
    has revised its transaction formats, added anchor outputs, and is still evolving PTLC support; a consensus
    change to Bitcoin Core requires the entire network to upgrade, which takes years, while a BOLT revision only
    needs the participating Lightning nodes to agree. (2) minimality — Core's job is to provide the smallest
    stable set of primitives (timelocks, multisig, hash locks) that many different Layer 2 protocols can build on;
    baking Lightning-specific logic into consensus would privilege one design over channel factories, statechains,
    or whatever comes next. (3) failure containment — a bug in a Lightning implementation can lose funds in
    channels using that implementation; a bug in Core's script interpreter is a critical vulnerability across every
    Bitcoin holder simultaneously. Keeping the enforcement primitives generic and small in consensus, and the
    actual protocol logic in independently-implemented, independently-upgradable software, is the same
    minimal-trusted-computing-base instinct chapter 4's Script chapter argued for when explaining why loops were
    excluded.
