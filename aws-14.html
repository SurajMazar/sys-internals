Cost Optimization
Fundamentals

Cost-Optimized Architectures is worth 20% of the Associate exam on its own — and it rewards a completely different instinct than the other three domains: instead of "what makes this more resilient/secure/ performant," it asks "what's the smallest amount of money that still satisfies everything the scenario actually requires." This chapter builds that instinct through right-sizing, purchasing options, and the governance tools AWS gives you to keep spend honest.

ARC 01 · EXAM CONTEXT

Where this sits on the real blueprint

This chapter maps to Design Cost-Optimized Architectures — 20% of the SAA-C03 blueprint. It also underpins two Professional-level domains you'll meet later in this course: Continuous Improvement for Existing Solutions (25% of SAP-C02, chapter 21 goes deeper on cost governance at organization scale) builds directly on the tools introduced here, and cost is one of the recurring "hidden constraints" inside Design Solutions for Organizational Complexity (26% of SAP-C02) scenarios.

Nearly every cost question on either exam is testing one of exactly three ideas: (1) is the resource even sized correctly for its actual load, (2) given the workload's shape — steady vs bursty, interruption- tolerant vs not, committed vs unpredictable — which purchasing option fits, and (3) which AWS tool is the right one to notice, analyze, or alert on a given cost signal. Get comfortable naming all three quickly and this domain becomes one of the fastest 20% of points on the whole exam.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE REST OF THIS CHAPTER

Cost questions rarely name a dollar amount. They describe a workload's behavior — how long it runs, whether it can be interrupted, how predictable it is — and expect you to translate that description into a purchasing decision. That translation is the actual skill; the specific discount percentages AWS currently offers are not something either exam will test, and they change over time anyway.

It helps to notice how this domain differs in kind from the other three SAA-C03 domains. Design Secure, Resilient, and High-Performing Architectures each ask "what does this scenario require, and what satisfies it?" Design Cost-Optimized Architectures asks that same question and then adds a second constraint on top: among every option that satisfies the requirement, which one costs the least? That's why nearly every question in this chapter's decision framework (§9) has a "don't use when" column pointing back at a cheaper option — cost questions are almost never about whether something works, they're about whether something cheaper also works.

ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

Two reflexes the exam is built to catch

Ask a room of candidates to "optimize cost" and two wrong reflexes show up constantly:

  • "Buy commitment first, everywhere." Reserved Instances or Savings Plans feel like the obvious cost lever — commit to spend, get a discount — so candidates reach for them before checking whether the underlying resources are even the right size for the workload in the first place.
  • "Spot is cheap, so use it wherever the budget is tight." Spot's steep discount makes it tempting to apply everywhere cost matters, without checking whether the workload can actually tolerate an instance disappearing with two minutes' notice.
  • "A tight budget means the cheapest single instance-level lever, applied uniformly." A related reflex treats every line item in a bill the same way, reaching for one purchasing trick across an entire fleet instead of asking whether different parts of that fleet actually have different shapes and deserve different answers.

Both instincts skip a step. §3 explains exactly which step, and why skipping it is the specific mechanism that makes both reflexes wrong — not just "risky," but concretely, quantifiably wasteful or unsafe. A third, quieter reflex sits underneath both: assuming a purchasing decision is a one-time, set-and-forget action rather than something that needs the ongoing governance tooling covered in §7 — a right answer today can silently become a wrong one a year later if nothing is watching for drift.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

Commitment on the wrong size, and Spot on the wrong workload

A purchasing-option discount is a multiplier on whatever you're already paying for — it does nothing to fix a resource that's oversized for its actual load. If an application runs comfortably on a smaller instance type than the one it's deployed on, committing to a 1- or 3-year Reserved Instance or Savings Plan on the oversized instance locks in the waste for the length of the commitment, and often makes it operationally awkward to right-size later without leaving a partly-unused commitment behind. Right-sizing first, purchasing option second isn't a stylistic preference — it's the only order that doesn't waste money twice.

Spot's failure mode is different and just as mechanical. AWS can reclaim a Spot Instance with a two-minute interruption notice whenever EC2 needs the capacity back — a real, stable, load-bearing fact about the product, not a rare edge case. That's a fine trade for a workload that can checkpoint, retry, or simply lose that one worker without consequence: batch processing, stateless horizontally-scaled workers behind a queue, CI build fleets, big-data/analytics jobs, some categories of ECS/EKS-scheduled worker tasks. It is the wrong trade for anything that can't tolerate an abrupt, uncoordinated loss of capacity — a single-instance database, a stateful session server with no replication, anything on the critical path of a customer-facing request with no redundancy behind it. "Cheap" and "interruption-tolerant" are two separate facts about a workload, and the exam tests whether you check the second one before reaching for the first.

THE PATTERN TO INTERNALIZE

Cost optimization is a two-gate check, always in this order: (1) is the resource sized correctly for its real utilization — fix this before anything else — then (2) does the workload's duration and interruption tolerance match the purchasing option being considered. Skipping straight to gate 2 without clearing gate 1 first is the single most common wrong answer pattern in this domain.

Both gates fail in the same underlying way: they treat a purchasing decision as independent of the workload it's attached to, when it never is. A purchasing option is a wrapper around a resource that already exists — it cannot retroactively fix that resource's size, and it cannot retroactively make an interruption-sensitive workload interruption-tolerant. Every wrong answer in this domain, traced back far enough, is really an attempt to solve a sizing or interruption-tolerance problem with a billing lever instead of an architecture change.

Once you notice that shape, a large fraction of both exams starts to feel like the same small number of questions wearing different service names.

THE PATTERN UNDERNEATH ALL FOUR OPTIONS

Every option is really trading along one axis: how much certainty you give AWS about your future usage, in exchange for how much discount AWS gives you back. On-Demand gives zero certainty and gets zero discount. Spot gives AWS the right to reclaim capacity on short notice — a different kind of certainty trade — and gets the deepest discount as a result. Reserved Instances and Savings Plans sit in between, trading a time commitment for a middle-depth discount, with Savings Plans generally trading a bit of specificity (which exact instance family/Region) for more flexibility than a classic Reserved Instance.

Framed this way, "which purchasing option" stops being four separate facts to memorize and becomes one question answered on a sliding scale: how much certainty can this specific workload honestly offer AWS about its own future? A workload that can answer "very little" belongs on the left of this explorer; one that can answer "a great deal, for years" belongs on the right.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

What the checklist still has to respect

  • A Savings Plan is a dollar-per-hour commitment, not an instance reservation. It applies automatically to usage up to the committed hourly amount across eligible usage (compute or EC2-specific, depending on the plan type), which makes it flexible across instance family and size changes — but it does not guarantee capacity availability the way a specific Reserved Instance can.
  • A standard Reserved Instance can include a zonal capacity reservation; a Savings Plan does not guarantee capacity at all. If a scenario specifically needs guaranteed capacity in a given Availability Zone (not just a bill discount), that detail alone can rule out a Savings Plan as the complete answer.
  • Spot's interruption notice is a fixed, real number: two minutes. Any design relying on Spot needs to actually do something useful with that warning — checkpoint progress, drain a queue, let an orchestrator reschedule the work — or the "discount" becomes a reliability problem in production.
  • Reserved Instances and Savings Plans both require a term commitment — typically 1 or 3 years — and neither can be meaningfully "returned" mid-term the way you might hope; a wrong or oversized commitment is a cost of its own for the rest of the term, which is exactly why right-sizing has to happen first.
  • Compute Optimizer's recommendations are only as good as the CloudWatch data behind them. A resource that's new, or whose monitoring was recently changed, may not yet have enough utilization history for a confident right-sizing recommendation — the exam sometimes tests whether you recognize that a recommendation needs a minimum observation window to be trustworthy.
  • Free, generic advice is not the same as billing-account-specific analysis. Trusted Advisor's cost checks are broad, rules-of-thumb recommendations (idle load balancers, low-utilization instances) available to every account at some level; Cost Explorer and Compute Optimizer instead work from your account's actual billing and utilization history. A scenario asking for "quick, broad recommendations with minimal setup" points at Trusted Advisor; one asking for "detailed analysis of our specific spend or workload" points at Cost Explorer or Compute Optimizer.
  • On-Demand is not "the expensive option to avoid" — it's the correct answer for a real class of scenarios. A workload that's genuinely new, genuinely short-lived, or genuinely still being profiled has no honest steady-state data to commit against yet. Recommending a commitment there isn't cautious, it's guessing with a multi-year price tag attached — and the exam occasionally rewards "stay on On-Demand for now" as the correct, disciplined answer over a tempting-looking commitment.
  • A purchasing decision made for the wrong reason can still look right in hindsight. A team that buys a 3-year Reserved Instance on a whim and happens to keep that exact workload shape for three years got lucky, not right — the exam is testing the decision process (data-driven right-sizing, then a matched purchasing option), not whether a given guess happened to pay off.
A TRAP WORTH NAMING TWICE

"Switch everything to Spot" and "buy a 3-year Reserved Instance for everything" are both wrong for the identical reason stated in opposite directions: neither checks the workload's actual shape before committing to a purchasing strategy. The exam will dangle both as tempting-sounding "the cheap option" distractors.

One more edge case worth holding onto: a fleet can be a mix of purchasing options at the same time, and often should be. A common, real pattern is a baseline of Reserved Instances or a Savings Plan sized to the fleet's steady minimum load, topped up with On-Demand or Spot capacity for whatever traffic exceeds that baseline. The exam sometimes tests whether you recognize that "which purchasing option" doesn't have to be a single, fleet- wide answer — it can be a blend matched to the different shapes of load within the same fleet. This is the same "steady floor vs. variable peak" split that shows up again in chapter 21's organization-wide Savings Plans material, just applied at a single fleet's scale instead of across an entire account structure.

WHY TAGS ARE AT THE BOTTOM OF THE STACK

None of the analysis or alerting tools above can attribute spend to a team, project, or environment without consistent cost allocation tags underneath them. A scenario describing "we can't tell which team is spending what" is almost always a tagging-strategy gap first, no matter which analysis tool it also mentions.

Notice, too, that this diagram has no arrows pointing back down into the resources themselves — every one of these tools is observational or advisory, not enforcing. Nothing here stops a team from launching an oversized instance or an untagged resource; that enforcement layer (budgets with actions, service control policies, provisioning guardrails) belongs to the multi-account governance material in chapters 16 and 21. This chapter's tools tell you the truth about what's happening; they don't, by themselves, prevent what happens next. Keep that distinction sharp for the exam: a scenario asking "how do we find out we're overspending" wants a tool from this section; a scenario asking "how do we stop it from happening at all" wants a guardrail from later in the course.

This table is the single most exam-relevant artifact in the chapter — read it by first asking "can this workload be interrupted?" If no, Spot is eliminated immediately, regardless of cost — and the remaining choice is purely about how predictable and steady the usage is. If yes, Spot becomes the default first choice to evaluate, because nothing else touches its discount depth.

It's worth being explicit about what this table deliberately leaves out: a literal discount percentage for any option. That's not an oversight — it's the point. AWS periodically adjusts specific discount depths by instance family, Region, and term length, and neither exam anchors a question to a number that changes on a schedule outside the exam's control. What doesn't change is the ordering itself: Spot below Reserved Instances and Savings Plans, both of those below On-Demand. Memorize the shape of the table, not a number that was never going to be tested in the first place.

ARC 10 · RELATIVE DISCOUNT ORDER

The ordering that doesn't change, even though the numbers do

AWS's specific discount percentages move over time and depend on instance family, Region, and term length — which is exactly why this exam does not test a specific number. What it does test, reliably, is the ordering: On-Demand is the ceiling every other option discounts against; Spot is reliably the deepest discount of the four; Reserved Instances and Savings Plans sit in between, with Savings Plans generally trading a little of a Reserved Instance's specificity for more flexibility at a broadly comparable discount depth.

Relative cost index vs. flexibilityILLUSTRATIVE INDEX, NOT DOLLARS
━ relative cost index (On-Demand = baseline)

Treat the curve as ordinal, not arithmetic: it tells you Spot is reliably cheapest and On-Demand is reliably priciest of the four, not that any specific gap between them is a fixed percentage.

One consequence of this ordering is worth stating plainly: it never flips. AWS has adjusted specific discount depths many times over the years, and instance-family pricing varies, but no repricing event has ever made On-Demand cheaper than a matched Reserved Instance/Savings Plan commitment, or made a commitment-based option cheaper than Spot for the same capacity. If a scenario or an answer option implies otherwise — a Reserved Instance somehow beating Spot on raw discount depth, say — treat that as a signal you've misread the scenario, not as a new fact to learn.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three original, exam-shaped scenarios

Original constructions in the style the real exam uses — not leaked or real exam content.

Ferro Analytics — nightly batch processing

Profile: a data-analytics company runs a large nightly batch job on a fleet of horizontally-scaled worker nodes that read from a queue, process a chunk of data, and write results, retrying automatically on failure. Constraints: the job has no fixed deadline within the night and easily tolerates a worker disappearing mid-task. Correct choice: Spot Instances, sized to whatever instance types are cheapest and available at run time. The workload's shape — retryable, stateless, horizontally scaled, no hard real-time deadline — is exactly Spot's target profile, and passing up its discount here would be leaving the cheapest correct answer on the table. A well-built version of this fleet also diversifies across several instance types and Availability Zones, since drawing from a wider pool of spare capacity reduces how often the whole fleet feels an interruption wave at once.

Ledgerly — always-on transaction database

Profile: a fintech company runs a single always-on primary database instance that has shown flat, predictable utilization for the past year and has no plans to change instance family. Constraints: the company wants to reduce compute cost without changing its architecture. Correct choice: a standard Reserved Instance (or a Savings Plan if some flexibility across family/size is still desired). Spot is immediately eliminated — this is a stateful, always-on primary with no tolerance for interruption — and the workload's steady, predictable, long-term nature is precisely what a 1- or 3-year commitment is built to discount. Note what would change the answer: if this same database sat behind a Multi-AZ deployment with a promotable standby, the primary's purchasing decision still wouldn't move — Multi-AZ is an availability feature layered on top, not a change to how predictable or interruption-tolerant the workload is.

Voltra Media — spiky, unpredictable traffic

Profile: a media startup's traffic is highly seasonal and unpredictable, with no reliable pattern year over year yet. Constraints: the team wants to avoid overcommitting before they understand their own usage shape. Correct choice: On-Demand, at least until enough utilization history exists to right-size and identify a steady baseline worth committing to. Reaching for a Reserved Instance or Savings Plan here — before the workload's actual shape is known — is the "buy commitment first" trap from §2 in its purest form. Once a full seasonal cycle of utilization data exists, the team can split the fleet the way the callout in §6 describes: a Savings Plan sized to the observed floor, On-Demand or Spot layered on top for the seasonal peaks.

WHY THE EXAM CARES ABOUT THIS

The exam tests which tool answers which question, not CLI syntax: Cost Explorer/ce get-cost-and-usage answers "where has money already gone," Compute Optimizer answers "is this resource the right size," Budgets answers "tell me before I overspend," and Trusted Advisor answers "give me broad, low-effort recommendations without a deep analysis setup." Confusing "analysis" tools with "alerting" tools is a common wrong-answer pattern.

If you take only one thing from this section into the exam room, make it this: every one of these commands is answering a different question, and a scenario's wording almost always tells you which question is being asked, in plain English, before it ever mentions a service name.

GATE · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

Before chapter 15: what actually earns points

  • Right-size before changing a purchasing option — always in that order, never the reverse.
  • Check interruption tolerance before reaching for Spot's discount; a cheap answer that breaks the workload is still wrong.
  • The relative ordering — On-Demand priciest, Spot deepest discount, Reserved Instances/Savings Plans in between — is the stable fact; specific percentages are not, and the exam won't test them.
  • Match the tool to the question: Cost Explorer for "where did the money go," Compute Optimizer for "is this the right size," Budgets for "warn me before I overspend," Trusted Advisor for "quick broad checks."
  • A Savings Plan is a spend commitment, not a capacity reservation — if a scenario needs guaranteed capacity, that's a different question than getting a discount.
  • A fleet doesn't have to pick one purchasing option — a steady baseline on a commitment plus On-Demand or Spot layered on top for variable load is a legitimate, commonly correct real-world pattern.

Chapter 14 complete

You can now right-size before purchasing, match a workload's shape to the correct purchasing option, and name the right governance tool for a given cost question.
