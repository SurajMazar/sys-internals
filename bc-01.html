BLOCKCHAIN FUNDAMENTALS · CHAPTER 01

The Problem:
Digital Money Without a Bank

A digital coin is a bit string, and bit strings copy perfectly. That single fact — not cryptography, not networking, not game theory — is the entire reason building digital cash is hard. This chapter derives the double-spend problem from first principles, shows precisely how three plausible naive designs each collapse, and studies the four real research efforts (DigiCash, b-money, bit gold, hashcash) that got closer than anyone before 2008 without quite closing the gap. Everything from chapter 2 onward is the answer to what's left open here.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

Copying is not the exception. It's the default.

Start with the fact that makes this whole course necessary: a digital object is trivially copyable. A JPEG, a PDF, a save file, a digital coin — all of them are, at the hardware level, just bytes in memory, and copying bytes is what computers are for. There is no operation called "move" at the level of physics for data the way there is for a physical object. Every "move" you have ever performed on a computer — dragging a file, "sending" a message — is implemented as copy, then (maybe) delete the original. Nothing stops a program from skipping the delete.

Physical cash never had this problem, and it's worth being precise about why. A ten-dollar bill is a single object occupying a single location in space. Handing it to someone is rivalrous by the laws of physics: after the handoff, you do not have it and they do. Nobody needed to invent a protocol for this — matter enforces it for free. The question "did Alice really give Bob the bill, or does she still have it too?" is not a question you can even coherently ask about a physical object changing hands once.

Now ask the same question about a digital coin — say, a file called coin_4471.dat containing Alice's digital signature authorizing a transfer to Bob. Alice can email that exact file to Bob. She can also email the exact same bytes to Carol, thirty seconds later, from the same laptop, without doing anything technically unusual at all. Both emails are byte-for-byte legitimate. Nothing about the act of copying a file is different depending on whether the file represents a birthday photo or an alleged unit of money. "Spending" a bit string cannot mean "giving it away" in the physical sense, because bit strings don't have the physical property that makes giving-away well-defined. This is the double-spend problem, and everything else in this chapter — every scheme, every failure, every historical attempt — is a response to that one paragraph.

WHAT A BANK ACTUALLY SELLS YOU

Before dismissing banks as legacy middlemen, be precise about what a bank account gives you that a signed digital IOU alone does not. Three things, and you will spend the rest of this course rebuilding each one without a bank:

  • A single authoritative ordering of events. When you check your balance, the bank has already decided, unambiguously, which of your transactions happened first, second, third. There is exactly one history, not one per branch.
  • Finality. Once a transfer clears, it is done. The bank will not let you re-spend money that left your account five minutes ago, no matter how you ask.
  • Identity and recourse. The bank knows who you are, can reverse fraudulent transfers, can freeze a thief's account, and can be sued, subpoenaed, or regulated when something goes wrong. There is an accountable party at the end of the chain.

Notice what these three things have in common: none of them is a cryptographic property. You do not need a bank to prove that a signature is valid — public-key cryptography, decades old by 2008, already does that perfectly well without any central party. What you need a bank for is exactly the thing cryptography by itself cannot give you: agreement, among people who don't fully trust each other, on which of two conflicting events happened first. That is an ordering problem, not a secrecy problem, and it is why this chapter is titled "digital money without a bank" and not "digital money without cryptography." Chapters 2–4 build the cryptographic primitives (hashing, Merkle trees, signatures). Chapters 5 onward build the ordering mechanism. This chapter's only job is to make sure you feel, in your bones, why the ordering mechanism is the hard and interesting part.

THE DOUBLE-SPEND PROBLEM, PREVIEWED FORMALLY

Section 4 will state this precisely once you've seen it fail in code, but hold the shape of it now: without a total order on transactions, two conflicting spends of the same coin are both individually valid. Not "hard to detect" — valid. Each one, examined alone, is a perfectly well-formed, correctly signed instruction. The only thing that makes them conflict is each other, and "each other" is exactly the information a purely local check can never see.

ARC 02 · HISTORICAL CONTEXT

1982–2008: twenty-six years of getting close

Bitcoin's 2008 whitepaper is often taught as if it appeared from nowhere. It didn't. Every major ingredient — blind signatures, cryptographic timestamping, proof-of-work as a cost function, proposals for decentralized ledgers — existed in the cryptography and cypherpunk literature well before Satoshi Nakamoto's paper, and Nakamoto's own references cite several of them directly. What was missing was not a piece of math; it was a way to combine the pieces so that strangers, with no trusted third party, could agree on order. Nearly every name on this timeline after 1992 was a subscriber to the cypherpunks mailing list, an open forum devoted to the idea that cryptography could enforce social and political guarantees — privacy, anti-censorship, freedom from centralized control — that law alone wasn't delivering. ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: a coin is just a signed IOU

Derive the naive approach honestly, because it is not stupid — it is the obvious first move, and it gets the cryptography completely right. Represent a coin as a small record, and represent "spending" it as producing a digital signature that authorizes a transfer:

Pythonnaive_cash.py — the strawman, annotated
class Coin:
    def __init__(self, coin_id, amount, owner_pubkey):
        self.coin_id = coin_id            # e.g. a random 128-bit serial
        self.amount = amount
        self.owner_pubkey = owner_pubkey  # whoever currently "holds" it

def spend(coin, new_owner_pubkey, owner_privkey):
    """'Spending' just means: sign a message authorizing transfer."""
    msg = f"{coin.coin_id}:{coin.amount}:{new_owner_pubkey}"
    signature = sign(msg, owner_privkey)          # real ECDSA/RSA — ch. 4
    return (coin, new_owner_pubkey, signature)     # <-- this tuple IS the payment

def verify(payment):
    coin, new_owner, sig = payment
    msg = f"{coin.coin_id}:{coin.amount}:{new_owner}"
    return verify_signature(msg, sig, coin.owner_pubkey)  # True/False. Nothing else.

This is genuinely good cryptography. If Mallory tries to forge a transfer of Alice's coin without Alice's private key, verify_signature correctly returns False — nobody can steal a coin they don't hold the key for. Public-key signatures solve authorization completely: they prove beyond cryptographic doubt that the legitimate owner, and only the legitimate owner, approved this exact transfer. For decades this was treated as most of the problem. It is not. Watch what verify() actually checks, and — just as importantly — what it does not.

  • verify() takes one payment as input and asks a purely local question: "was this message signed by the claimed owner's key?"
  • It has no memory. It doesn't consult a database, a log, or any other verify() call that ever happened anywhere else.
  • It therefore cannot distinguish "the only time this coin was ever spent" from "one of several times this coin was spent" — those are not properties of a single message, they're properties of a history of messages, which this function never sees.
ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

Not probabilistically. Deterministically. Every time.

Run the attack. Alice holds one Coin worth $50. She calls spend() twice on the identical coin object — once naming Bob as the new owner, once naming Carol — using the same private key both times:

  • payment_bob = spend(coin, bob_pubkey, alice_privkey)
  • payment_carol = spend(coin, carol_pubkey, alice_privkey)

She emails payment_bob to Bob and payment_carol to Carol. Bob runs verify(payment_bob) on his machine: True. Carol runs verify(payment_carol) on hers: also True. Both verifications are completely correct — Alice's signature really does authorize each transfer, in isolation. There is no bug to patch here and no edge case to catch; the scheme is doing exactly what it was designed to do, twice, for two mutually exclusive outcomes. This is not a probabilistic weakness that occasionally lets an attacker get lucky. Given no shared state between Bob and Carol, the attack succeeds with probability 1, every single time, and costs Alice nothing beyond the price of sending one extra email.

THE DOUBLE-SPEND PROBLEM, STATED FORMALLY

Without a total order on transactions, two conflicting spends of the same coin are both individually valid. "Individually valid" is doing the work in that sentence: each transaction, checked against the rules that apply to it alone (correct signature, correct format, sufficient claimed balance), passes. What makes them a double-spend is a relationship between the two transactions — they reference the same coin — and a relationship between two events is not a property either event carries by itself. You cannot fix this by making signatures stronger, hashes longer, or keys bigger. The defect is architectural: nothing in the design produces or checks an ordering, so there is no fact of the matter about which spend happened "first." Every remaining section of this course is, in one way or another, a mechanism for manufacturing that missing fact.

Now put a number on the canvas below: watch Alice's identical coin object clone itself toward two recipients continuously. There is no animation trick here — the diagram is literally showing you that the "clone" operation for a bit string and the "spend" operation for this naive scheme are the same operation.

A coin has no physics preventing thisLIVE

The same 96 bytes, twice, at the speed of your network. Nothing pictured here — not the signature, not the hash of the coin ID, not the network transport — differs between "the legitimate transfer" and "the fraudulent duplicate." They are bit-identical acts.

Two more properties worth naming precisely, because they recur for the rest of the course whenever a design is evaluated:

  • Marginal cost of the attack ≈ $0. Producing the second payment costs Alice one extra function call. Nothing about the naive scheme makes a second spend more expensive, slower, or riskier than the first — a property every later scheme in this chapter (and this course) tries to break by attaching a real cost to something.
  • Detection requires communication that the scheme never specifies. Bob and Carol will eventually discover the conflict only if they happen to compare notes — talk to each other, or to some third party who saw both payments. The naive scheme defines no such channel, so in practice detection happens (if at all) only after both merchants have already handed over $50 of goods.
ARC 05 · BETTER SOLUTION

Attempt #2: give someone a single sequential logbook

The naive scheme's defect was precise: no component of the system ever produces an order. So add exactly one: a server that every payment must pass through, which stamps each transaction with a sequence number and refuses any transaction that conflicts with one it has already stamped. This is not a new cryptographic primitive — it's a single point that gets to say "this happened, and it happened before that." Step through the derivation:

Notice the ledger's job is narrower than it looks. It does not re-verify Alice's signature — the coin scheme from §3 already does that correctly. Its only new contribution is: given two individually valid transactions that conflict, decide which one is "real" by recording it first, and reject the other. That is a total-order function, next(seq_number), bolted onto a signature scheme that was already sound. This is precisely the DigiCash and b-money-style architecture explored in §7 — and precisely the piece Nakamoto's design (chapters 5–10) replaces with something that needs no single server at all.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL HURTS

The bill for a ledger, and the tempting "fix" that doesn't work

The central ledger solves double-spending completely — as long as you trust the operator and the operator stays up. Both of those turn out to be expensive assumptions:

  • Single point of failure. If the ledger server is down, nobody can transact — not because anything about their coins is invalid, but because the one place capable of producing an order is unreachable. Compare this to physical cash, which works fine during a power outage.
  • Single point of censorship. The operator can refuse to record any transaction it doesn't like — freeze an account, blacklist a recipient, reverse an inconvenient transfer — and there is no appeal beyond whatever legal or political recourse exists outside the system. "Identity and recourse" from §1 cuts both ways: the same authority that can undo fraud against you can also undo a transaction you wanted to keep.
  • Single point of trust. Depositors are trusting the operator not to print extra balance for itself, not to leak withdrawal patterns, and not to go bankrupt holding their money. This is precisely a bank, with all of a bank's obligations and none of a bank's regulatory apparatus unless someone builds that too (§11 shows what happens when nobody does).

The tempting next move is obvious: remove the single server, and have everyone broadcast transactions to everyone else instead. No operator, no single point of failure, no one to censor you — merchants just listen for announcements and accept whatever they see. Walk through why this fails too, because the specific shape of its failure — a race, not a crash — is the shape every later chapter on consensus (6, 8, 10) exists to fix.

Give every node the seemingly reasonable local rule "accept the first valid transaction you see for a given coin, reject anything that conflicts with it afterward." Each node, individually, behaves correctly — it never accepts two conflicting spends itself. The catastrophe is that "first" is a purely local, network-position-dependent fact. Bob's node and Carol's node can each apply the identical rule flawlessly and still end up believing two different, mutually exclusive things about who owns the same coin, because each saw a different message arrive first. Nobody lied, nobody's software has a bug, and there is still no agreement. Whichever message physically reaches a given node earliest wins at that node — a race decides it, and an attacker who can influence network position (connect closer to one merchant, delay traffic to another — this is the eclipse-attack family studied in chapter 11) can bias the race deliberately. §10 quantifies exactly how much of an advantage a small timing edge buys, and §15 lets you cause this exact disagreement yourself.

WHY "JUST WAIT LONGER" DOESN'T FIX IT

The instinct is to have nodes wait before accepting, hoping conflicting messages arrive in time to be compared. This helps only if there's also a rule for what to do when two valid, differently-timed messages for the same coin both eventually arrive — and "which one do we keep" is exactly the total-order question from §4, restated. Waiting longer changes the odds of the race, but a race with no tiebreaker rule is still a race. You need either a single party everyone defers to (§5's ledger, with its costs from above) or — and this is the entire rest of the course — a way for strangers with no single party to agree on a tiebreaker anyway.

ARC 07 · WHAT WAS ACTUALLY TRIED (1982–1997)

The two failure modes above — trust a central party, or let a race decide — are not hypothetical strawmen invented for this chapter. Line them up and the missing piece is the same piece every time: none of the four specifies a concrete way for parties with no pre-existing trust to converge on a single agreed order of events, without relying on a party everyone already trusts. DigiCash solves privacy but keeps the trusted mint. b-money and bit gold both reach for proof-of-work as a way to make things costly, but neither pins down the exact rule by which independent nodes resolve a conflict the way Nakamoto consensus later will (chapter 8: the heaviest-work chain wins, and everyone can verify "heaviest" without asking anyone). You now have, in concrete historical form, a checklist for evaluating the rest of this course: for every mechanism you meet from here on, ask "does this produce order among strangers, or does it still assume someone trustworthy?"

ARC 08 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

The two mechanisms, in real arithmetic

Prose about "blind signatures" and "proof-of-work" is cheap; the actual mechanisms are short enough to compute by hand, and doing so removes any sense that either is magic. Two pieces: (a) Chaum's blind signature, worked with genuine (if tiny, insecure-at-this-size) RSA arithmetic, and (b) Back's hashcash cost function, computing genuine hashes live in your browser.

(a) Blind signatures: the bank signs what it cannot read

The trick behind DigiCash's privacy: Alice wants the bank to authorize a token, but doesn't want the bank to see the token's serial number — otherwise the bank could later link her withdrawal to her specific purchase, exactly the way a bank statement links you to every merchant you've paid. Chaum's insight was that RSA signing and multiplication commute, which lets Alice mathematically "blind" the serial number before the bank ever sees it, and "unblind" the bank's signature afterward without invalidating it. Blinding severs the link between withdrawal and spend — the bank cannot connect the token Alice cashes at a merchant to the specific blinded request it signed weeks earlier, because it never saw the unblinded serial number until redemption. That is real, mathematically-guaranteed privacy, not a policy promise. But look at what step 5 still requires: the bank maintains one database of redeemed serial numbers and rejects a serial number it has already seen. That database is still a single, central, always-online authority — blinding changes who can link a payment to a withdrawal; it does not change who gets to decide the order in which spends are recorded. DigiCash bought privacy without buying decentralization.

(b) Hashcash: buy scarcity with computation, not identity

Adam Back's 1997 hashcash was built to stop email spam, not to move money — keep that in mind, it matters for what it does not solve. The idea: attach to your email a stamp — some value whose hash, under a fixed hash function, has at least D leading zero bits. Because a hash function's output is effectively unpredictable from its input, the only way to find such a stamp is brute-force search: try random nonces until one happens to hash to something with enough leading zeros. Finding one costs, in expectation, 2D hash computations; checking whether a given stamp is valid costs exactly one. That asymmetry — expensive to produce, free to verify — is the entire mechanism, and it is the single idea chapter 8 reuses, unchanged, to make mining costly.

WHY HASHCASH ALONE ISN'T A CURRENCY

Notice what the grinder above produces: proof that some computer recently burned real work. It proves nothing about balances, ownership, or transfers, because hashcash was never given a concept of any of those. There is no "coin" object here to double-spend — you can grind the exact same stamp difficulty twice with two different nonces and nothing conflicts, because nothing represents a claim on scarce value in the first place. Hashcash is a pure cost function, the sharpest tool this chapter studies for making an action expensive without a central issuer of permission — but a tool is not a system. §7's gap analysis called this precisely: a mechanism waiting for a currency to be built around it. Chapter 7 (Sybil resistance) and chapter 8 (proof-of-work as consensus) are that construction.

ARC 10 · PERFORMANCE / SECURITY CHARACTERISTICS

Quantifying the race, and what an attack actually costs

§6 asserted that in a naive broadcast scheme, "a race decides" who a merchant believes owns a coin, and that an attacker with a network-position advantage can bias that race. Put a number on it. The curve is an analytic sketch (a logistic function of Δ/jitter), not a measurement of any deployed system — this course has not yet built one. The point is the shape: with no consensus rule, even a modest, cheap-to-obtain network advantage (connecting with lower latency to one merchant, or delaying traffic to the other — chapter 11's eclipse attacks are exactly this, professionalized) pushes the outcome heavily in the attacker's favor, for an attack that costs roughly the price of a well-placed server. The dashed reference line for the central ledger sits near zero regardless of Δ, because in that architecture the network race is irrelevant — order is decided by the ledger, not by whoever's packet arrives first. That's the entire value of §5's design, quantified, and also exactly why its cost (a single trusted operator) is the price of removing this race.

Rough orders of magnitude worth carrying forward, since later chapters will make each one precise:

  • Forging a signature (breaking §3's authorization layer): computationally infeasible with modern curves — chapter 4 puts a real number on this (~2128 operations for secp256k1).
  • Double-spending the naive IOU scheme (§3–4): the cost of sending one extra message — effectively $0, and deterministic, not probabilistic.
  • Double-spending past a central ledger (§5): requires compromising, bribing, or legally compelling the operator — a security and governance cost, not a computational one.
  • Winning the naive-broadcast race (§6, §10 above): the cost of a modest network-positioning advantage against a specific victim — cheap, and this course's chapter 11 shows real techniques for obtaining it.
  • Double-spending past Nakamoto consensus (preview, ch. 8): requires sustained majority computational power across the entire honest network — the first defense in this chapter's history whose cost genuinely scales with the size of the network defending against it, rather than staying fixed.
ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS

Three times this exact tension broke something real

Incident 1: DigiCash Inc. files for bankruptcy (1998)

Chaum's cryptography worked. DigiCash's ecash protocol shipped, ran in pilots with Deutsche Bank, Credit Suisse, and Mark Twain Bank in the US, and delivered exactly the blinded-token privacy derived in §8. The company still filed for Chapter 11 in 1998 and was sold off in 1999. The root cause was not cryptographic — it was the architectural dependency §6 warned about: a system requiring one issuer to participate needs that issuer, and only that issuer, to say yes. Getting any bank, let alone enough banks and merchants at once to escape a chicken-and-egg adoption trap, proved to be a business and licensing problem Chaum's company never solved — accounts differ on how much of that was strategic misjudgment versus a genuinely hard market problem, but everyone agrees the blocker was adoption of the central party, not the math underneath it.

Incident 2: the e-gold prosecution (2007–2008)

e-gold, launched in 1996, was a centralized, gold-backed digital currency — architecturally a bank-style ledger (§5), just denominated in grams of gold instead of dollars. In 2007 its operators were indicted for operating an unlicensed money-transmitting business and money laundering; a 2008 guilty plea followed. This is the other side of §1's "identity and recourse": a central operator that can freeze a thief's account can equally be compelled, prosecuted, or shut down wholesale by a government that objects to what it's being used for — because there is exactly one legal entity to serve papers on. A system with no central operator has no such chokepoint to prosecute, which chapters 6–10 will show is a security property with real double-edged consequences of its own.

Incident 3: PayPal's fraud crisis (2000–2001)

PayPal in its first two years absorbed enormous losses to organized credit-card fraud rings — attackers funded accounts with stolen card numbers, moved the money, and left PayPal holding the chargeback. Losses were severe enough that PayPal's leadership later described the fraud as an existential threat to the company, and PayPal ultimately built an internal machine-learning fraud detection system (reported internally as "Igor") to survive it. This is §1's "identity and recourse" pillar made concrete and expensive: providing dispute resolution and reversibility is not a free side effect of running a ledger — it is a standing security and operations cost that a real payment operator must build and continuously fund, precisely because a central ledger makes reversal possible, which means someone has to decide, constantly, when to use that power.

Pythoncentral_ledger.py — the total-order fix from §5
class CentralLedger:
    """One sequencer. Every valid-looking payment must clear here."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.next_seq = 0
        self.spent = set()          # coin_ids already recorded as spent
        self.log = []               # the append-only sequence itself

    def submit(self, payment):
        coin, new_owner, sig = payment
        if not verify_signature(f"{coin.coin_id}:{coin.amount}:{new_owner}", sig, coin.owner_pubkey):
            raise Rejected("bad signature")          # §3's check — still necessary
        if coin.coin_id in self.spent:
            # THIS is the one new line the naive scheme was missing.
            raise Rejected(f"coin {coin.coin_id} already spent at seq {self.find_seq(coin.coin_id)}")
        seq = self.next_seq; self.next_seq += 1
        self.spent.add(coin.coin_id)
        self.log.append((seq, coin.coin_id, new_owner))
        return seq                                       # finality: this seq number is now permanent
Pythonblind_sign.py — Chaum's protocol from §8a, real modexp
# Toy-sized RSA (production: 2048+-bit n). Bank's keypair: (n, e) public, d private.
n, e, d = 3233, 17, 2753

def withdraw(serial_m, r):
    """Alice: blind her serial number so the bank never sees it."""
    blinded = (serial_m * pow(r, e, n)) % n
    return blinded                      # --> sent to the bank

def bank_sign(blinded):
    """Bank: sign the blinded value. It cannot recover serial_m from this."""
    return pow(blinded, d, n)          # --> sent back to Alice

def unblind(signed_blinded, r):
    """Alice: remove the blinding factor. Result is a valid sig on serial_m alone."""
    r_inv = pow(r, -1, n)               # modular inverse, Python 3.8+
    return (signed_blinded * r_inv) % n

def verify_token(serial_m, sig):
    """Merchant (or anyone): confirm the bank really signed serial_m."""
    return pow(sig, e, n) == serial_m

def mint_stamp(challenge: bytes, difficulty_bits: int) -> int:
    """Brute force a nonce whose hash has >= difficulty_bits leading zero bits."""
    nonce = 0
    while True:
        digest = hashlib.sha256(challenge + str(nonce).encode()).digest()
        if leading_zero_bits(digest) >= difficulty_bits:
            return nonce                    # expected cost: ~2**difficulty_bits hashes
        nonce += 1

def verify_stamp(challenge: bytes, nonce: int, difficulty_bits: int) -> bool:
    digest = hashlib.sha256(challenge + str(nonce).encode()).digest()
    return leading_zero_bits(digest) >= difficulty_bits   # cost: exactly 1 hash
WHY THIS FILE LAYOUT, SPECIFICALLY

Three deliberate choices worth naming. First, Python and the standard library only — the point of this chapter is the mechanism, not performance, and pow(base, exp, mod) makes the modular arithmetic in blind signatures legible in one line instead of buried in a bignum library. Second, the RSA modulus is small enough to trace by hand (you could factor 3233 yourself in a minute) — production blind-signature schemes, where anyone still uses them, run on 2048-bit or larger moduli, and nothing about the protocol's logic changes at that size, only the numbers' length. Third, and unlike the RSA toy example, hashcash.py uses the real SHA-256 from Python's standard library, not a shrunk demonstration hash — because unlike modular exponentiation by hand, hashing at full strength costs nothing extra to show correctly, so there's no pedagogical reason to weaken it. The browser widget in §8b uses a faster toy hash purely so grinding stays interactive without freezing your tab; this reference file has no such constraint.
