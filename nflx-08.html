NETFLIX STREAMING INTERNALS · CHAPTER 08

Content Placement &
Predictive Caching

Chapter 7 established that Open Connect Appliances (OCAs) put Netflix's content roughly one network hop from a customer — inside the ISP's own network or at a nearby internet exchange point. But every one of those boxes has finite disk, and Netflix's catalog does not fit on all of them, especially the smaller appliances embedded deep inside an access network. Someone — or something — has to decide, before a single customer clicks play, exactly which slice of the catalog earns a spot on this box's disk tonight. This chapter is about that decision: how it's made, why it has to be made in advance rather than reactively, and what happens to a region's playback economics when the decision is wrong.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

A box with a few terabytes, a catalog measured in petabytes

An Open Connect Appliance is, physically, a server with local disks (or, on newer appliance classes, flash) sitting in a rack inside an ISP's facility or at a peering site. Netflix's public Open Connect hardware documentation describes several appliance classes — compact, ISP-embedded units built around tens of terabytes of storage, up through larger flash- and disk-based appliances at bigger peering and exchange-point sites carrying substantially more. Exact model numbers change as hardware generations turn over and are Netflix's to publish precisely; treat the figures in this chapter as order-of-magnitude, not a spec sheet.

Now put that next to the catalog side of the ledger. Chapters 2 and 5 already did the bitrate math you need: a single title, encoded per-title across a full resolution ladder (roughly 240p up through 4K HDR where licensed), in more than one codec family for device compatibility (an H.264 baseline for universal decode support, plus more efficient VP9/AV1 renditions for newer decoders — chapter 3), with a dozen-plus dubbed audio tracks and subtitle files for a global catalog, can occupy tens of gigabytes of encoded storage once every rendition is counted. Netflix has never published an exact global title count — it varies by country for licensing reasons and isn't the useful number here anyway — but even a deliberately conservative back-of-envelope (a few thousand titles, tens of gigabytes of total encoded footprint each) lands the full encoded catalog in the hundreds-of-terabytes-to-low-petabytes range, before counting trailers, extras, or per-region catalog differences.

Compare that to a single embedded appliance's tens of terabytes and the mismatch is stark: a modest OCA can hold, at best, a low-single-digit percentage of the full encoded catalog's footprint. It is not close, and it was never going to be close — Open Connect's economics (chapter 7) depend on appliances being cheap enough to give away for free and small enough to fit inside an ISP's existing facilities, which puts a hard ceiling on disk that scales far more slowly than the catalog does. The question this chapter answers is not "how do we make the disk bigger" — it's "given a disk that will never hold everything, which slice of the catalog goes on it?"

  • The decision is per-appliance, not global. A single fill decision does not exist — there are thousands of OCA sites worldwide, each with different capacity and, more importantly, a different local audience with different tastes.
  • The decision has a deadline. Whatever gets decided has to be transferred onto the appliance's disk before the next peak-traffic window, or it doesn't help that day at all.
  • Getting it wrong is expensive in a very specific way — not "the movie doesn't play," but "the request has to travel farther than one hop to be served," which is precisely the cost Open Connect exists to eliminate. Every miss un-does a slice of chapter 7's investment.
WHY THIS ISN'T JUST "CACHING"

A textbook cache (an OS page cache, a web CDN edge, a database buffer pool) is filled reactively: something is requested, it's fetched once, and it's kept around under some eviction policy in case it's requested again. That works when the penalty for a miss is small and misses are frequent enough to keep the cache warm from real traffic. Netflix's placement problem inverts both assumptions: the penalty for a miss during peak hours is exactly the expensive long-haul fetch Open Connect was built to avoid, and — as §4 shows with real numbers — waiting for a request to happen before deciding to cache it is often waiting too long. That's what turns this from an eviction-policy question into a forecasting problem.

DEEP CONTEXT · AN OLD PROBLEM IN A NEW COSTUME

Deciding what finite shelf space should hold, before you know exactly who's going to ask for what, is not a new problem — Netflix's own DVD-by-mail-era warehouses had to decide how many copies of a given disc to stock at each regional distribution center, based on demand forecasts derived from historical rental patterns and new-release hype. The medium changed completely — physical discs in warehouses became encoded bits on appliance disks — but the underlying operations-research problem (forecast demand, allocate finite capacity, accept that you'll sometimes be wrong) is structurally the same one this chapter teaches. That continuity is a useful sanity check: you don't need Netflix-specific magic to reason about this problem, you need inventory-forecasting fundamentals applied to a new kind of shelf.

ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: don't predict anything — just cache what gets asked for

The obvious starting point, and a completely reasonable one to reach for first: treat each OCA like any other cache. Start empty. The first time a title is requested and missed, fetch it from a parent OCA or origin, store it locally, and serve it locally next time. When the disk fills up, evict the least-recently-used title to make room for a new one. No forecasting model, no regional demand data, no overnight batch job — just ordinary reactive caching with a standard eviction policy.

pseudocodereactive_fill.py — illustrative strawman, not Netflix source
class ReactiveOCA:
    def __init__(self, capacity_bytes):
        self.capacity = capacity_bytes
        self.used = 0
        self.store = {}           # title_id -> (bytes, last_used_at)

    def handle_request(self, title_id, size_bytes, now):
        if title_id in self.store:
            self.store[title_id][1] = now      # cache HIT — touch recency
            return "served locally"

        # cache MISS — fetch from a parent OCA or origin, then store it
        while self.used + size_bytes > self.capacity:
            evict_least_recently_used(self.store)   # make room
        fetch_from_upstream(title_id)                     # the EXPENSIVE hop this chapter is about
        self.store[title_id] = [size_bytes, now]
        self.used += size_bytes
        return "fetched, then served"

This is not a strawman built to be knocked down for sport — it is a legitimate, simple, self-tuning policy. It requires zero data about the region's viewing habits, zero forecasting infrastructure, and it will, given enough time and a stable content catalog, converge toward keeping genuinely popular titles cached (they get requested often enough to survive LRU eviction) while genuinely unpopular ones age out. For a slowly changing catalog with steady, predictable demand, this could plausibly be good enough. The next section is about exactly where that assumption breaks.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

Every cold miss happens at exactly the wrong time, at Netflix's actual scale

Reactive caching's hidden assumption is that learning by being wrong once is an acceptable cost. Two facts about Netflix's real scale and release pattern break that assumption badly.

Fact 1: releases are synchronized, not staggered

A new season or a big film typically drops at a single global (or single-region) moment — midnight local time, simultaneously for every subscriber in that market. Under pure reactive caching, every OCA serving that region starts that title with zero copies cached, because nothing about a title's popularity can be learned before it exists to be requested. The very first wave of demand — which for a hit title is also the largest wave, concentrated in the first hours after release — is guaranteed to be almost entirely cache misses, at precisely the moment the load on the fallback path (parent OCA, regional cache, or ultimately origin) is highest. A caching policy that only learns from misses learns slowest exactly when the stakes are highest.

Fact 2: the fallback path competes with the very peak traffic it's meant to relieve

Netflix's own reason for building Open Connect in the first place (chapter 7) is that video is such an enormous share of internet traffic that routing it efficiently — one hop, inside the ISP — measurably changes network economics for both Netflix and the ISP. Various independent internet-traffic studies over the years (for example, Sandvine's annual reports on global internet phenomena) have repeatedly found Netflix responsible for a large, double-digit percentage of downstream traffic in North America during peak evening hours; the exact figure moves year to year and by region, but the scale is genuinely enormous. That is precisely the traffic Open Connect exists to keep off of expensive backhaul and peering links. A reactive-only OCA that discovers a hit title's popularity only through live cache misses has to pull that traffic back across those same links, during that same peak window — competing for capacity with the very prime-time streaming load Open Connect was built to absorb locally.

THE NUMBERS — ILLUSTRATIVE, BUT ORDER-OF-MAGNITUDE REAL

Netflix has publicly reported paid membership figures on the order of 300 million and rising worldwide in its recent disclosures — treat the exact current figure as a moving target, not a fixed constant. Take a single mid-size regional OCA serving, at peak, on the order of a few thousand concurrent streams at an average blended bitrate around 5 Mbps: that's roughly 15–25 Gbps of local demand. Now suppose a hit release causes this appliance's effective hit ratio to fall from a healthy ~90% (mature, well-cached content) to ~40% for the first few hours after launch — purely reactive caching's worst case, since the title has no history to have been cached from. Miss traffic that must be fetched upstream jumps from roughly 10% to roughly 60% of total demand — call it a 5–6× increase in expensive backhaul, landing during the single highest-demand hour of the day, on links that were already carrying that appliance's ordinary peak load. That is not a rounding error; it is the kind of spike that turns a routine release into a regional capacity incident.

The fix is not a cleverer eviction policy — LRU, LFU, or any reactive variant all share the same defect: they can only react to demand that has already been observed locally. What's needed is a way to have the right content on disk before the first request ever arrives — which means predicting demand, not measuring it.

ARC 05 · BETTER SOLUTION

Predict, then fill — before anyone presses play

The fix Netflix has publicly discussed on its Open Connect engineering blog is exactly what the failure mode demands: forecast which titles a given region's subscribers are likely to want, generate a ranked fill list sized to each appliance's actual capacity, and push that content onto the appliance during its off-peak hours — commonly overnight, when the appliance's local access link and the ISP's backhaul both have spare capacity that isn't competing with anyone's Tuesday-night viewing. By the next evening's peak, the content a subscriber is statistically likely to request is already sitting on the disk one hop away, and the appliance never has to prove a title is popular by first failing to have it. THE GENERAL TECHNIQUE THIS IS AN INSTANCE OF

Strip away the video specifics and this is demand forecasting applied to finite-capacity allocation — the same structural problem retail inventory planning solves every day: use historical sales (here, historical view counts), known upcoming catalog changes (here, release schedules and marketing spend, which retail calls promotional calendars), and similarity to already-known items (here, "this new title resembles other titles this region already loved" — retail's classic answer to a brand-new SKU with no sales history) to predict demand before it's observed, then allocate limited shelf space — or disk space — against that prediction. Netflix did not invent demand forecasting; it applied an established operations-research technique to a new kind of inventory. Netflix's exact current forecasting model architecture is not public, and this chapter does not pretend otherwise — what follows teaches the general, industry-standard technique this class of system uses.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL COSTS

Prediction is not the same thing as knowing

Forecasting turns a hard-deadline reactive problem into a softer, statistical one — it does not make the problem disappear. Four costs remain, and a serious system has to be built around all four rather than pretend a good-enough model erases them:

  • Forecast error is structural, not a bug. Any model that predicts "how many people in this region will watch this title in the next 24 hours" is estimating a random variable from noisy, partial signals. It will sometimes be meaningfully wrong in both directions — over-provisioning disk for a title that underperforms, and under-provisioning for one that overperforms.
  • Cold start is unavoidable for genuinely new content. A brand-new title, especially an original with no built-in franchise fanbase, has no viewing history of its own to forecast from. The model must fall back to weaker signals — similarity to previously popular titles, genre and cast metadata, pre-release marketing reach — which are real, useful signals but are structurally less reliable than "people actually watched this already."
  • Regional taste genuinely diverges. A single global popularity ranking is the wrong unit for a service operating in dozens of countries with different languages, genres, and cultural preferences — a title that tops the forecast in one region can be a modest performer in another. Every additional region the forecaster must serve independently is another surface where the model can be locally wrong even if it's globally reasonable.
  • A finite disk stays finite even with a perfect forecast. Even a forecaster with zero error cannot fit an entire catalog's demand tail onto a small embedded appliance — it can only make sure the disk holds the highest-value slice available for that capacity. Long-tail requests for niche or older titles will still miss on smaller appliances no matter how good the prediction is; the honest goal is maximizing hit ratio for a given disk budget, not eliminating misses entirely.
THE HONEST FRAME: THIS IS RISK MANAGEMENT, NOT A SOLVED PROBLEM

A mature predictive-fill system doesn't chase a forecast accuracy of 100% — that's not achievable for a genuinely novel title, and chasing it is a waste of engineering effort. It instead treats forecast error as an expected, bounded cost and designs around it: fill lists sized with some margin rather than to the exact predicted demand, near-real-time monitoring of early request patterns so an under-provisioned surprise hit can trigger an emergency top-up fill before the *next* off-peak window rather than waiting a full day, and periodic re-forecasting so yesterday's prediction error doesn't compound across an entire week. §11 walks through exactly this failure pattern end to end.

DEEP CONTEXT · WHY THE FALLBACK PATH STILL HAS TO EXIST

Even a well-run predictive-fill pipeline deliberately keeps a fallback path alive from every OCA back toward a parent cache or origin. That's not a design failure — it's the honest acknowledgment from §6 that a finite disk plus an imperfect forecast will always leave some slice of real demand uncached. The fallback path is what turns an inevitable miss into "slightly slower, from one hop farther away" instead of "content unavailable." A predictive system's job is to make that fallback path rarely used, not to make it disappear.

ARC 08 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

From raw signals to bytes on disk

Two things happen between "we have viewing history" and "the appliance's disk is full of the right titles": (a) the forecast itself gets turned into a concrete, capacity-bounded fill list, and (b) that list has to actually cross the network during a bounded window without disrupting anything else running on that link. The capacity/accuracy tradeoff, in one formula

Here's the load-bearing relationship for this whole chapter, made concrete. Suppose demand for titles in a region roughly follows a Zipf-like curve — a small number of titles account for a disproportionate share of requests, with a long tail of much less popular ones (this is a standard, widely used approximation in the CDN and caching literature for how content popularity distributes; it is a simplifying model, not a Netflix-published exact exponent). If a forecast were perfect, filling the top K titles by true rank would capture whatever fraction of total demand mass those top K titles actually represent. If a forecast were pure noise — no better than picking K titles at random — it would capture, in expectation, only K⁄N of total demand, since every title is equally likely to be picked regardless of its real popularity. A real forecast sits somewhere between those two extremes, and the sliders below compute exactly where:

What actually crosses the wire: a knapsack, not a simple top-K cut

One wrinkle the formula above hides: titles are not uniform size. A per-title-encoded 4K HDR film with a dozen dubbed audio tracks (chapter 5) can be many times larger than a lower-resolution catch-up title. Filling "the top K titles by rank" isn't quite right when capacity is measured in bytes, not title count — the fill-list generator is really solving a knapsack problem: maximize forecast-weighted expected demand captured, subject to a total-bytes capacity constraint. A cheap, effective approximation — greedy selection by predicted-demand-per-byte rather than raw predicted-demand — captures most of the achievable value without needing an exact solver, which matters because this planning step has to run for every appliance, every night, at Open Connect's real global fleet size.

WHY GREEDY-BY-DENSITY IS THE RIGHT ENGINEERING CALL HERE

An exact knapsack solution is NP-hard in general; at fleet scale — thousands of appliances, each re-planned on a nightly cadence, against a catalog with tens of thousands of size/value pairs — an exact solver is not worth its cost. Greedy selection by value-per-byte is a well-known, cheap approximation that is provably within a small, bounded factor of optimal for this class of problem, and it re-plans fast enough to run nightly across an entire fleet without becoming the bottleneck. This is the same "good approximation beats slow exact answer" judgment call this course has made before (per-title encoding's convex-hull search in chapter 5 is a sibling example) — optimality that arrives too late to hit the fill window is worth nothing.

Read this table the same way chapter 1's process-model comparison should be read: there is no dominant strategy, only different placements of the same underlying cost. Pure reactive caching pays its cost as peak-hour cold misses on every new title, forever. Pure predictive fill pays its cost as forecast error on titles it couldn't have known about, bounded to the fraction of demand that's genuinely unpredictable. A hybrid pays engineering and operational complexity to shrink both of the other costs at once — which is why, in practice, systems like this tend to converge on some version of "predict, fill, then allow bounded reactive correction," rather than picking one extreme.

Two scale-relevant conclusions follow directly from this curve's shape, and both hold regardless of the exact Zipf exponent assumed:

  • Diminishing returns on capacity, at any fixed forecast quality. The first slice of disk captures the steep head of the popularity curve; each additional gigabyte buys a smaller marginal hit-ratio gain, because you're moving further into the long tail. This is exactly why smaller embedded appliances aren't a lost cause — a modest disk captures most of the achievable benefit if the forecast ranking is good, because the head of the curve is where most demand actually lives.
  • Forecast accuracy multiplies against total fleet traffic, not against one appliance. A few points of hit-ratio improvement look small on one box's dashboard. Across a fleet of thousands of appliances serving a meaningful share of global internet video traffic, the same percentage-point improvement in forecast quality compounds into a large, real shift in how much traffic has to travel the expensive, farther path back toward origin. Netflix has publicly stated that the large majority of its streaming traffic is served directly from Open Connect appliances rather than transiting back to origin — which is exactly why fleet-wide forecast quality, not any single box's tuning, is the lever that actually moves the company's bandwidth economics.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS

The failure mode every predictive-cache system eventually meets

No specific, publicly detailed Netflix outage matching this exact pattern is documented here with confidence — what follows is the realistic, industry-standard incident pattern for predictive-cache and CDN-fill systems in general, seen across the streaming and content-delivery industry, stated plainly as a generalized pattern rather than a specific dated event. Netflix's own public engineering writing about Open Connect has acknowledged, in general terms, that forecasting demand for new content is inherently imperfect and that a fill system has to be built to tolerate days where actual demand diverges meaningfully from prediction.

The pattern: a surprise breakout title outruns its own forecast

A new title launches with a modest pre-release forecast — nothing in its marketing reach, cast profile, or genre similarity data suggested it would be exceptional, so it's ranked and fill-listed like any other mid-tier release. Then word-of-mouth and social sharing drive demand far beyond what any pre-release signal could have predicted — a genuine organic breakout, the kind of virality that by definition has no reliable leading indicator before it happens. Regional OCAs, especially smaller embedded appliances that had allocated only a proportionally modest slice of their limited capacity to this title based on its forecast rank, start missing on it heavily as real demand climbs. Because the miss happens mid-cycle — after the overnight fill window has already closed for the day — the affected appliances can't simply re-fill until the next off-peak window, hours away. The result: a visible, measurable dip in that region's cache-hit ratio for exactly this title, precisely during the window when its demand is highest, with the excess load falling on the fallback path (parent OCA or origin) that Open Connect exists to avoid using.

The mitigation, in the general case this industry has converged on: real-time monitoring of early request telemetry per title, so a forecast miss shows up as a live hit-ratio anomaly within hours rather than being discovered the next morning in a batch report; a mechanism to trigger an emergency, out-of-cycle fill push for a small number of clearly-underprovisioned titles rather than waiting for the next scheduled window; and, after the fact, feeding the realized (not predicted) demand back into the forecasting model so a genuine breakout's signal — the actual viewing pattern, now known — improves next time a similar title is forecast. None of this prevents the first day's dip; it bounds how long it lasts.

pseudocodepopularity_model.py — per-region demand score, with a cold-start fallback
def forecast_score(title, region):
    if title.has_sufficient_view_history(region):
        # the strong signal: how this title has actually performed so far
        return weighted_recent_views(title, region, half_life_days=10)

    # COLD START: no history yet — this is the honestly weaker case
    similar = find_similar_titles(title, by=["genre", "cast", "franchise"])
    similarity_score = average_performance(similar, region)
    marketing_signal = pre_release_reach_estimate(title, region)
    return (0.6 * similarity_score) + (0.4 * marketing_signal)   # illustrative blend, not a real weighting
pseudocodefill_list_generator.py — greedy knapsack by predicted-demand-per-byte
def build_fill_list(scored_titles, capacity_bytes):
    # sort by VALUE DENSITY, not raw predicted demand — a huge 4K title
    # with modest demand can lose to several smaller titles with the
    # same total predicted demand and a fraction of the disk cost
    ranked = sorted(scored_titles, key=lambda t: t.forecast_score / t.encoded_size_bytes, reverse=True)

    fill_list, used = [], 0
    for title in ranked:
        if used + title.encoded_size_bytes > capacity_bytes:
            continue            # doesn't fit — skip, keep scanning for a smaller fit
        fill_list.append(title)
        used += title.encoded_size_bytes
    return fill_list   # tonight's assignment for exactly ONE appliance
pseudocodeovernight_scheduler.py — throttled delivery inside the off-peak window
def run_fill_window(appliance, fill_list, window_start, window_end):
    budget_bps = available_bandwidth(appliance, window_start)  # spare capacity, not total link speed
    for title in fill_list:
        if now() >= window_end:
            log("window closed — remaining titles roll to tomorrow's plan")
            break
        throttled_transfer(title, appliance, rate_limit=budget_bps)
        # re-check spare capacity periodically: if the ISP's own overnight
        # maintenance traffic or an early-riser demand uptick shows up,
        # the fill must yield bandwidth rather than fight for it
        budget_bps = available_bandwidth(appliance, now())
WHY BUILT THIS WAY — THREE CHOICES WORTH STEALING

1. Value density, not raw value, drives the knapsack. Optimizing for total predicted demand captured without dividing by size systematically over-favors a few giant titles at the expense of many smaller ones that would have captured more total value for the same disk cost — the same mistake a naive shopper makes filling a grocery cart by "biggest item first" instead of "best value per dollar." 2. The fill window yields bandwidth rather than claims it. Fill traffic is explicitly designed to be the lowest-priority consumer of a shared link — it exists specifically to use capacity nobody else needs at that hour, so it must back off the moment something else needs that capacity, not fight for a fixed reservation. 3. An incomplete fill degrades gracefully, it doesn't fail loudly. If the window closes before every title on the list transfers, the lower-priority remainder simply rolls into tomorrow's plan — there's no all-or-nothing transaction here, because a partially filled disk running slightly stale content is a vastly better outcome than delaying the whole night's fill waiting for a perfect run.
