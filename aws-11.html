Messaging & Decoupling: SQS, SNS
& EventBridge

Three services, three genuinely different jobs — and the exam knows exactly how to test whether you actually know which is which. This chapter derives the real decision framework: point-to-point buffering versus pub/sub fan-out versus content-routed event bus, why a single queue cannot broadcast to independent consumers, and how a dead-letter queue keeps one bad message from taking the rest of the system down with it.

ARC 01 · EXAM CONTEXT

Where this chapter sits on the real blueprint

This chapter maps to Design Resilient Architectures on the Associate exam (SAA-C03, 26% of the exam) and Design for New Solutions on the Professional exam (SAP-C02, 29% of the exam). Decoupling is one of the most consistently tested resilience techniques on both exams: a tightly coupled system where one component's slowdown or failure directly stalls another is treated as a design defect, and messaging services are the standard fix.

These three services are also among the exam's favorite "which one, precisely" questions, because their real behaviors are genuinely distinct rather than interchangeable — the exam is testing whether you know the difference between a queue, a pub/sub fan-out, and a content-routed event bus, not whether you've memorized a list of service names.

WHY THIS MATTERS ON TEST DAY

SQS, SNS, and EventBridge solve genuinely different decoupling problems, and the exam rewards knowing which one to reach for cold: point-to-point buffering, one-to-many fan-out, or many-to-many content-based routing. Confuse any two of them and you will reliably miss the "two plausible answers" trap this course's first chapter warned you about.

It also matters because decoupling shows up as a supporting justification in questions that aren't primarily about messaging at all — a scenario about a spiky-traffic web tier, a scenario about a multi-region failover design, even a scenario about cost optimization, will often quietly depend on "and the two tiers are decoupled through a queue" as one clause of the correct answer. Recognizing that clause quickly, rather than treating messaging as an isolated topic confined to this chapter, is worth real points across the rest of both exams.

ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"One SQS queue can broadcast to everyone who needs it"

Here is the instinct: a scenario describes an order-placed event that needs to reach three independent systems — fulfillment, analytics, and a customer-notification service. The tempting design puts a single SQS queue between the producer and all three "consumers," on the assumption that each system will simply read the messages it cares about.

A second, equally common wrong instinct: whenever a scenario mentions "decoupling" at all, candidates default to reaching for SNS, treating it as the universal decoupling hammer — even in scenarios that actually describe a single producer and a single logical consumer needing buffering and load-leveling, which is squarely an SQS problem, not a pub/sub one.

WHY IT'S TEMPTING

"Just add a queue" is genuinely good general advice for decoupling — it's the specific shape that's wrong. A queue reads naturally as "a buffer everyone can pull from," which is exactly backwards from how SQS actually behaves once more than one consumer is involved.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

SQS is point-to-point, not broadcast

An Amazon SQS queue implements a competing consumers pattern: each message is delivered to, processed by, and deleted by exactly one consumer. If fulfillment, analytics, and notifications all poll the same queue, each message goes to whichever one of the three happens to receive it first — the other two never see it at all. This isn't a misconfiguration to fix; it's the fundamental contract SQS makes. A single queue can scale out identical workers processing the same kind of work faster, but it cannot deliver one message to three different, independent consumer systems.

THE FIX, STATED PRECISELY

To get the same event to three independent systems, you need three independent, durable subscriptions — and that is exactly what an SNS topic fanning out to three separate SQS queues (one per consumer system) gives you: SNS publishes once, and each subscribed SQS queue receives its own full copy, buffered and independently consumed at whatever pace that system can manage. This fan-out pattern is one of the single most-tested architectures across both exams.

The second wrong instinct — reaching for SNS whenever "decoupling" appears — fails for a quieter reason: SNS has no buffering of its own for a given subscriber beyond its own limited retry policy, and no polling model; if a subscriber's endpoint is down, SNS retries on a schedule and then gives up (optionally to a dead-letter queue attached to the subscription), whereas SQS gives a consumer complete control over its own pace, including being offline for a while and catching up later. A single producer feeding a single logical consumer that just needs buffering and load-leveling is an SQS problem, full stop — reaching for pub/sub there adds complexity with no corresponding benefit.

ARC 04 · THE CORRECT APPROACH

Deriving the right service from the shape of the decoupling need

Walk through the decision logic the exam actually expects, one step at a time.

Choosing between SQS, SNS, and EventBridgeSTEP-BY-STEP
THE ONE-LINE VERSION

One producer, one logical consumer, needs buffering/load-leveling → SQS (Standard for throughput, FIFO for strict per-group ordering and exactly-once processing). One event, several independent consumer systems that all need their own copy → SNS fanning out to multiple SQS queues. Many event types from many producers (including third-party SaaS) that need to be routed to many different targets based on the event's own content → EventBridge.

If you've used Kafka before, EventBridge's rule-based routing across many independent producers and consumers will feel conceptually familiar — though the mechanics underneath (a managed rules engine over discrete events, not a partitioned replicated log) are entirely different, and that comparison stops there for this course.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

What the correct answer still has to respect

  • Message/event size ceilings are shared across all three. SQS, SNS, and EventBridge each cap a standard message/event around 256 KB. For SQS, the Amazon SQS Extended Client Library is the standard answer whenever a scenario needs to pass a larger payload — it stores the body in S3 and passes a reference through the queue.
  • At-least-once delivery means your consumer must be idempotent. A Standard SQS queue can, in rare circumstances, deliver the same message more than once, and a consumer that isn't safe to run twice on the same message is a design bug waiting to be tested by a scenario question.
  • Visibility timeout is a promise, not a guarantee, if your consumer is too slow. If a consumer takes longer to process a message than the queue's visibility timeout, the message becomes visible again and a second consumer may pick it up — the fix is either raising the visibility timeout to comfortably exceed the worst-case processing time, or extending it dynamically mid-processing.
  • Dead-letter queues are the standard answer for a message that keeps failing. A redrive policy's maxReceiveCount defines how many delivery attempts are allowed before a message is moved to a configured DLQ — this is what stops one poison-pill message from blocking or endlessly recycling through a queue.
  • FIFO trades some throughput ceiling for ordering and exactly-once processing. A FIFO queue guarantees strict order and exactly-once processing within a single message group, and removes the occasional-duplicate/best-effort-order behavior of Standard queues — in exchange, its overall throughput ceiling is lower than a Standard queue's, though batching narrows that gap considerably. The exam's signal is the requirement itself ("strict order," "exactly once," "no duplicates") rather than a specific number.
  • SNS subscription filter policies matter more than most candidates expect. Without a filter policy, every subscriber on a topic receives every message published to it; a filter policy lets a subscriber declare which message attributes it actually wants, which is often the real fix for "too much irrelevant traffic hitting this consumer" rather than splitting into more topics.
  • EventBridge rules match near real-time, not instantaneously, and buses can be default, custom, or partner. The default event bus receives AWS service events automatically; a custom bus is for your own application events; a partner event bus is how a supported third-party SaaS vendor's events land in your account for content-based routing alongside your own.
  • SNS FIFO topics pair with FIFO SQS queues to preserve ordering across the fan-out hop. Strict ordering only holds end-to-end if every hop in the chain is ordering-aware — the exam occasionally tests whether you know a Standard SNS topic feeding a FIFO queue silently breaks that guarantee.
  • EventBridge Pipes and EventBridge Scheduler are close cousins, not substitutes for rule-based routing. Pipes connects one source to one target with optional filtering/enrichment — a narrower point-to-point integration tool — and Scheduler handles one-time or recurring invocations. Neither replaces EventBridge's own rule engine, and the exam expects you to recognize each by its narrower job rather than treat all three as interchangeable names for "EventBridge."
A CLASSIC COMBINED TRAP

A scenario says a single failing order (out of millions) should never block processing of the rest, and that the team must be able to inspect exactly which orders failed and why. The trap answer deletes failed messages to "keep the queue moving." The correct answer: a redrive policy with a sensible maxReceiveCount routing failures to a DLQ — nothing is silently lost, and the DLQ becomes the inspection point.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three original vignettes, written the way the real exam phrases them

These are original scenarios written for this course to illustrate the exam's phrasing style — they are not drawn from, and do not claim to reproduce, any real exam question.

Case 1 — the retailer with three independent downstream systems

A retailer's order service needs the same "order placed" event to independently trigger fulfillment, analytics, and customer-notification processing, and any one system being slow or briefly down must never affect the other two. Correct approach: an SNS topic fanning out to three separate SQS queues, one per consumer system, each with its own DLQ — exactly the reference architecture in §7.

Case 2 — the fintech company with strict per-account transaction ordering

A financial platform must process transactions for a given account in the exact order they were submitted, with a hard requirement that no transaction is ever processed twice. Correct approach: an SQS FIFO queue using the account ID as the message group ID — this guarantees strict order and exactly-once processing within each account's group, while different accounts' transactions can still be processed in parallel across groups.

Case 3 — the logistics platform integrating a third-party shipping partner

A logistics company receives shipment-status events from its own services and from a third-party shipping partner's SaaS platform, and needs to route different event types to different targets (a Lambda function, a Step Functions workflow, and a partner analytics system) based on the event's own content — with the number of producers and consumers expected to keep growing. Correct approach: Amazon EventBridge, using a partner event bus for the third-party events and content-based rules to route by event detail — this is precisely the many-producer, many-consumer, content-routed scenario SNS is not built to scale into.

Case 4 — the SaaS company debating SNS vs. a fourth SQS queue for a new team

A growing SaaS company already fans an "order placed" event out through SNS to three SQS queues. A fourth team (fraud detection) now wants the same event, but only for orders over a certain value. Correct approach: add a fourth SQS queue subscribed to the same SNS topic, with a subscription filter policy limiting it to messages whose value attribute exceeds the threshold — no changes to the producer, the topic, or the other three queues are required, which is precisely the operational benefit this decoupled shape is supposed to deliver.

ARC 16 · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

What to be sure of before you move on

Four facts carry most of this chapter's exam weight. SQS is point-to-point — one message, one consumer, competing-consumers style — which is exactly why it cannot broadcast to independent consumer systems on its own. SNS fanning out to multiple SQS queues is the standard, heavily-tested pattern for delivering one event to several independent systems, each buffered and consumed at its own pace. EventBridge is the answer once the scenario is about routing many event types from many producers (including third-party SaaS) to many targets by content, not just broadcasting one event type to a fixed set of subscribers. And a dead-letter queue with a redrive policy is the standard, explicit failure-handling primitive whenever a scenario says a message must never be silently lost or allowed to block everything behind it.

Keep one more thing in view heading into the rest of this course: almost every resilient architecture you'll design from here on has a messaging layer quietly doing the decoupling work underneath it, even in chapters that aren't nominally "about" SQS, SNS, or EventBridge. Get comfortable naming the right one on sight, because you will be asked to, directly or indirectly, on both exams.
