Associate-level cost questions ask "which purchasing option is cheapest." Where this sits on the blueprint

ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"Each team should buy its own Reserved Instances / Savings Plans"

Here is the trap: it feels organizationally clean for each team's account to own its own commitment-based discount — that team pays for what it commits to, full accountability, no shared complexity. Candidates extend this instinct to rightsizing too: "just have each team eyeball their peak CPU/memory graphs and resize accordingly," and to tagging: "we'll add cost allocation tags once we notice untagged spend in a report."

THE INSTINCT, STATED PLAINLY

"Commitment discounts are cleanest when each account manages its own." "Rightsizing is a judgment call based on watching dashboards." "We'll tag resources for cost allocation after the fact, once we see a report that needs it." All three feel organizationally tidy — and all three leave real, quantifiable savings and accuracy on the table that a Professional-level answer is expected to catch.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

Fragmented commitments leave utilization gaps; retroactive tags leave permanent report holes

Per-account commitment purchasing fails for a concrete, quantifiable reason: usage doesn't split evenly across teams, and a commitment is only "used" (and therefore only saving you money) during the hours your actual usage in that commitment's scope meets or exceeds it. If Team A's account is quiet on weekends and Team B's account is busy exactly then, each holding its own separate commitment means Team A's unused capacity and Team B's overflow to On-Demand can't offset each other — the discount is scoped per account, per commitment. An organization-level (management-account) Savings Plan commitment, by contrast, can cover eligible usage across every linked account automatically — Team A's quiet weekend and Team B's overflow net out against the same shared commitment, achieving materially higher coverage for the same total dollar commitment.

The manual-rightsizing instinct fails because "eyeballing a CPU graph" ignores memory, network, and I/O utilization simultaneously, and ignores percentile behavior over time — a workload that peaks at 90% CPU for five minutes a day looks completely different from one that sits there constantly, and a human skimming a chart will typically pick the safer (i.e., more expensive) reading. Compute Optimizer computes its recommendations from actual CloudWatch utilization history across multiple metrics and gives you a specific, data-backed target instance type or size — removing the guesswork, and removing the bias toward over-provisioning "just in case" that occurs when a human is making the call from a glance at a graph.

The retroactive-tagging instinct fails for a mechanical reason, not a philosophical one: cost allocation tags must be activated in the Billing and Cost Management console before their spend gets reported by tag, and once activated, tags apply to cost data going forward, not retroactively. A team that waits until they need a report to activate tagging has already permanently lost the ability to attribute the spend that happened before activation — there's no way to backfill it after the fact.

THE MECHANISM, PRECISELY

An AWS Organizations management account can purchase a Savings Plan whose commitment is evaluated against the combined, eligible usage of every linked account in the organization by default (this sharing behavior can be scoped down, but the default is organization-wide sharing) — meaning the "which account gets the discount first" allocation happens automatically across the whole org's usage each billing hour, not per-account in isolation. This single mechanism is the real answer to "how do we get commitment discounts without locking every team into their own plan."

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

Where the correct approach still has sharp edges

  • Cost allocation tags are not retroactive. Activating a tag in Billing and Cost Management makes it appear in cost reports from that point forward — spend before activation cannot be attributed by that tag after the fact.
  • AWS Organizations has a default limit on the number of accounts in an organization (a modest default, commonly cited as 10, that is a soft/adjustable service quota AWS will raise on request as an organization grows) — a scenario describing "we're standing up dozens of new accounts and hitting an account-creation ceiling" is describing this adjustable quota, not a hard architectural wall.
  • Savings Plans are a commitment to spend a fixed dollar amount per hour, not a commitment to a specific instance type or family the way a standard Reserved Instance can be — this flexibility is exactly why Compute/EC2 Instance Savings Plans are easier to apply org-wide than instance-specific RIs.
  • Compute Optimizer needs enough utilization history (typically at least 14 days, more for higher confidence recommendations) before it can generate a reliable recommendation — a freshly launched workload won't have meaningful findings yet.
  • Cost Anomaly Detection needs a baseline of historical spend to learn "normal" before it can flag deviations confidently — and a genuinely planned, expected spend increase (a deliberate scale-up) can still trigger a flagged anomaly if it deviates sharply from the learned pattern; this is a feature, not a bug, but the exam expects you to know anomaly alerts are not automatically "wrong" just because the spend was intentional.
  • Tag activation and tag policies are two different things. Activating a cost allocation tag makes it usable in cost reports; an AWS Organizations tag policy enforces that resources are tagged consistently (e.g., a required cost-center key) — a scenario about "preventing untagged resources from being created" is describing tag policies, not just activation.
EDGE CASE THE EXAM LOVES

A company activates cost allocation tags and is confused why last quarter's spend still shows as "untagged" in this month's report. The correct diagnosis: tag activation is not retroactive — that spend happened before activation and will permanently show as untagged in reports, regardless of how the resources are tagged today. The only fix going forward is ensuring tagging (and activation) happens as early as possible for future spend.

ARC 09 · DECISION FRAMEWORK

Use X when / use Y when — the single most exam-relevant table in this chapter

DECISIONUSE THIS WHENNOT WHEN
Org-wide Savings PlanMultiple linked accounts have variable, offsetting usage patterns and you want one commitment to cover eligible usage across all of them automatically. A single account's usage is stable and predictable enough that a dedicated plan already achieves near-full coverage on its own — added org-wide complexity buys little extra.
Reserved InstancesYou need instance-family/Region-scoped capacity reservation guarantees alongside a discount (e.g., capacity reservation in a constrained Region), not just a bill discount. You just want billing flexibility with no need for a capacity reservation — a Savings Plan's flexibility across instance families/sizes is usually the simpler answer.
Spot InstancesThe workload tolerates interruption (batch processing, stateless horizontally scaled tiers, CI runners) in exchange for the deepest discount tier. The workload cannot tolerate a 2-minute-notice interruption — Spot is disqualified regardless of the discount's size.
Compute OptimizerYou need data-driven rightsizing recommendations across many resources, backed by actual utilization history rather than a guess. A resource is brand new with insufficient utilization history for a confident recommendation yet.
Cost Anomaly DetectionYou need automatic detection of an unexpected spend deviation from a learned baseline, without waiting for a fixed threshold like Budgets requires. You already know the exact dollar threshold that should trigger an alert regardless of historical pattern — that's a Budgets alert's job specifically.
Cost allocation tags + tag policiesYou need to attribute spend to teams/cost-centers going forward and enforce that new resources are tagged consistently org-wide. You need to attribute spend that already happened before tags were activated — that data is permanently unattributed by tag.
ARC 10 · LIMITS & SCALING NUMBERS

Organization growth vs. commitment fragmentation risk, charted

AWS Organizations enforces a real, documented default quota on the number of accounts an organization can contain — a genuinely adjustable service quota (check current values in the console; AWS raises it on request as organizations grow), commonly cited around 10 by default. Paired with that quota, here's the real, stable relationship this chapter's decision hinges on: as linked-account count grows, per-account commitment fragmentation risk compounds, while an org-wide commitment's coverage advantage grows with it.

Linked accounts vs. commitment coverage gapCANVAS CHART
━ per-account commitments — coverage gap from unoffset usage variance ━ org-wide commitment — coverage gap shrinks as usage variance nets out

The shape is the lesson: with one account there is no difference between the two approaches (nothing to net out against). As linked-account count grows, per-account commitments leave a widening coverage gap because each account's idle capacity and overflow usage can never offset another account's — while an org-wide commitment's shared pool increasingly nets variable usage patterns against each other, narrowing the gap. This is why AWS Organizations' default account quota (adjustable, but real) matters here: the bigger the org gets, the more this specific architectural decision is worth getting right.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three constructions in the exam's actual voice

Original scenario constructions written for this course — not real exam content, and not claimed to be.

Case 1: The conglomerate with 30 separately billed subsidiaries

A holding company's 30 subsidiaries each run their own AWS account, each buying its own Reserved Instances, and finance suspects they're collectively overpaying despite each subsidiary "doing the right thing" locally. The correct answer: consolidate under AWS Organizations with consolidated billing, and replace the fragmented per-account RIs with a single management-account-level Savings Plan commitment sized to the organization's aggregate, offsetting usage — capturing coverage that no subsidiary's isolated commitment could reach on its own.

Case 2: The Lambda concurrency loop that blindsided finance

A SaaS company's monthly bill spiked sharply because a misconfigured Lambda function recursively invoked itself under specific error conditions, running for days before anyone in finance noticed at month-end billing review. The correct answer: Cost Anomaly Detection monitors configured per service/account, which would have flagged the deviation from the learned baseline within hours rather than at the next invoice — a fixed Budgets threshold alert might also have caught it eventually, but only if someone had already guessed the right dollar threshold in advance; anomaly detection needs no such guess.

Case 3: The chargeback dispute between dev, staging, and prod

An engineering org wants to charge each product team for its actual dev/staging/prod usage but currently has no reliable way to attribute shared-account spend, and teams are disputing each other's estimates. The correct answer: a consistent cost allocation tagging strategy (team, environment, cost-center keys), activated in Billing and Cost Management as early as possible, enforced going forward via an Organizations tag policy — with the explicit caveat to leadership that historical, pre-activation spend cannot be retroactively attributed and any chargeback model for past months will need an separately agreed estimation approach.

ARC 13 · CLI / CODE REFERENCE

Illustrative CLI & policy syntax

Realistic, illustrative AWS CLI v2 and JSON syntax — the shapes you should recognize on sight, not copy verbatim into production without review.

cost-governance/
├── tag-policy.json — Organizations tag policy enforcing a required cost-center key
├── create-anomaly-monitor.sh — aws ce create-anomaly-monitor + create-anomaly-subscription
├── get-rightsizing.sh — aws compute-optimizer get-ec2-instance-recommendations
└── describe-savings-plans.sh — aws savingsplans describe-savings-plans
JSONtag-policy.json — required cost-center tag (illustrative)
{
  "tags": {
    "cost-center": {
      "tag_key": { "@@assign": "cost-center" },
      "enforced_for": {
        "@@assign": [ "ec2:instance", "s3:bucket", "rds:db" ]
      }
    }
  }
}
/* attached to the Organizations root or an OU — new resources of the enforced
   types must carry this tag key going forward; it does not retag existing resources */
bashcreate-anomaly-monitor.sh — illustrative example syntax
# monitor per AWS service across the linked account(s) in scope
aws ce create-anomaly-monitor \
  --anomaly-monitor '{"MonitorName":"prod-service-monitor","MonitorType":"DIMENSIONAL","MonitorDimension":"SERVICE"}'

# subscribe an SNS topic to alerts above a defined impact threshold
aws ce create-anomaly-subscription \
  --anomaly-subscription '{"SubscriptionName":"finance-alerts","MonitorArnList":["arn:aws:ce::111111111111:anomalymonitor/abc123"],"Subscribers":[{"Address":"arn:aws:sns:us-east-1:111111111111:cost-anomalies","Type":"SNS"}],"Threshold":100,"Frequency":"DAILY"}'
bashget-rightsizing.sh — Compute Optimizer (illustrative)
# requires Compute Optimizer to be enrolled and enough utilization history
aws compute-optimizer get-ec2-instance-recommendations \
  --account-ids 222222222222 \
  --filters name=Finding,values=Overprovisioned
WHY THE EXAM CARES ABOUT THIS SYNTAX

You will not be asked to author these documents from scratch, but you will be shown a tag policy fragment and asked "will this retag our existing 500 EC2 instances" (no — it only enforces the key going forward on new/modified resources within its scope) or shown an anomaly subscription and asked what triggers it (a learned deviation, not a fixed dollar threshold) — recognizing the mechanism behind the syntax is the tested skill.

GATE · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

Before chapter 22: what to actually remember

If you remember nothing else from this chapter: (1) a management-account Savings Plan commitment can cover eligible usage across every linked account, netting out offsetting usage patterns that fragmented per-account commitments never could; (2) cost allocation tags apply going forward only — activate them as early as possible, because pre-activation spend is permanently unattributed by tag; (3) Compute Optimizer's recommendations are data-backed from real utilization history, not intuition, and need enough history to be confident; (4) Cost Anomaly Detection catches deviations from a learned baseline automatically, which is meaningfully different from a fixed Budgets threshold you'd have to guess in advance; and (5) AWS Organizations' account-count limit is a real but adjustable default quota, not a hard ceiling.
