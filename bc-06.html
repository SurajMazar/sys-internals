BLOCKCHAIN FUNDAMENTALS · CHAPTER 06

Distributed Consensus:
FLP, CAP & Byzantine Faults

Chapter 5 gave you a data structure — a hash-linked, tamper-evident ledger — that one machine can maintain trivially. This chapter asks the question that makes a ledger into a distributed system: how do several machines, some of which may lie, agree on what that ledger says next? We derive the answer from first principles — formally, with the actual impossibility proofs and the actual bound — rather than asserting it. Everything here predates blockchain by decades; Nakamoto consensus (chapter 8) is a specific, clever answer to a problem this chapter states precisely.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

State it precisely before you try to solve it

"Consensus" is one of the most overloaded words in distributed systems. Before writing a line of protocol, fix the definition every result in this chapter refers back to. A consensus protocol runs on n nodes. Each node i starts with a proposed value vi and must, through message exchange alone, reach a decide(v) that satisfies three properties:

  • Agreement (safety). No two correct (non-faulty) nodes decide different values.
  • Validity (safety). If every correct node proposed the same value v, the decided value is v — a protocol that always decides "0" trivially satisfies Agreement but is useless, so Validity rules that out.
  • Termination (liveness). Every correct node eventually decides, in a finite number of steps.

Notice the shape: two properties are about safety — "nothing bad ever happens" — and one is about liveness — "something good eventually happens." That split matters more than it looks: §6 shows a famous result that cannot be fixed by trying harder, and it is a liveness result, not a safety one. A protocol can be perfectly safe forever by simply never deciding; the hard part is deciding correctly and promptly.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE NEXT 8 CHAPTERS

Every mechanism from here to chapter 14 — proof-of-work, proof-of-stake, fork-choice rules, finality gadgets — is an answer to "how do we get Agreement, Validity, and Termination out of nodes that might be down, might be slow, or might be actively lying, without a trusted referee?" The fault model you assume changes the answer completely, which is why §1 has to be about fault models before it can be about algorithms.

Three fault models, not one

"A node can fail" hides three very different threat models, and conflating them is the single most common mistake in this area. Rank them by how much damage one faulty node can do:

  • Crash-stop. A faulty node simply stops — no more messages, ever. It never sends a wrong message. This is the model Paxos and Raft assume (§9). It matches hardware failure, process kills, and clean network partitions where the partitioned side is treated as dead.
  • Omission. A faulty node may fail to send or receive some messages (dropped packets, a stalled NIC) but never fabricates content. Strictly harder than crash-stop, strictly easier than what follows.
  • Byzantine (arbitrary/malicious). A faulty node may do anything: stay silent, send garbage, send different, mutually contradictory messages to different peers (called equivocation), collude with other faulty nodes, or perfectly mimic a correct node until the exact moment it is most damaging to deviate. Named for Lamport, Shostak & Pease's 1982 paper (§2), which used treacherous Byzantine generals as the storytelling device for "a component that actively works against you."
WHY BYZANTINE IS THE RIGHT MODEL HERE — NOT PARANOIA, ECONOMICS

Distributed-systems folklore treats Byzantine fault tolerance as overkill for anything but spacecraft and nuclear launch systems — and for a cluster you administer, where every operator is your employee and has no incentive to attack you, crash-stop is usually the honest model (that is exactly why Chapter 9's Paxos/Raft baseline exists). A blockchain's validators are a different population: anonymous or semi-anonymous parties with a direct financial stake in the outcome of the vote they are casting. A miner who can double-spend by equivocating gains real money by doing so; a validator who can convince half the network of one block and half of another can profit from both. Once "correct behavior" and "profitable behavior" can diverge, you must assume a participant will deviate the moment deviation pays — which is definitionally the Byzantine model, not crash-stop. This is a first-principles argument, not a slogan: the fault model should match the incentives of the population, and open, value-bearing networks have adversarial incentives baked in from day one.

Hold two numbers in your head for the rest of this chapter: n, the number of participants, and f, the maximum number that may be simultaneously Byzantine. Nearly everything that follows — the bound in §5, the message complexity in §8, the CAP tradeoff in §8c — is a function of the relationship between these two numbers, and of one more assumption we haven't named yet: whether the network delivers messages promptly (synchronous) or with unbounded, unpredictable delay (asynchronous). That axis turns out to matter as much as the fault model itself — it is the subject of §6.

DEEP CONTEXT · WHY "GENERALS," WHY A STORY AT ALL

Lamport later admitted the byzantine-generals framing was chosen deliberately to make an abstract impossibility result vivid and hard to misquote — early drafts used anonymous "Albanian generals," changed after (accurate) complaints, before settling on the fictional and inoffensive "Byzantine" empire, chosen precisely because no modern nation could take offense. The dramatization stuck because it is the right dramatization: a general who might be a traitor, coordinating an attack by messenger with no way to verify anything except by cross-checking stories, is the Byzantine fault-tolerance problem, message for message.

ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: broadcast, collect, take the majority

Derive it honestly, the way an engineer who has never heard of Byzantine faults would. n nodes need to agree on one of two values. The obvious algorithm: everyone broadcasts their proposal to everyone, everyone waits to hear from everyone else, and everyone independently computes the majority of what they received.

pythonnaive_consensus.py — the strawman, annotated
def naive_consensus(my_id, my_value, peers, network):
    # Step 1: tell everyone what I propose.
    for p in peers:
        network.send(p, {"from": my_id, "value": my_value})

    # Step 2: wait for a message from EVERY other node.
    received = {my_id: my_value}
    while len(received) < len(peers) + 1:
        msg = network.recv()          # ← blocks. what if it never arrives?
        received[msg["from"]] = msg["value"]

    # Step 3: decide the majority value among everything received.
    return majority(received.values())

This is not a joke architecture — three real, still-used systems are built on almost exactly this idea when the environment is trusted and synchronous: leader-less gossip protocols in a private data center, N-of-N commit protocols behind a single administrative boundary, and (with a twist you'll meet in §9) two-phase commit. It gets the definitions from §1 right in the one setting where every node is honest and every message arrives: Agreement holds trivially (everyone computed the majority of the identical multiset), Validity holds trivially, and Termination holds as soon as the slowest message arrives.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

One crash hangs it forever; one liar breaks it silently

Two independent failures, and it's important to see they are different kinds of failure.

The crash-fault failure is a liveness bug, and it is trivial to see. Step 2 of naive_consensus waits for exactly n messages. If even one node has crashed, that while loop never exits — every correct node blocks forever waiting for a message that will never come. Termination is violated by a single crash, full stop. The obvious patch — "wait for any n−1 of n, not all n" — looks like it fixes this, and for pure crash faults (with a synchronous network) it does. It does not fix the second failure.

The Byzantine failure is a safety bug, and it requires the concrete argument below to see why no patch of this shape can fix it. This is the original 1982 Byzantine Generals scenario, at its minimum size: n = 3, f = 1 — a commanding general and two lieutenants, one of the three a traitor, communicating only by messenger.

The 3-general impossibility, concretelySTEP-BY-STEP
THE ARGUMENT IN ONE PARAGRAPH, FOR WHEN YOU NEED TO REPRODUCE IT ON A WHITEBOARD

Lieutenant B receives "attack" directly from the Commander, and receives "the Commander told me retreat" relayed from Lieutenant C. B cannot tell whether the Commander lied to C, or C is lying about what the Commander said — both scenarios produce an identical message pattern at B. Any deterministic decision rule B applies must therefore give the same output in both scenarios. Now construct a second scenario where the Commander is actually honest and truly said "attack" to both lieutenants, but C is the traitor and falsely reports "retreat" to B anyway — B's local view is exactly the same as before, so B must reach the same decision. But Validity demands that when the Commander is honest, B obeys the Commander's true order — "attack." Running the mirror argument for C forces C toward "retreat" in the very same original scenario. Two loyal officers, two different decisions: Agreement fails, and no algorithm, however clever, escapes this at n=3, f=1 — the argument never used any property of a specific algorithm, only of the information available to B.

Quantify what just happened: with 3 nodes and 1 traitor, the two honest nodes only have 2 data points each (one direct message, one relayed message) to decide between two indistinguishable worlds. There simply is not enough independent information in the system for a majority vote — of anything — to be trustworthy. §5 asks how many honest data points would be enough, and derives the answer instead of quoting it.

ARC 05 · BETTER SOLUTION

Deriving 3f+1: how many honest votes make a lie outvoted?

Generalize the 3-general argument instead of special-casing it. Suppose n generals, up to f traitors, and every general collects reports (direct and relayed) about what every other general said, then decides by majority over whatever it collected. For that majority to be trustworthy, two disjoint quorums of reporters must each be guaranteed to contain at least one honest voice — otherwise an adversary controlling exactly the overlap can show one quorum "attack" and the other "retreat," and no outside observer can tell which quorum was lied to.

THE RESULT — LAMPORT, SHOSTAK & PEASE, 1982

Interactive Consistency (their term for Agreement + Validity together) is achievable with oral messages (unsigned, but a recipient can identify who sent a message) if and only if n ≥ 3f + 1, i.e. strictly more than two-thirds of all participants must be honest. Their constructive algorithm achieving this bound exactly — the recursive Oral Messages, OM(f) protocol — is what §8 implements byte-for-byte, and what §15's simulator runs live. Note what changes if messages are digitally signed (so a traitor cannot forge another node's signature, only refuse to relay it): the bound relaxes to n ≥ 2f + 1, because forgery — not just lying about content — is now cryptographically impossible; this is the "Signed Messages" variant in the same 1982 paper, and it's the version every modern BFT protocol (§8, PBFT, Tendermint) actually deploys, which is why you'll see 2f+1 quorums quoted for PBFT even though the node count requirement is still n ≥ 3f+1 — the quorum size and the population size are different numbers answering different questions, and conflating them is a common interview mistake.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL HURTS

3f+1 nodes fix Agreement. They do not fix Termination.

§5 solved safety. It bought nothing on liveness — and liveness turns out to have a ceiling that no amount of extra replication, extra signatures, or extra cleverness can remove, as long as you insist on two specific assumptions: the protocol must be deterministic, and the network must be fully asynchronous (messages are eventually delivered, but with no bound on delay — the network can reorder and stall a message for an adversarially chosen, arbitrarily long time, indistinguishable from a very slow link).

FLP IMPOSSIBILITY — FISCHER, LYNCH & PATERSON, 1985

In an asynchronous system, no deterministic consensus protocol can guarantee Termination if even a single process may crash. Not "may be Byzantine" — the result is proven for the strictly weaker crash-stop model, which makes it strictly stronger: if you can't solve consensus reliably with mere crashes and no bad actors, you certainly can't with liars. The 1985 Fischer–Lynch–Paterson paper (Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process, JACM) is one of the most cited results in computer science precisely because it is a wall, not a gap — a genuine theorem, not an engineering shortfall waiting for a smarter protocol.

The proof sketch is worth internalizing because the intuition transfers directly to how real systems are built around it. Consider the set of all possible global states the system could be in partway through a run. Call a state bivalent if both a decision of 0 and a decision of 1 are still reachable from it, and univalent if only one outcome remains possible. FLP shows: (1) some initial configuration is bivalent — because inputs can legitimately differ, the system can't know the "right" answer on step one; and (2) from any bivalent configuration, an adversarial scheduler can always choose which message to deliver next so that the system stays bivalent — it can always find one more delay that keeps both outcomes alive. Chain that second fact forever and you get an infinite run that never commits to a value: safe (nothing wrong is ever decided), but live forever without deciding — a valid execution that violates Termination.

Precision matters here, because FLP is the most misquoted theorem in distributed systems. It does not say:

  • "Consensus is impossible" — plainly false; Paxos, Raft, PBFT and Bitcoin all run in production today.
  • "Byzantine fault tolerance is impossible" — FLP is about the weaker crash-fault model and about asynchrony, not about the fault type.
  • "A specific protocol will hang on your network tomorrow" — FLP proves a worst-case adversarial schedule exists; it says nothing about the probability of hitting it under normal conditions, which in practice is effectively zero.

It says exactly this: no deterministic protocol can promise, for every possible execution, that it will terminate in bounded time, while remaining correct in every execution. Something has to give — and real systems give up one of the theorem's three load-bearing assumptions (determinism, full asynchrony, or "must terminate in every execution") rather than give up on consensus altogether. §7 names the three escapes.

THE HONEST SUMMARY

None of these hatches make FLP false — they make it inapplicable, by changing the model it was proven about. Partial synchrony protocols (PBFT, Tendermint) remain always safe even during full asynchrony; they simply stop making progress until the network cooperates — a bounded-liveness escape, not a full one. Randomized protocols (Ben-Or, and — arguably — Nakamoto's mining lottery) guarantee termination with probability 1 rather than in every execution, converting a hard impossibility into a vanishingly small tail risk. Failure detectors convert "no information about crashes" into "eventually accurate suspicion," which is exactly enough extra power to route around the adversarial scheduler FLP relies on. Every consensus protocol you will ever operate is honestly described by picking one of these three rows — and by what it gave up to sit in it.

ARC 08 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

The actual mechanisms: quorum math, PBFT's three phases, and CAP applied to ledgers

Three pieces, each load-bearing: (a) the quorum-intersection arithmetic that makes 3f+1 concrete for any n and f you choose, (b) PBFT's pre-prepare/prepare/commit state machine — the canonical partial-synchrony BFT protocol, byte-for-byte, (c) CAP and PACELC mapped onto real chains' behavior during a network split.

(a) Quorum intersection, made concrete

A Byzantine quorum system needs any two quorums to intersect in at least one honest node. With signed messages, quorums of size 2f+1 out of n = 3f+1 guarantee this: any two such quorums overlap in at least (2f+1) + (2f+1) − (3f+1) = f+1 nodes — and since at most f of those can be Byzantine, at least one is honest, always. (b) PBFT: pre-prepare, prepare, commit

Castro & Liskov's Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (OSDI 1999) is the protocol that made BFT usable outside a research paper — the first to run at speeds competitive with crash-fault systems on a LAN. WHY THREE PHASES, NOT TWO

Pre-prepare gets everyone a candidate (primary, sequence number, value) — but a replica can't act on just that, because the primary might be lying or might crash mid-broadcast, leaving replicas in different states. Prepare is a replica-to-replica cross-check: collecting 2f+1 matching prepares (a prepared certificate) proves that a quorum agrees on this (sequence number, value) pairing within the current view — but a replica that crashes right after preparing could leave that fact undiscoverable if the view changes, risking two different values being finalized across a view boundary. Commit exists purely to survive that case: collecting 2f+1 matching commits (a committed certificate) proves the prepared certificate is durable across future view changes, because any future primary must prove, via the new-view protocol, that it has seen this certificate before proposing anything conflicting. Two phases give you agreement within a view; three give you agreement that survives the primary itself failing.

(c) CAP and PACELC, applied to a ledger during a partition

Brewer's CAP theorem (formalized by Gilbert & Lynch, 2002) says a distributed data store facing a network Partition must choose between Consistency and Availability for the duration of the partition — it cannot have both. A blockchain is a replicated data store, so CAP applies to it directly, and the two major consensus families answer differently:

DURING A PARTITIONTENDERMINT-STYLE BFTNAKAMOTO PoW
What happensBlock production halts the instant fewer than 2f+1 of 3f+1 validators can hear each other — no quorum, no new block, by construction. Both partitions keep mining independently, each producing its own valid chain — the protocol has no notion of "not enough voters," only "whoever has more accumulated work."
CAP choiceC over A — the chain is unavailable for new writes until connectivity returns, never fabricates a second history. A over C — the chain keeps accepting transactions on both sides, producing two temporarily inconsistent histories.
After partition healsNo reconciliation needed — there was only ever one chain. Fork-choice (ch. 10) discards the lighter chain; any transaction that was only in the discarded fork is reverted — consistency is restored probabilistically, after the fact, not guaranteed during the split.
PACELC (else: latency vs consistency, no partition) Pays a full O(n²) BFT round every block for instant finality — higher steady-state latency, zero reorg risk. Pays almost no per-block latency (whoever finds a block broadcasts immediately) but finality is only probabilistic, asymptotically approaching certainty with confirmation depth (ch. 8's exact math) — lower latency, non-zero reorg risk that decays over time.

Neither choice is "more decentralized" or "more correct" in the abstract — they are different, honest answers to the same theorem, and the right one depends entirely on whether your application can tolerate a chain that stops (a bank settlement network, probably not — see §12) or a chain that occasionally reorganizes its last few blocks (a public payment network, apparently yes, for over 15 years).

ARC 10 · PERFORMANCE / SECURITY CHARACTERISTICS

Cost, in messages, bytes, rounds, and dollars

Precision here compounds — every number below is the reason classical BFT stays inside permissioned networks of dozens to low hundreds of nodes, never thousands:

  • Message complexity. PBFT's prepare and commit phases are each an all-to-all broadcast among n replicas: n x (n-1) messages per phase, O(n squared) total per client request. At n=100 that's roughly 20,000 authenticated messages for one decision. At n=1,000 it's ~2,000,000 — the quadratic term dominates fast.
  • Rounds, not just messages. The Lamport-Shostak-Pease OM(f) algorithm needs exactly f+1 sequential communication rounds to tolerate f Byzantine faults — a hard lower bound (Fischer & Lynch, 1982) for oral-message protocols reaching agreement with certainty. More replication buys tolerance, not speed; buying speed means bounding f low or moving to a signed/pipelined variant like HotStuff.
  • Bandwidth. A 256-byte signed vote x n squared messages: at n=100 that is already ~6.5 MB of consensus traffic for a single decision, entirely independent of how large the actual proposed value (a block) is.
  • Attack cost in a permissioned network. With n known identities, an attacker needs to compromise f+1 = floor((n-1)/3) + 1 of them — a headcount, and headcounts have a real-world price: background checks, legal contracts, insider-threat monitoring, hardware security modules. That price is exactly what chapter 7 shows collapses to zero the moment identities are free to create.

WHY HOTSTUFF EXISTS — AND WHAT IT COST

HotStuff (Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta & Abraham, PODC 2019 — built for Facebook/Meta's Diem, and later the basis for Aptos's and Sui's consensus) collapses PBFT's all-to-all broadcasts into a leader-collects, leader-broadcasts pattern using threshold signatures — replicas send their vote only to the leader, who aggregates 2f+1 of them into one certificate and rebroadcasts that, turning each phase from O(n squared) into O(n). The price: correctness now leans harder on the leader (mitigated by frequent, cheap leader rotation) and on the threshold-signature scheme's own cryptographic assumptions — there is no free lunch, only a different place to put the risk.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS

Where the theory met production, and what broke

Incident 1: Medalla — Ethereum's testnet, August 2020

Ethereum's Casper FFG finality gadget (a BFT-family protocol structurally related to Tendermint — full treatment in chapter 9) finalizes a checkpoint once >= 2/3 of staked validators vote for it within the required window. During the Medalla testnet's run-up to mainnet, a leap-second-adjacent NTP misconfiguration in a dominant client caused a large share of validators to drift out of clock sync simultaneously and go offline for attestation purposes. Honest-but-offline participation fell close enough to the two-thirds line that the chain stopped finalizing for several days — not because anyone lied, but because "honest" and "responsive" are different properties, and the protocol's safety margin assumes both simultaneously. It is the cleanest public demonstration available of what this chapter calls "shattering below the threshold": no single bad actor, just not enough simultaneously online honest votes to clear 2/3.

Incident 2: the XRP Ledger consensus protocol — analyzed, not attacked, 2020

Amores-Sesar, Cachin & Mićić's academic analysis of the XRP Ledger Consensus Protocol found that its safety argument — participants trust a configurable list of validators, a UNL — requires pairs of validators' UNLs to overlap by more than a very high fraction (the paper's bound approaches 90%) to guarantee no fork, stricter than the informally assumed threshold operators had been configuring in practice. No fork has occurred in production from this gap as of this writing, but the episode is a textbook case of this chapter's central lesson: a Byzantine safety bound is not a suggestion, and a protocol that departs from the classical n>=3f+1 quorum-intersection structure (as XRPL's overlapping-but-not-identical UNLs do) needs its own from-scratch proof, not an inherited one.

Incident 3: why permissioned chains quietly abandoned classical BFT for Raft

Hyperledger Fabric's ordering service shipped its early releases atop Kafka (crash-fault, not Byzantine) and later migrated to a Raft-based orderer (also crash-fault) rather than a PBFT-family protocol — a deliberate choice consistent with S1's fault-model argument: Fabric's ordering nodes are typically run by consortium members under legal agreements with liability for misbehavior, closer to the "administrators, not adversaries" population that justifies crash-stop rather than the "financially incentivized to cheat" population that justifies Byzantine tolerance. The lesson generalizes: BFT is not free, and mature systems apply it only where the incentive analysis in S1 actually demands it — not by default.

ARC 13 · CODE WALKTHROUGH

Reading the algorithms: OM(f), PBFT's state machine, and view-change timing

There is no single "consensus.c" the way there is a postmaster.c — these algorithms are described in papers and reimplemented per-project. What follows is reference pseudo-code, faithful to the 1982 and 1999 papers' actual structure, organized the way a from-scratch implementation would be:

First, the algorithm section 5 derived and section 15's simulator runs — the recursive core, unchanged in structure since 1982:

pythonoral_messages.py — OM(f), the Lamport-Shostak-Pease algorithm
DEFAULT = "RETREAT"   # fixed tie-break value, per the original paper

def OM(f, commander, held_value_or_fn, lieutenants, get_actual_sender_fn):
    """
    held_value_or_fn: the value the commander broadcasts. A constant for an
    honest commander (same value to everyone); a function(recipient) -> value
    for a traitor commander, who may equivocate per recipient.
    """
    # Step 1: commander broadcasts to every lieutenant, directly.
    direct = {}
    for lt in lieutenants:
        v = held_value_or_fn(lt) if callable(held_value_or_fn) else held_value_or_fn
        direct[lt] = v

    if f == 0:
        # Base case: no more cross-checking possible — trust the direct message.
        return {lt: direct.get(lt, DEFAULT) for lt in lieutenants}

    # Step 2: each lieutenant i now acts as commander in OM(f-1), relaying
    # whatever it received in step 1 to every OTHER lieutenant.
    sub_decisions = {}
    for i in lieutenants:
        others = [x for x in lieutenants if x != i]
        relay_fn = get_actual_sender_fn(i, others, direct[i])  # honest: constant; traitor: may equivocate
        sub_decisions[i] = OM(f - 1, i, relay_fn, others, get_actual_sender_fn)

    # Step 3: each lieutenant j takes the MAJORITY of its direct value and
    # every value it obtained about j from every other lieutenant i.
    decisions = {}
    for j in lieutenants:
        votes = [direct[j]] + [sub_decisions[i][j] for i in lieutenants if i != j]
        decisions[j] = majority(votes)
    return decisions
WHY IT WAS BUILT THIS WAY — THREE CHOICES TO STEAL

1. Recursion depth exactly equals fault tolerance. OM(f) makes exactly f+1 rounds because each round "peels off" one layer of possible lying — after f+1 independent cross-checks, a lie can no longer hide behind another lie, which is precisely why the correctness proof needs n>3f and not one node fewer. 2. The default value is fixed, not adaptive. Ties break to a hardcoded constant (RETREAT), never to "my own value" — an adaptive tie-break would let a clever attacker exploit the very asymmetry it's supposed to remove. 3. The recipient, not the content, is authenticated. Oral-message OM assumes a lieutenant can always identify who sent a message (no spoofing the network layer) but never assumes the content is truthful — that separation of "who" from "what" is exactly what the Signed Messages variant (section 5) strengthens with cryptography, dropping the bound from 3f+1 to 2f+1.

Second, PBFT's replica state machine — the part that actually runs in production systems today:

pythonpbft/replica.py — normal-case phase transitions
class Replica:
    def on_pre_prepare(self, msg):
        # Accept only from the current primary, only for an unused sequence number,
        # only within the current view. Any violation -> discard, don't crash.
        if not self.valid_pre_prepare(msg):
            return
        self.log[msg.seq] = {"phase": "pre-prepared", "digest": msg.digest}
        self.broadcast(Prepare(msg.view, msg.seq, msg.digest, self.id))

    def on_prepare(self, msg):
        self.prepares[(msg.view, msg.seq, msg.digest)].add(msg.sender)
        if has_quorum(self.prepares[(msg.view, msg.seq, msg.digest)], self.f):
            # 2f+1 matching prepares == a PREPARED CERTIFICATE for this (seq, digest)
            self.log[msg.seq]["phase"] = "prepared"
            self.broadcast(Commit(msg.view, msg.seq, msg.digest, self.id))

    def on_commit(self, msg):
        self.commits[(msg.view, msg.seq, msg.digest)].add(msg.sender)
        if has_quorum(self.commits[(msg.view, msg.seq, msg.digest)], self.f):
            # 2f+1 matching commits == a COMMITTED CERTIFICATE: durable across view changes.
            self.execute(msg.seq)
            self.reply_to_client(msg.seq)

Third, the timeout logic that makes the whole thing an honest instance of "partial synchrony," not a silent assumption of full synchrony:

pythonpbft/view_change.py — the partial-synchrony escape hatch, concretely
class ViewChangeTimer:
    def __init__(self, base_ms=2000):
        self.base_ms = base_ms
        self.attempt = 0          # resets to 0 on every successful commit

    def on_timeout(self):
        # No progress heard from the primary in time -> suspect it, don't assume
        # it's Byzantine, just try a new one. Backoff means: if the REAL cause is
        # slow network (not a bad primary), we keep giving the network more room,
        # which is exactly "eventually synchronous," not "always synchronous."
        self.attempt += 1
        self.timeout_ms = self.base_ms * (2 ** self.attempt)
        return ViewChangeMsg(new_view=self.current_view + 1, proof=self.last_certificates())

Notice what none of these three snippets ever do: none of them try to detect a lie by inspecting message content for plausibility. Every safety property comes from counting matching, signed messages against a quorum threshold — the entire discipline is "trust arithmetic on signatures, never trust semantics," which is the only thing that scales to an adversary who is free to send arbitrarily plausible-looking lies.
