ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: a one-way refund channel via nSequence (2013)

Derive it the way Satoshi's 2013 bitcointalk post and Mike Hearn's follow-up code did. Two parties, Alice (payer) and Bob (payee), want Alice to pay Bob in small increments without a transaction per increment. Start from primitives you already have from chapters 3 and 4: a 2-of-2 multisig output and the transaction's nSequence field, which pre-SegWit was documented (if unreliably implemented) as a hint that a later version of a transaction spending the same input could replace an earlier, not-yet-confirmed one in miners' mempools.

pseudocodespilman_channel.py — the 2013 construction, annotated
# 1. Alice funds a 2-of-2 multisig with 1.0 BTC. She does NOT
#    broadcast this until step 2 exists, to avoid being stuck.
funding_tx = multisig_output(alice_pk, bob_pk, amount=1.0)

# 2. Before broadcasting funding_tx, Alice pre-signs a REFUND that
#    returns everything to her, timelocked far in the future (T).
#    This bounds her downside if Bob disappears: worst case, she
#    waits until T and reclaims all 1.0 BTC.
refund_tx = spend(funding_tx, to=alice_pk, amount=1.0, locktime=T)

# 3. Now Alice broadcasts funding_tx. The channel is open.

# 4. Each time Alice wants to pay Bob some amount, she signs a NEW
#    commitment paying Bob more and refunding herself less, sets a
#    high nSequence, and hands the fully-signed tx to Bob directly
#    -- off the network, off-chain, no fee paid yet.
commit_1 = spend(funding_tx, outs=[(bob_pk, 0.1), (alice_pk, 0.9)], nSequence=HIGH)
commit_2 = spend(funding_tx, outs=[(bob_pk, 0.3), (alice_pk, 0.7)], nSequence=HIGH+1)
# ... commit_N, whenever Alice wants, no on-chain footprint yet ...

# 5. Bob holds only the LATEST commitment he's been given. When he
#    wants to be paid, or T approaches, he broadcasts it.

This is a genuine payment channel: after the single on-chain funding transaction, Alice and Bob can exchange an arbitrary number of increasingly generous payments with zero additional on-chain transactions, and Bob is protected because each new commitment he receives strictly increases his balance. It already demonstrates the core mechanism this whole chapter builds on: a signed-but-unbroadcast transaction is a valid, enforceable claim the moment its predecessor confirms — broadcasting it is optional and can be deferred indefinitely.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

One direction, one relationship, and a replacement rule nobody enforces

Three failures, in increasing order of fundamental-ness — the first two are engineering defects, the third is structural and survives even a perfect implementation:

  • nSequence replacement was never a consensus guarantee. "Higher sequence number replaces lower" was a mempool policy convention in reference software, not a rule enforced by proof of work. A miner under no obligation to run that policy — or one who simply already has an earlier commitment in a block template — can confirm commit_1 instead of commit_9, and once any valid spend of the funding output confirms, the UTXO is gone; every other pre-signed commitment is now spending an already-spent input and is permanently invalid. Bob has no recourse: the chain doesn't know he was owed more, because he was never given anything that says so except a transaction Alice can suppress by getting an earlier one mined first, which she is financially motivated to do near the end of the relationship.
  • It is strictly one-directional. Every commitment must pay Bob more than the last (monotonic by construction, or Bob would simply broadcast the more generous ancestor). There is no way to pay Alice back without a second, independently funded channel running in the opposite direction — doubling the capital two parties must lock up to transact bidirectionally, which for anything resembling a real commercial relationship (a merchant refunding a customer, a market maker) is disqualifying.
  • The channel has a hard expiry, T, baked in at funding time. Reaching T forces an on-chain close and reopen to continue transacting — for a long-lived relationship this reintroduces exactly the per-relationship on-chain cost the construction was built to amortize away, just at a lower frequency.
QUANTIFY IT

Let p be the probability that some miner within the confirmation window includes an old, superseded commitment instead of the newest one Bob holds — driven by mempool propagation gaps, miners running non-default policy, or Alice paying an out-of-band side fee to a friendly pool to jump the queue. Bob's expected loss per channel lifetime is p × (final_balance − Bob's_confirmed_balance_if_cheated), and critically p is not under Bob's control — he cannot inspect miners' mempool policies, only hope. A construction whose security depends on unenforced, unobservable mempool convention is not a security property; chapter 11 will formalize this same failure mode as pinning, and you'll see the identical shape reappear in §11 of this chapter against the fixed version.

The fix for direction and expiry is comparatively easy — sign commitments both ways and let either party extend the timeout. The fix for the replacement problem is where the real engineering is, and it requires rethinking what a "commitment" is, not just how it's replaced.

ARC 05 · BETTER SOLUTION, EXAMINED

Attempt #2: symmetric, cooperatively-replaced commitments — and the cheat it invites

Drop reliance on mempool policy entirely. Instead of many pre-signed transactions racing for a single spend of the funding output, keep exactly one live commitment transaction per party at any moment, and make updating the balance a cooperative act: to move from state N to state N+1, both parties sign a fresh commitment transaction reflecting the new split, and both agree — verbally, by protocol convention — to discard transaction N. Nothing about consensus enforces "discard"; it is a promise. Step through what that promise is worth.

Symmetric commitments: the promise and the exploitSTEP-BY-STEP

Notice what changed for the better: both directions are now representable (either party can be the one whose balance increases in the next state), there's no expiry forcing periodic on-chain churn, and there is no ambiguous mempool race — any of the fully-signed commitment transactions is unconditionally valid the instant it's broadcast, by construction (both signatures are already on it). That last property is also exactly the new problem: validity no longer depends on being first past the post, it depends on nothing at all except having a copy. Every old commitment stays a live, spendable, fully-authorized transaction forever.

THE INCENTIVE, MADE CONCRETE

Suppose Alice and Bob open a channel at 50/50, transact for months, and now sit at 10/90 in Bob's favor — 40 coins have net flowed to Bob. Alice still possesses a validly, mutually signed copy of the very first commitment transaction, splitting the funds 50/50. Nothing on-chain distinguishes "the first state" from "the current state" — both are equally well-formed, equally signed spends of the same funding outpoint. If Alice broadcasts the old one and Bob isn't watching the chain at that exact moment, Alice recovers 40 coins she no longer has any economic claim to, at the cost of one transaction fee. The cooperative-discard promise has exactly the enforcement strength of an honor system between two people who opened this channel because they didn't want to have to trust each other.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL FAILS

The requirement this forces: make the old state worse than broadcasting nothing

You cannot prevent Alice from holding the old commitment — it is a fully signed, valid Bitcoin transaction and nothing in cryptography lets you un-sign it. You also cannot prevent her from broadcasting it — nodes will relay and mine any valid transaction. The only lever left is economic: make it so that if the old state ever reaches the chain, the counterparty can respond within a window and take the entire channel balance, not just their fair share. If broadcasting a revoked state has strictly negative expected value — you might gain your old balance back for an instant, but you will almost certainly lose everything, including funds that were legitimately yours — no rational party ever does it. This is the same move chapter 8 studied in mining: raise the cost of the attack above any plausible gain, rather than trying to make the attack impossible.

Deriving the mechanism from that requirement, three things must be simultaneously true of every commitment transaction:

  • Asymmetry. The two parties cannot hold identical copies of "the current state," because a penalty needs a party to react to a broadcast — if Alice's and Bob's copies were byte-identical, Bob's copy is exactly what got broadcast and there is no separate "Bob's honest output" to protect. Each party must instead hold a different transaction that pays out to the same two people but delays the broadcaster's own payout, giving the other side a reaction window.
  • A revocable delay on your own output. Whoever broadcasts a commitment must wait — via a relative timelock, chapter 8's OP_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY, typically hundreds of blocks — before spending their own balance out of it. That delay is the reaction window.
  • A trapdoor that opens only for old states. When the parties move to a new state, they must exchange something — a secret — that specifically unlocks an alternate spending path on the state they just agreed to abandon, and only that state. Whoever receives the secret for a given old state can, if that state is ever broadcast, claim the entire balance immediately, bypassing the delay entirely.
THE COST OF THIS FIX, HONESTLY STATED

This is not free. Every commitment now needs a delayed output with a penalty branch (more script, more on-chain bytes if it's ever force-closed); every party must retain every revocation secret it's ever received, for the life of the channel, or it cannot punish a cheat on an old state — a channel that has updated 100,000 times naively needs 100,000 stored 32-byte secrets; and — the sharpest operational cost — you must be online and watching the chain to see a cheat and react within the delay window, or the penalty right expires unused and the broadcaster keeps their unearned balance. Sections 8 and 11 derive the exact data structure that fixes the storage cost (a shachain, O(log n) not O(n)) and the exact service (a watchtower) that fixes the online-availability cost. Neither cost disappears; each is engineered down to something tolerable.
