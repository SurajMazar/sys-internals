Exam Overview & Study Strategy

Two real exams, eight real domains, and a scoring model most candidates never actually read before they start studying. This chapter gives you the blueprint numbers you'll lean on for the rest of this course, the elimination strategy that gets you through the "two plausible answers" trap, and a planner that turns domain weights into an actual study schedule instead of a vague feeling that you should "know more AWS."

You will be able tostate both exams' full blueprints — question counts, timing, pass scores, fees, and domain weights — from memory before you open chapter 2.
You will understandwhy domain-weighted study time beats breadth-first cramming, and how AWS's scaled-score model changes what "failing" actually means.
You will predictwhich entry point — SAA-C03 first, or straight into SAP-C02 — fits a given experience level, using the decision framework in section 9.
You will simulateallocating real study hours across real domain weights in the planner in section 15, and see exactly where instinct over- or under-invests.
MAPS TO: ORIENTATION — SAA-C03 & SAP-C02, ALL DOMAINS

Two exams, one organizing spine

Every later chapter in this course opens with a line telling you which exam and which domain it belongs to. That convention only works if you've memorized the spine first — two blueprints, four domains each, fixed percentage weights that AWS treats as a binding contract with its question bank. This chapter is that spine. Nothing here is deep AWS mechanism; it's the meta-knowledge that tells you where to spend the next several weeks.

AWS Certified Solutions Architect — Associate (SAA-C03)

  • 65 questions total — 50 scored plus 15 unscored. The unscored questions are trial content for future exam versions and are not identifiable during the exam, so you answer every question as if it counts, because from your perspective it might.
  • 130 minutes for the full 65-question set — roughly two minutes per question, less once you account for reading multi-paragraph scenarios.
  • Pass score: 720 out of 1000 on AWS's scaled scoring range (more on what "scaled" means in section 6).
  • Fee: about $150 at the time of writing. Exam pricing is exactly the kind of detail that changes — check the current AWS certification pricing page before you register rather than trusting any fixed number, including this one.
  • Domains and their real weights: Design Secure Architectures — 30%, Design Resilient Architectures — 26%, Design High-Performing Architectures — 24%, Design Cost-Optimized Architectures — 20%.
  • Recommended background: 1+ year of hands-on experience designing AWS solutions. There is no prerequisite certification — SAA-C03 is a legitimate first AWS credential.

AWS Certified Solutions Architect — Professional (SAP-C02)

  • 75 questions total — 65 scored plus 10 unscored, same "answer everything as if it counts" logic as above.
  • 180 minutes — more time than SAA-C03, which matches its longer, multi-constraint scenario style.
  • Pass score: 750 out of 1000 — a higher bar on the same 100–1000 scaled range.
  • Fee: about $300 at the time of writing, again subject to change — professional-level AWS exams have historically cost roughly double the associate-level fee.
  • Domains and their real weights: Design Solutions for Organizational Complexity — 26%, Design for New Solutions — 29%, Continuous Improvement for Existing Solutions — 25%, Accelerate Workload Migration and Modernization — 20%.
  • Recommended background: 2+ years of experience designing and implementing AWS solutions. There's no formal prerequisite certification, but the exam guide's assumed knowledge level makes attempting it without solid multi-account, multi-account-governance exposure a rough experience.
WHY THIS MATTERS ON TEST DAY

Both exams publish their domain weights in the official exam guide, and both exams are built to those weights — not approximately, but as a contract the question bank has to satisfy. A 30%-weighted domain on a 65-question exam maps to roughly 15–20 scored questions; a 20%-weighted domain maps to roughly 10–13. If you study all domains equally, you are guaranteed to be over-prepared for the smallest domain and under-prepared for the largest one, on every single attempt.

THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

"I'll just learn all of AWS"

Open any AWS console and the service list runs past 200 entries. The most common — and most understandable — instinct for a first-time certification candidate is to treat that entire list as the syllabus: work through every service's documentation page, watch a comprehensive video course front to back, and assume that broad familiarity will translate into a passing score. It feels thorough. It is also the single biggest time sink candidates report after failing an attempt.

This wrong instinct shows up in two common variants that are worth naming separately, because they fail for slightly different reasons:

  • The completionist. Tries to reach equal depth on every AWS service, from core compute and storage down to services that appear in maybe one question per exam sitting, if that. Equal depth across ~200 services with a fixed number of study hours means shallow depth everywhere, including on the services the exam actually leans on hardest.
  • The passive binge-watcher. Watches 20+ hours of video course content, nodding along, without ever attempting a timed multiple-response question under exam-like conditions. Recognition ("yes, I've heard of that") is mistaken for recall-under-pressure ("I can eliminate three wrong answers in 90 seconds"), and those are different skills.
THE INSTINCT

"If I know a little about everything AWS offers, I'll be ready for whatever the exam throws at me." It sounds like diligence. It's actually a refusal to prioritize — and prioritization is exactly what the domain weights in section 1 are handing you for free.

There's a third, quieter version of this instinct worth naming: the hours-logged tracker, who measures progress purely in hours spent studying — "I did four hours today" — without ever asking whether those four hours were spent on the domain that's actually worth the most points, or whether they produced anything that would survive a timed multiple-response question. Hours are a cost, not a result. The correct approach in section 4 measures progress in something closer to "consistent practice-exam score above target," which is a result, not a cost.

WHY IT'S WRONG

The arithmetic the wrong instinct ignores

The domain weights aren't marketing copy — they're a direct statement of how the question bank is constructed, and they make equal-effort-per-service provably inefficient the moment you do the arithmetic.

Take SAA-C03: 65 questions spread across exactly four weighted domains. Design Secure Architectures at 30% maps to roughly 15–20 of the 50 scored questions; Design Cost-Optimized Architectures at 20% maps to roughly 10–13. Spend your finite study hours split evenly across four domains and you've deliberately under-invested in the domain that's worth 50% more of your score, while over-investing in the one worth the least. Do that across ~200 services instead of four domains and the mismatch gets far worse — most services on that list barely register in any domain's real question distribution at all.

Then there's the format mechanic that makes shallow-but-broad knowledge fail disproportionately: multiple-response questions carry no partial credit. A "choose two" question where you correctly identify one of the two right answers and one wrong answer scores exactly the same as getting both wrong — zero. Shallow familiarity is often just enough to eliminate the obviously wrong options and land you on one correct answer plus one plausible-sounding distractor, which under multiple-response scoring is indistinguishable from having no idea at all.

Finally, understand what "wrong" actually costs you, because it's not what most candidates assume. AWS reports your performance per domain on the score report only as a qualitative label — something like "needs improvement" or "competent" — never an exact percentage, and critically, never a domain-level pass/fail gate. Only your overall scaled score (100–1000) determines whether you pass. A candidate who is genuinely weak in one domain can still pass comfortably if their overall scaled score clears the bar — provided the other domains, weighted by their real percentages, carry enough correct answers to compensate. That's precisely why weighting your study time to the real percentages is a directly optimal strategy under the actual scoring rule, not just a vague good habit: every hour spent on a 30%-weighted domain is worth more expected scaled-score points than an hour spent on a 20%-weighted one, and the mechanism that turns "weak domain" into "failed exam" is running out of scaled-score cushion elsewhere, not tripping some domain-specific gate.

THE CORRECT APPROACH

A study sequence derived from the blueprint, not from habit

None of this is exotic. It's the direct consequence of taking the domain weights and the no-partial-credit scoring rule seriously, then sequencing your effort so each stage builds on the one before it.

The correct study sequence6 STEPS

The sequence above is deliberately ordered: allocate before you learn, learn before you decide, decide before you practice, practice before you drill, and drill before you schedule anything with your money on the line. Skipping straight to practice questions without the fundamentals underneath them just teaches you to memorize this course's specific scenarios; skipping practice entirely and stopping at "I understand the services" leaves the actual tested skill — eliminating distractors on a timer — completely untrained.

LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

The rules of the exam itself, not just its content

The "correct approach" from section 4 still has to operate inside a set of administrative rules that trip up candidates who've never sat a Pearson VUE or PSI exam before. None of these are AWS service limits — they're exam-program limits, and they matter just as much on test day.

  • Retake waiting period. If you don't pass, AWS enforces a waiting period before you can retake the same exam. That waiting period is currently documented at 14 days for most AWS certification exams — but policies like this are exactly the kind of detail that can change, so confirm the current retake policy on AWS's certification site before you plan a retake date around it.
  • Three-year recertification validity. Both SAA-C03 and SAP-C02 certifications are valid for three years from the date you pass. After that, you recertify — either by retaking the current version of the same exam or, in some cases, a higher-level exam that also renews the associate credential.
  • No domain-level pass/fail — ever. Worth repeating from section 3 because it's the single most misunderstood mechanic: your score report shows qualitative per-domain performance ("needs improvement" / "competent"), never an exact percentage and never a domain gate. Only the overall scaled score (100–1000, pass at 720 for SAA-C03 or 750 for SAP-C02) determines the outcome.
  • NDA acceptance is mandatory. Before any exam question appears, you must accept AWS's certification exam Non-Disclosure Agreement on-screen. Declining ends the exam attempt immediately, with no refund path implied by declining alone.
  • Testing center vs. online proctored — a real tradeoff. A physical Pearson VUE or PSI test center controls the environment for you (no webcam checks, no room-scan friction) but requires travel and fixed scheduling. Online proctoring is more convenient but requires a private room, a clear desk, ID checks via webcam, and a stable connection — connection drops mid-exam are one of the most common avoidable failure modes candidates report, and they're entirely within your control to prevent by testing your setup in advance.
  • The exam is not pausable once started. There's no "save and resume later." Whatever interruption risk exists in your environment — a knock at the door, a dependent needing attention, a laptop battery — needs to be eliminated before you click start, not managed during the attempt.
  • Score report timing. A provisional pass/fail typically appears on screen immediately after the exam ends, but the full report — the scaled score plus the qualitative per-domain breakdown — usually takes a few business days to populate in your certification account. Don't plan a same-day retake decision around the detailed breakdown; you'll have the pass/fail long before you have the domain-level detail to act on.
TRAP

Candidates sometimes read "no domain-level pass/fail" as "domain weights don't matter, since only the overall score counts." That's backwards. The weights are exactly why a bad performance in a 30%-weighted domain is so much harder to compensate for than a bad performance in a 20%-weighted one — there are more scored questions riding on it, so more scaled-score points are exposed to your weakest area.

DECISION FRAMEWORK

Which exam first, and how many hours — by experience level

This is the single most exam-relevant artifact in the chapter. It's a starting point, not a guarantee — adjust the hour estimates against your own learning speed and how much of your "hands-on experience" was genuinely architecture-level versus purely operational.

Experience levelRecommended entry pointEstimated study hoursResource mix
No AWS experience Start with fundamentals, then SAA-C03. Do not attempt SAP-C02 directly. 120–160 hrs Structured fundamentals course + guided hands-on labs + a full practice-exam bank, in that order.
<1 year hands-on SAA-C03. You likely have enough exposure to skip pure fundamentals and go straight to domain-weighted study. 80–120 hrs Official exam guide + whitepapers for your weakest domain + heavy practice-exam volume.
1–2 years hands-on SAA-C03 as a fast confidence-building step, or straight to SAP-C02 if your experience already spans multi-account setups. 60–100 hrs (SAA-C03) / 100–150 hrs (SAP-C02) Domain-weighted scenario practice; light on fundamentals, heavy on decision frameworks like section 9-style tables for each service pairing.
2+ years, multi-account production experience SAP-C02 directly. SAA-C03 would mostly retest things you already do at work. 80–120 hrs Organization-level whitepapers (multi-account governance, large-scale migration patterns), scenario-only practice exams, minimal service-feature review.
HOW TO READ THIS TABLE

"Estimated hours" assumes you're following the sequence from section 4 — proportional allocation, then fundamentals, then decision frameworks, then practice, then drilling. Candidates who skip straight to practice exams without the earlier stages typically need more total hours, not fewer, because they spend the practice-exam time re-learning fundamentals instead of refining exam technique.

One more adjustment worth making to any row in this table: if your hands-on experience is heavily concentrated in a single domain — say, years of hands-on networking work but almost no exposure to cost-management tooling — shift a modest number of hours away from that table's flat estimate and toward whichever domain your actual experience doesn't cover, even if the table's row would otherwise suggest a lighter total. The table gives you a starting split by experience level; the section 15 planner lets you refine that split further once you have a clearer sense of which specific domains are already strong versus which are genuinely new to you.

REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three learners, three constraints

These are original vignettes written by this course to illustrate the decision framework from section 9 — they are not real exam content, and none of the numbers below are claimed to come from any actual leaked question.

CASE 1 — PRIYA

Priya has three years of backend engineering experience but has never deployed anything to AWS in production — her team runs on a different cloud provider. Her new employer requires an AWS certification within ten weeks as a condition of a role transfer. She has roughly 12 hours a week to study.

Which study approach should she choose? Ten weeks at 12 hours/week is about 120 hours — squarely in the "no AWS experience" row of the section 9 table. She should not attempt SAP-C02 or skip fundamentals; a structured fundamentals pass followed by domain-weighted SAA-C03 study, with heavy hands-on lab time in weeks 1–4, is the only approach that fits both her actual experience level and her fixed deadline.

CASE 2 — DIEGO

Diego has four years of experience as a solutions architect at a mid-size company running substantial AWS workloads across multiple accounts. He passed SAA-C03 two years ago. His manager has given him six weeks to prepare for SAP-C02 ahead of a promotion review.

Which study approach should he choose? Diego sits in the "2+ years, multi-account" row — he should skip fundamentals entirely and go straight to domain-weighted deep dives on SAP-C02's four domains, especially the two he's had least direct exposure to (organizational complexity and migration/modernization tend to be the ones day-to-day architects touch least, even at his level), backed by scenario-only practice exams rather than service-feature review he already knows cold.

CASE 3 — AISHA

Aisha is a DevOps engineer with eight months of hands-on AWS experience. Her contract renewal requires "an AWS certification" but doesn't specify which one, and she has four months before the renewal date.

Which study approach should she choose? Eight months of hands-on puts her between the "<1 year" and "1–2 years" rows — not yet ready for SAP-C02's assumed multi-account depth, but plausibly ready for SAA-C03 without a full fundamentals pass. Since the requirement is satisfied by any AWS certification, SAA-C03 is the correct target: it's the credential her actual experience level supports achieving reliably inside four months, and passing it also builds the exact foundation SAP-C02 would require later if her role keeps growing.

Neither exam is a typing test, but both assume you've been in a terminal talking to AWS at least once. WHY THE EXAM CARES

Scenario questions frequently describe a company that already has automation, scripts, or a CI/CD pipeline talking to AWS — and the correct answer often hinges on understanding that a named profile, an assumed role, or a CLI-driven step is already how that company operates. If you've never run aws configure or aws sts get-caller-identity yourself, that class of scenario reads as abstract instead of obvious. This is exactly the gap the hands-on lab accompanying this course is built to close.

EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

What to remember from this chapter

Five things worth being able to recite without looking them up:

  • Both blueprints are fixed contracts, not suggestions — SAA-C03 is 65 questions / 130 minutes / pass 720, weighted 30/26/24/20 across Secure, Resilient, High-Performing, and Cost-Optimized; SAP-C02 is 75 questions / 180 minutes / pass 750, weighted 26/29/25/20 across Organizational Complexity, New Solutions, Continuous Improvement, and Migration & Modernization.
  • Multiple-response questions have no partial credit — getting one of two correct answers scores the same as getting zero, which is why shallow-but-broad study fails disproportionately on "choose two/three" items.
  • Only the overall scaled score gates pass/fail — domain performance is reported qualitatively, never as a hard per-domain gate, but a weak domain still costs you real scaled-score points proportional to its weight.
  • Study time should be allocated proportional to domain weight, then layered fundamentals → decision frameworks → practice questions → weak-area drilling → real exam, gated on consistently hitting your target practice-exam score.
  • The exam-day rules are administrative, not technical — NDA acceptance, no pausing once started, a real retake waiting period, and three-year recertification are all things to plan around before you ever open a practice question.

Chapter 2 moves from meta-strategy into the first real content domain: IAM & Account Security Fundamentals — users, groups, and roles; how policy evaluation actually resolves an allow/deny decision when multiple policies apply; the principle of least privilege in practice; and how STS AssumeRole lets one identity temporarily borrow another's permissions. It's not a coincidence that this is chapter 2 — Design Secure Architectures is the highest-weighted domain on SAA-C03, and IAM is the mechanism underneath almost every security decision you'll be asked to make.
