NETFLIX STREAMING INTERNALS · CHAPTER 07

Open Connect:
Netflix's Real CDN

Every one of Netflix's hundreds of millions of subscribers pulls video chunks from somewhere physically close to them — increasingly, not from a generic third-party content delivery network at all. This chapter derives, from the physics and economics of moving enormous, highly repetitive byte streams, why Netflix designed and now operates its own purpose-built CDN, Open Connect, and why its core hardware unit — the Open Connect Appliance (OCA) — lives one network hop away, physically racked inside the ISP's own building.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

Almost all of your bytes are the same few thousand files

Strip away the UI and Netflix's traffic splits into two workloads with almost nothing in common. One is small, unique-per-request, and latency-sensitive to interaction: "what's in my queue," "what should I watch next," "am I allowed to play this title here." The other is enormous in raw byte volume, drawn from a relatively small and slowly-changing catalog, and watched by an overlapping audience of millions of households — the video itself.

That second workload has three properties worth naming precisely, because each one changes what "the right architecture" means:

  • Volume dwarfs everything else. A single hour of video, even efficiently encoded (chapters 2–4 covered why), is many orders of magnitude more bytes than any number of API calls a household could generate in that hour. Delivery bytes, not control-plane requests, are the resource problem.
  • The catalog is finite and highly cacheable. Netflix licenses and produces a catalog on the order of thousands of titles per region — not billions of unique objects like a general web cache has to handle. The same encoded files get requested, by different households, over and over.
  • Demand is skewed and largely predictable. A small fraction of titles absorb a disproportionate share of total viewing on any given day or region (chapter 8 formalizes this), and viewing concentrates in predictable evening hours per timezone. This is a workload you can plan for, not one that arrives as unpredictable noise.

A generic third-party CDN is a perfectly good answer to a different problem: accelerating many different customers' heterogeneous, less-predictable objects — HTML, images, API responses, software downloads — across a broad but comparatively shallow footprint of internet-facing points of presence. Nothing about that design assumes any single tenant will need a large, sustained fraction of the capacity of any one ISP's own access network, every evening, for a catalog whose popularity is knowable in advance. This chapter is about what happens once that assumption stops holding — and about the fact that Netflix's control-plane traffic (personalization, manifests, licensing checks — the subject of chapters 8–13) keeps running on generic cloud infrastructure precisely because it doesn't share these three properties.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE REST OF THIS ARC

Every remaining chapter in the delivery arc assumes this split: a small, dynamic, latency-to-interaction control plane running on general-purpose cloud infrastructure, and a huge, cacheable, physically-placed delivery plane running on purpose-built hardware. Chapter 8 asks the next question this split creates — given finite storage on each piece of that hardware, which bytes do you put where, before anyone asks for them?

DEEP CONTEXT · TWO SEPARATE 2011-ERA MIGRATIONS, OFTEN CONFUSED

Around the same years, Netflix made two big infrastructure moves that are frequently conflated but are architecturally unrelated. Migrating its control plane and back-end services onto AWS is about where the small, dynamic, personalization/API workload runs — generic cloud compute. Building Open Connect is about where the huge, cacheable video-byte workload is physically placed — purpose-built delivery hardware, much of it living inside other companies' networks. Netflix runs both, simultaneously, for exactly the reason chapter 1 of this arc argues: the two workloads don't share an optimal architecture, so there's no reason to force them onto the same one.

ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: treat video like any other web asset

Reason from first principles, the way an engineer actually would in 2007. You have video files. You need them to reach browsers and devices worldwide, quickly. The industry-standard answer to "deliver files quickly worldwide" is: put a commercial CDN in front of your origin, point DNS at it, and let its anycast network do the geographic routing. Nothing about video says you need anything more specialized than that.

yamlnaive_cdn_config.yaml — the strawman: "just use a CDN"
# the simplest possible answer to "deliver our video worldwide"
origin: s3://netflix-master-origin/encodes/
cdn_provider: generic-cdn-vendor
routing: anycast            # nearest PoP by IP geolocation / BGP anycast
cache_policy:
  ttl: 24h
  eviction: LRU              # generic, not popularity-aware
  same_policy_for:            # video gets no special treatment
    - homepage_thumbnails
    - api_responses
    - video_chunks
regions: [us-east, us-west, eu-west, ap-south]  # a handful of continental PoPs
isp_integration: none          # no Netflix hardware inside any ISP's network

This is not a strawman built to be laughed at — it is exactly what Netflix actually ran on for several years after the 2007 streaming pivot, using established third-party vendors (publicly, at various points, providers including Akamai, Limelight Networks, and Level 3). It gets a lot right: no hardware to build, no ISP relationships to negotiate, mature anycast routing, and a bill you can pay monthly instead of a balance sheet full of appliances. For the overwhelming majority of companies delivering video at moderate scale, this remains the correct answer today — Open Connect is not "the right way to run a CDN," it is the right answer to a scale and cost problem Netflix specifically had.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

The arithmetic that breaks a rented CDN at Netflix's scale

Push real numbers through the naive design and the cracks are arithmetic, not opinion. As of Netflix's most recent public disclosures the service has over 300 million paid memberships worldwide — and viewing concentrates heavily in a few evening hours per timezone, so peak concurrency is a large fraction of the subscriber base, not a smooth 24-hour average.

Take a deliberately conservative back-of-envelope: suppose on the order of tens of millions of streams are active simultaneously at a regional peak, each consuming somewhere in the low single-digit megabits per second once you blend HD, SD, and mobile-bitrate viewing (chapters 2–4 covered why the number for any one stream varies by title, device, and network condition). Multiply, and you get sustained delivery demand in the tens of terabits per second, concentrated into the same few hours every single day. Public, independent measurements of internet traffic composition (Sandvine's periodic "Global Internet Phenomena" reports, among others) have at various points put Netflix's share of peak downstream traffic in some regions at roughly a quarter to a third of all traffic on that network during the peak hour — the exact figure moves year to year and isn't the point; the point is the order of magnitude: one company's video bytes, concentrated in a short daily window, can rival a meaningful fraction of an entire ISP's total downstream capacity.

WHY A RENTED, GENERIC CDN BREAKS HERE — NOT SOMEWHERE ELSE

1. Transit cost scales with bytes moved, and the bytes are enormous. A commercial CDN vendor prices delivery per gigabyte (however it's packaged); even a fraction of a cent per GB, multiplied across an estimated hundreds of petabytes moved globally per day, compounds into a delivery bill of many millions of dollars a month — real money whose marginal cost never falls, because you're always renting someone else's capacity. 2. The CDN's PoPs still aren't inside the ISP. A generic vendor's cache sits at its own facility or a shared exchange point; bytes bound for a customer still cross that ISP's own paid peering/transit links to reach the customer's neighborhood — links sized for that ISP's overall traffic mix, not for one content owner's predictable, concentrated evening spike. 3. Generic caching policy isn't tuned to a Zipfian catalog. LRU across "everything" wastes cache capacity relative to a policy that knows a small number of titles absorb most of the requests (§10 makes this precise). 4. No control over placement or fill timing — you cannot ask a rented CDN to guarantee headroom for your specific evening peak, because its capacity is shared across every other tenant with their own peak too.

None of this means third-party CDNs are badly engineered — they are excellent solutions to a more heterogeneous problem. It means that once your own single catalog's bytes are large enough to rival a meaningful share of an ISP's total downstream capacity, the economics that make renting sensible for everyone else stop applying to you specifically.

Every intermediate idea in the deck above still crosses some external network boundary between Netflix's equipment and the customer — and every external boundary is a link that Netflix doesn't own, doesn't fully control the capacity of, and shares with every other source of traffic that ISP carries. An Open Connect Appliance racked inside the ISP's own facility removes that boundary from the request path entirely for anything it has cached: the bytes never cross an interconnection link at all. §7 shows the full architecture that results once you also handle the titles that a given box hasn't cached.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL COSTS

Embedding hardware inside a stranger's network is not free

Open Connect trades a recurring per-byte bill for a different, less visible set of costs. Be precise about where they come from:

  • Capital and logistics. Netflix must design, manufacture or procure, ship, install, and eventually replace physical hardware across thousands of ISP facilities on every populated continent — a genuine hardware supply chain and reliability program, not a configuration change. Unlike a CDN bill, this shows up on a balance sheet as depreciating assets, not an operating expense line.
  • Placement is now Netflix's problem, not the vendor's. With thousands of appliances of finite storage scattered across the world, something has to decide exactly which slice of the catalog each one holds. Guess wrong for a region and that box's cache-miss rate rises, sending requests back over a backhaul link to a bigger appliance or the origin — quietly reproducing part of the very hop-count problem embedding was supposed to eliminate. This is the entire subject of chapter 8.
  • Fill traffic needs its own traffic pattern. Refreshing an appliance's cache competes for the same ISP link that customers are using to watch. The practical answer is to schedule most of that fill during the ISP's own off-peak overnight window, which means Open Connect effectively runs two coordinated traffic patterns — serving live requests, and filling caches for tomorrow — on a bandwidth budget it must not let collide.
  • Thousands of individual relationships, not one vendor contract. Instead of paying one CDN invoice, Netflix maintains technical and operational relationships with a very large number of ISPs of wildly different size and sophistication — which is exactly why Netflix publishes an open, standardized peering policy: it needs a process that scales to "any qualifying network can ask," not a bespoke deal each time.
  • A single embedded box is a single point of local failure. The steering system must detect an unhealthy or overloaded appliance and fail requests over to another nearby option — which means Netflix must keep non-embedded delivery paths (IXP peering, and ultimately origin) alive as a permanent fallback, not a transitional step it can ever fully retire.
  • Long-tail titles never fully escape the original problem. No single regional appliance can hold the entire global catalog. A request for an obscure title that a local OCA was never filled with still has to travel further — for a much smaller fraction of bytes than before, but the physics haven't vanished, only shrunk to the cases that matter least.

So the final architecture is not simply "OCAs everywhere" — it's embedded hardware for the bulk of predictable, popular demand, backed by IXP-peered clusters and an origin for everything embedding can't reach. Let's meet the whole system.

DEEP CONTEXT · THE MANIFEST IS WHERE THESE TWO PLANES MEET

Chapter 6 covered the manifest as a bitrate-ladder description. It is also, mechanically, the hand-off point between the control plane and Open Connect: the control plane's steering service is what decides which specific OCA hostnames go into that manifest for this client, this title, right now. ARC 09 · TRADEOFFS

The same delivery problem, four ways

Every large-scale video service faces this exact spread of options. None dominates the others — they only move the same underlying cost between "pay a vendor per byte" and "own the hardware and the relationships":

APPROACHWHAT THEY BOUGHTWHAT THEY PAID
Generic third-party CDN (single vendor) zero hardware ownership fast to adopt no control over ISP-level placement shared multi-tenant capacity exposed to peering-link congestion
Multi-CDN (several vendors) vendor diversity some negotiating leverage still crosses external peering links more integration & ops overhead doesn't fix the ISP-edge problem
IXP-peered OCA (Netflix-operated) Netflix controls placement & caching policy no per-ISP hardware footprint needed works for ISPs that won't embed still crosses one peering boundary exposed to that link's congestion
ISP-embedded OCA one hop, zero external boundaries best latency & throughput stability ISP saves its own transit cost too Netflix bears hardware capex & logistics needs thousands of individual ISP relationships useless for titles it wasn't filled with

Read the table as a proof by exhaustion: distance from the customer cannot be dodged, only bought — either with money paid to a third party per byte delivered, or with capital and thousands of operational relationships owned directly. Open Connect is Netflix's bet that, at its actual scale, owning was cheaper and more controllable than renting; it is not evidence that owning is the right answer at smaller scale, where the fixed costs of the second column dominate.

ARC 10 · PERFORMANCE & SCALE CHARACTERISTICS

Why caching only part of the catalog still works

No single appliance holds the whole catalog, so the entire economics of embedding rests on one empirical fact: content popularity is not remotely uniform. ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS

The peering dispute that made this architecture inevitable

Incident 1: the 2014 Netflix–Comcast (and Verizon) interconnection dispute

This is one of the most concrete, publicly documented illustrations of exactly the problem this chapter derives. Netflix's video, delivered via third-party transit providers into Comcast's network, was experiencing visibly degraded quality — buffering, forced lower resolution — for Comcast subscribers in late 2013 and early 2014. The underlying cause was specific and well documented: the interconnection points between Netflix's transit providers and Comcast's network were congested, not Netflix's own infrastructure and not Comcast's core network. Because neither company individually captured the full benefit of widening that link, neither had been in a hurry to pay for it — a classic externality. The dispute became a highly public, net-neutrality-adjacent argument over who should pay for interconnection capacity, and was resolved when Netflix agreed to pay Comcast directly for interconnection in early 2014, with a similar paid-peering arrangement following with Verizon.

State the nuance honestly: this was as much a commercial and regulatory dispute as an engineering one — reasonable people disagreed about who should have paid for that link, and it isn't this chapter's job to settle that. What is squarely an engineering lesson is where the failure lived: at the handoff between two networks, a location neither company was singularly incentivized to fix. Open Connect's free-appliance model can be read as Netflix's long-term structural answer to exactly this class of problem — instead of repeatedly negotiating (or fighting over) capacity at a boundary, remove the boundary from the request path for an ISP willing to host the hardware, aligning both companies' incentives instead of relying on either side unilaterally paying for a link upgrade.

Incident 2: a realistic caching-system failure pattern (industry-standard, not a specific disclosed Netflix outage)

Because the specific internal incident record for Open Connect isn't public, state this as what it is: a pattern well known across any large embedded-cache system, described honestly as such. A large simultaneous content event — a globally coordinated season release, dropping at local midnight in every timezone — creates a wave of near-first-time requests for a title that a given region's appliances may not yet have finished filling. Every one of those requests becomes a cache miss at the same moment, all falling back to backhaul from a parent OCA or the origin simultaneously, which can transiently saturate exactly the backhaul links embedding was meant to relieve pressure from, degrading quality regionally until the fill catches up. The fix is not a serving-side patch — it's a placement-side one: forecast the spike and pre-position the content before the drop, which is precisely what chapter 8's predictive caching exists to do.

ARC 13 · CODE WALKTHROUGH

Reading the shape of the system (illustrative reference pseudocode)

Open Connect's actual internal source isn't public. What follows is precise, representative pseudocode of how a system with these constraints is actually built — labeled as illustrative throughout, not a claim about real internal file names.

control-plane/ — runs on generic cloud compute (illustrative module layout)
├── steering/
│ └── ● manifest_builder — ranks candidate OCA hosts per session, builds the manifest
├── licensing/rights_check — region/title licensing gate (chapter 6 territory)
oca-agent/ — software running on every physical appliance, embedded or IXP-peered
├── cache/
│ └── ● chunk_store — local disk/flash cache keyed by title + byte-range
├── fill/
│ └── ● overnight_fill — rate-limited puller, scheduled off-peak
└── health/telemetry_reporter — reports load/health back to steering
origin/encode_store — durable store of every bitrate·codec variant (chapters 3–6)

Ranking candidate hosts for a manifest — this is the decision that determines whether a client ever gets routed to an embedded OCA at all:

pseudo-pythonsteering/manifest_builder — illustrative
def build_manifest(client_ip, title_id):
    # resolve which ISP / autonomous system this client is on
    as_number = resolve_as(client_ip)
    candidates = lookup_oca_candidates(as_number)   # embedded + nearby IXP-peered

    def score(oca):
        return (
            proximity_score(oca, as_number)          # embedded > IXP-peered > distant
            + health_score(oca)                      # live telemetry, not static
            + probability_has_title(oca, title_id)   # placement hint, ch. 8
        )

    ranked = sorted(candidates, key=score, reverse=True)
    return {
        "hosts": ranked[:5],          # ordered fallback list, never just one
        "origin_fallback": ORIGIN_URL,
    }

Serving a chunk request on the appliance itself — the hit/miss branch that decides whether this request ever crosses a network boundary:

pseudo-pythonoca-agent/cache/chunk_store — illustrative
def on_chunk_request(title_id, byte_range):
    if local_cache.has(title_id, byte_range):
        record_hit()
        return local_cache.serve(title_id, byte_range)  # never leaves this box

    record_miss()                                       # rare for well-placed popular titles
    data = fetch_from_parent_or_origin(title_id, byte_range)
    if cache_policy.should_retain(title_id):
        local_cache.store(title_id, byte_range, data)
    return data

The overnight fill loop — deliberately isolated from the serving path so it can never compete with a customer's primetime bandwidth:

pseudo-pythonoca-agent/fill/overnight_fill — illustrative
def run_off_peak_fill(this_oca):
    fill_list = placement_service.get_target_titles(this_oca.region)  # ch. 8's output
    for title in fill_list:
        if local_cache.has(title):
            continue
        pull_from_parent_or_origin(title, rate_limit=FILL_BANDWIDTH_CAP)
        # FILL_BANDWIDTH_CAP keeps fill traffic off the link during peak hours
WHY IT'S BUILT THIS WAY — THREE CHOICES WORTH STEALING

1. Separate the ranking decision from the serving mechanism. The steering service is cheap software that can be iterated on constantly; the OCA's serving path is deliberately simple and boring — exactly the split you want when one side changes weekly and the other must never go down. 2. Feed live health back into ranking, never treat it as static. An appliance degrading gracefully into "ranked lower" rather than a hard failure is the same principle chapter 6 used for bitrate switching, one layer lower in the stack. 3. Treat cache fill as an explicitly rate-limited background job, isolated from the foreground serving path. This is the same "never let maintenance compete with live traffic" principle that shows up in almost every system with a background write path and a foreground read path — the details differ, the shape doesn't.
