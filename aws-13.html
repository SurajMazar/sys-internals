Resilience &
Disaster Recovery

Nearly every disaster-recovery question on both exams is the same puzzle wearing a different costume: the scenario hands you two numbers — how long you can be down, and how much data you can lose — and asks you to pick the cheapest real AWS pattern that actually satisfies both. This chapter builds the exact mental checklist for solving that puzzle in under a minute, every time.

ARC 01 · EXAM CONTEXT

Where this sits on the real blueprint

This chapter maps directly to Design Resilient Architectures — 26% of the Associate exam (SAA-C03) — and to Continuous Improvement for Existing Solutions — 25% of the Professional exam (SAP-C02). Between the two exams, disaster recovery is worth roughly a quarter of every question you'll ever see, which is why it gets a full chapter of its own instead of a paragraph inside "resiliency in general."

Every DR question, at either level, is really asking you to solve for two variables stated as numbers somewhere in the scenario text:

  • RTO — Recovery Time Objective. How long the business can tolerate being down after a disaster, measured from the moment of failure to the moment service is restored.
  • RPO — Recovery Point Objective. How much data, measured in time, the business can tolerate losing — the gap between the last point your data was safely captured and the moment of failure.

Associate-level questions test whether you can read those two numbers and pick the matching AWS pattern directly. Professional-level questions bury the same two numbers inside a larger, multi-constraint scenario (chapter 23 revisits exactly that layering) — but the underlying arithmetic never changes. Get comfortable with it here and you've banked points on both exams at once.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE REST OF THIS CHAPTER

Underline RTO and RPO in your head every time you see a resilience scenario, before you even glance at the answer options. Nearly every wrong answer in this domain is wrong for exactly one reason: it silently violates one of these two numbers, usually while sounding perfectly reasonable otherwise.

One more framing worth carrying forward: RTO and RPO are business decisions dressed up as technical ones. Nobody derives "our RPO is 15 minutes" from first principles — a finance team, a compliance requirement, or a customer SLA states it, and the architecture's job is to satisfy it at the lowest cost that actually does. That is why so many resilience questions on both exams read like business scenarios rather than service trivia — they are testing whether you can translate a business statement into the two numbers this chapter is built around, not whether you've memorized a service's marketing copy.

ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

Two reflexes that quietly cost you the easy points

Ask a room of candidates to design for disaster recovery and two wrong instincts show up almost every time, often from the same person in the same sentence:

  • "We already run Multi-AZ, so we already have disaster recovery." A Multi-AZ RDS deployment or an Auto Scaling group spread across Availability Zones feels like it should cover "disaster recovery" because it clearly makes the system more resilient. It does — but not to the failure mode the term "disaster recovery" is testing.
  • "When in doubt, pick the fanciest-sounding option." Faced with four unfamiliar-sounding DR patterns, many candidates default to whichever one sounds most bulletproof — usually a multi-region active-active design — on the theory that more resilience can never be the wrong answer.

Both instincts are understandable, and both are exactly what the exam is designed to catch. Exam-writers build distractor options by taking a plausible-sounding pattern and attaching it to a scenario where it's subtly the wrong fit — "Multi-AZ" and "the fanciest tier" are two of the most reliable plausible-sounding patterns in the entire resilience domain, which is precisely why they show up as wrong answers so often. §3 explains the mechanism behind each one; §4 gives you the checklist that replaces the reflex.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

Two different failure domains, two different answers

Multi-AZ deployments replicate synchronously to a standby in a different Availability Zone — but still inside the same AWS Region. An AWS Region is a collection of multiple, physically isolated Availability Zones tied together by low-latency private networking. Multi-AZ is built to survive exactly the failure of one AZ: a power event, a network partition, a hardware fault confined to that facility. It is not built to survive — and provides no protection against — an event that affects an entire Region, because the standby it fails over to lives in that same Region.

That distinction is the whole trap. A scenario that says "the application must remain available if a single data center fails" is an AZ-failure scenario — Multi-AZ (plus multiple AZs for compute) is the correct, sufficient, and cheapest answer. A scenario that says "the application must remain available if an entire AWS Region becomes unavailable" is a completely different failure domain, and no amount of Multi-AZ configuration inside that one Region solves it — you need one of the four cross-region DR tiers this chapter builds toward.

The second reflex — reaching for the fanciest tier by default — fails on cost, not correctness. A full multi-region active-active design keeps a complete, continuously-serving production footprint running in two or more Regions simultaneously. That roughly multiplies your steady-state compute, storage, and data-transfer footprint, before counting the added operational complexity of keeping two live write paths consistent. If a scenario states an RTO of 24 hours is acceptable, paying for near-zero RTO anyway is not "extra safe" on this exam — it's the wrong answer, because the exam consistently rewards the cheapest architecture that satisfies every stated constraint, never the most resilient one technically possible. That single sentence is worth memorizing verbatim; it resolves more SAA and SAP questions than any single service fact in this course.

THE PATTERN TO INTERNALIZE

"Resilient" and "disaster-recoverable" are not synonyms, and "most resilient" is not "correct" once a scenario states numeric constraints. Read for the failure domain (AZ vs Region) first, then read for the two numbers (RTO, RPO), then — and only then — pick the cheapest tier that clears both bars.

There is a second, quieter version of the same cost mistake that shows up at the Professional level more than the Associate level: choosing a fast-recovery tier for part of an architecture while leaving another part on a slower tier, without checking whether the slower part is actually on the critical path. A multi-site active/active web and application tier wired to a database that only replicates via nightly backup & restore isn't a multi-site active/active design at all — it's a backup & restore design with an expensive, fast-looking front end bolted on. The exam sometimes hides exactly this mismatch inside an otherwise-plausible architecture diagram, and the fix is always the same: find the slowest-recovering component in the chain, because that component's RTO and RPO are the whole system's RTO and RPO, no matter how fast everything else recovers.

THE PATTERN UNDERNEATH ALL FOUR TIERS

Every tier is really just two independent dials turned by different amounts: how much of the data layer stays continuously replicated, and how much of the compute layer stays continuously running. Backup & restore turns both dials to zero. Pilot light turns the data dial up but leaves compute at zero. Warm standby turns both dials up partway. Multi-site active/active turns both dials all the way up, in both directions at once. Once you see the tiers this way, "which tier is this scenario describing" becomes a much smaller question — you're just reading off two dial positions instead of memorizing four names.

ARC 06 · LIMITS, QUOTAS & EDGE CASES

What the checklist still has to respect

The four-step process from §4 gets you the right tier on paper. These are the real-world (and real-exam) edge cases that determine whether it actually works:

  • Cross-region replication is asynchronous almost everywhere. S3 Cross-Region Replication, an RDS cross-region read replica, and DynamoDB global tables all replicate over real network distance with real lag. Even a "continuously replicating" pilot-light or warm-standby design has a nonzero RPO in practice — an exam option promising a flatly zero cross-region RPO with no qualification is usually a trap.
  • An untested DR plan is a hypothesis, not a plan. AWS's own resilience guidance treats regular recovery drills as part of the design, not an optional extra. A scenario that mentions the plan has never been exercised is telling you the current setup is unproven regardless of which tier it claims to be.
  • Pilot light and warm standby both require drift discipline. AMIs, launch templates, and schema changes in the DR region have to be kept in sync with production by hand or by pipeline. Configuration drift — a launch template two versions behind, a migration that never ran in the DR region — is the most common real-world way these tiers quietly fail; the exam sometimes tests whether you spot the missing sync step in a scenario.
  • Restore time scales with data size. "Restore from snapshot" sounds instantaneous; restoring a multi-terabyte database snapshot into a fresh instance is not. Larger data sets push backup & restore's RTO toward the slow end of its range, and that matters when a scenario gives you a specific data volume.
  • DNS failover has its own latency floor. Route 53 health-check-driven failover — the traffic-shifting mechanism underneath warm standby and multi-site designs — depends on the health check detecting failure and on client-side DNS caches expiring. A long time-to-live value or an aggressively caching client adds real minutes to an otherwise near-zero-RTO design; that gap is a favorite Professional-level trap.
  • The two failure domains stack, they don't substitute. A scenario asking for resilience to both an AZ failure and a regional disaster wants Multi-AZ inside each Region and one of these four tiers across Regions — not one instead of the other.
  • Not every AWS service is even regional. IAM, Route 53, and CloudFront are global services with a single control plane spanning all Regions; a "DR plan" for them looks completely different from a DR plan for a regional service like RDS or an EC2 fleet, because there is no second regional copy to fail over to in the first place — the resilience question for global services is usually about the global service's own multi-Region internal design, which you don't control, not about your account's configuration of it.
  • Dependencies fail over, or they don't. A DR-region compute fleet that calls back into a single-Region service — a third-party API pinned to one endpoint, an internal service that was never given a DR-region deployment — inherits that dependency's failure domain no matter how well the rest of the design is built. Tracing every external and internal dependency for "does this exist in the DR Region too" is unglamorous work, and it is exactly the kind of unglamorous work the Professional exam likes to test via a scenario that looks resilient until you check one overlooked arrow on the diagram.
A TRAP WORTH NAMING TWICE

"Our RPO is zero because we replicate continuously" is a sentence to be suspicious of on this exam unless it is qualified. Continuous does not mean instantaneous — it means "small, bounded lag," and that bounded lag is the RPO. Treat any answer option claiming a literal zero cross-region RPO as either a same-Region Multi-AZ scenario in disguise, or a wrong answer.

None of this is a reason to avoid stating a specific target RPO in an actual design — quite the opposite. A well-run team picks a numeric RPO target deliberately, measures real replication lag against it continuously, and alarms when the gap widens, exactly the way §15's simulator lets you check a chosen tier against a target you set. The trap is only in treating "continuous" as a synonym for "zero" without ever measuring the gap.

WHY WARM STANDBY, NOT PILOT LIGHT, HERE

The difference between pilot light and warm standby is entirely about compute: pilot light keeps only data replicated and compute defined as AMIs/launch templates scaled to zero; warm standby keeps a small but running fleet in the DR Region at all times. That running fleet is what shaves warm standby's RTO down to minutes — there's no boot-and-warm-up step, only a scale-up step.

Notice, too, what stays identical across both tiers: the Route 53 health check, the cross-region read replica, and the overall two-Region shape. Moving between adjacent tiers in the decision table from §9 is almost always a matter of dialing one or two components up or down — not redesigning the architecture from scratch — which is exactly why sketching this diagram once and treating the tier choice as a set of dials (per the callout in §5) pays off across an entire exam's worth of resilience questions.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD SCENARIO CASE STUDIES

Three original, exam-shaped scenarios

These are original constructions written in the style the real exam uses — not leaked or real exam questions — to practice the four-step checklist end to end.

Meridian Books — nightly catalog reporting

Profile: a mid-size online bookseller runs a nightly batch reporting pipeline that reconciles the day's sales against inventory. Constraints: the finance team states the pipeline "must resume within 24 hours of a disaster" and "can tolerate losing up to a day's worth of reporting data, since the underlying transactional records are captured elsewhere." Correct choice: backup & restore. Both numbers sit comfortably inside that tier's range, and every pricier tier would be spending money against a requirement that doesn't exist — the classic "don't reach for pilot light or warm standby just because they sound more professional" trap.

Northgate Payments — settlement processing

Profile: a payments company runs card-settlement processing under a contractual SLA. Constraints: the contract requires resuming operations within 30 minutes of a regional disaster and losing no more than 5 minutes of transaction data. Correct choice: warm standby. Pilot light's typical RTO range (tens of minutes to a few hours) risks blowing straight through the 30-minute bound depending on how long the compute layer takes to scale from zero — warm standby's already-running, scaled-down fleet is the cheapest tier that reliably clears both numbers. Multi-site active/active would also clear both numbers, and a candidate reaching for it isn't technically wrong about whether it would work — only about whether it's the cheapest architecture that does, which is the actual question being asked.

Solace Streaming — global live video

Profile: a live-video platform already runs active capacity in two Regions and is evaluating a third for true continuous availability. Constraints: the scenario explicitly states that even a few minutes of regional downtime causes visible, quantified subscriber loss and revenue impact, and that the business has budgeted for it. Correct choice: multi-site active/active. This is the rare case where the fanciest tier is also the correct one — but notice what makes it correct isn't "the workload is important," it's that the scenario explicitly ties a near-zero RTO/RPO requirement to a stated, budgeted business justification. Take either of those two conditions away and the answer would move down the table.

Notice the shape all three vignettes share: the company profile sets the scene, but the actual decision is made entirely from two sentences of stated numbers and one sentence of budget language. Strip away the industry detail from any of these three and the underlying math is identical to the worked example in §4 — which is exactly why the four-step checklist, not domain trivia, is what actually transfers to a real exam question you haven't seen before. If you can re-derive all three verdicts above from the decision table in §9 alone, without re-reading the vignette text, you have genuinely internalized the checklist rather than memorized three answers.

ARC 13 · CLI / CODE REFERENCE

Illustrative CLI you should recognize on sight

You will not be asked to write this syntax from memory on either exam, but you will be asked to recognize what a command block like this is for in a scenario. All syntax below is illustrative AWS CLI v2 shape, not a literal runnable transcript.

disaster-recovery/
├── replicas/
│ ├── ● create-cross-region-read-replica.sh — stand up the DR-region database copy pilot-light/warm-standby depend on
│ └── promote-replica.sh — the "make it a real primary" step during an actual failover
├── routing/
│ └── ● failover-record.json — the Route 53 failover routing policy that redirects traffic on health-check failure
└── backup/
└── ● backup-plan.json — the AWS Backup policy: schedule, retention, cross-region copy
bashcreate a cross-region read replica (illustrative)
# Run against the PRIMARY region's database; --region here targets the DR region.
aws rds create-db-instance-read-replica \
  --db-instance-identifier orders-db-dr-replica \
  --source-db-instance-identifier arn:aws:rds:us-east-1:111122223333:db:orders-db \
  --region us-west-2
bashpromote the replica during an actual failover (illustrative)
# One-way: once promoted, this instance becomes a standalone writable primary.
aws rds promote-read-replica \
  --db-instance-identifier orders-db-dr-replica \
  --region us-west-2
jsonfailover-record.json — Route 53 failover routing (illustrative)
{
  "Comment": "Failover from primary to DR region on health-check failure",
  "Changes": [{
    "Action": "UPSERT",
    "ResourceRecordSet": {
      "Name": "app.example.com",
      "Type": "A",
      "SetIdentifier": "primary",
      "Failover": "PRIMARY",
      "HealthCheckId": "hc-primary-1234",
      "AliasTarget": { "DNSName": "primary-alb.us-east-1.elb.amazonaws.com" }
    }
  }]
}
jsonbackup-plan.json — an AWS Backup plan with cross-region copy (illustrative)
{
  "BackupPlanName": "orders-db-daily",
  "Rules": [{
    "RuleName": "daily-backup",
    "TargetBackupVaultName": "orders-primary-vault",
    "ScheduleExpression": "cron(0 5 * * ? *)",
    "Lifecycle": { "DeleteAfterDays": 35 },
    "CopyActions": [{
      // This is the line that actually gets a copy of the backup out of the primary Region —
      // without it, "we take backups" and "we have a cross-region DR backup" are not the same claim.
      "DestinationBackupVaultArn": "arn:aws:backup:us-west-2:111122223333:backup-vault:orders-dr-vault",
      "Lifecycle": { "DeleteAfterDays": 35 }
    }]
  }]
}
WHY THE EXAM CARES ABOUT THIS

The exam won't quiz raw syntax, but it consistently tests whether you know which mechanism performs a given job: that promotion is a one-way, explicit action (not automatic), that failover routing needs a health check wired to it to be worth anything, and that a backup plan without an explicit cross-region CopyActions entry is still a single-Region backup no matter how good its retention policy looks — a favorite "looks right, one field is missing" distractor. Recognize the shape, not the flags.

GATE · EXAM-DAY TAKEAWAYS

Before chapter 14: what actually earns points

  • RTO and RPO are numbers you extract from scenario text, not vibes — underline them before reading the options.
  • Multi-AZ is not disaster recovery. AZ failure and Region failure are different failure domains with different correct answers.
  • Walk the four tiers cheapest → priciest and stop at the first one that clears both numbers — the exam rewards the cheapest sufficient answer, not the most resilient possible one.
  • Cross-region replication is asynchronous almost everywhere; a promised zero RPO across Regions with no qualification is a trap, not a feature.
  • Layer Multi-AZ with a cross-region tier when a scenario explicitly asks for resilience to both failure domains — they stack, they don't substitute for each other.
  • An architecture is only as resilient as its slowest-recovering component — find the weakest link in the chain before trusting the label on the fastest one.

Chapter 13 complete

You can now pull RTO/RPO out of a scenario, tell AZ-failure language apart from Region-failure language, and walk the four DR tiers to the cheapest sufficient answer. That same discipline — extract the numbers, walk the options cheapest-first, stop at the first one that actually clears every stated bar — is the engine behind most of the Cost-Optimized Architectures domain too. Chapter 14 turns it toward EC2 purchasing options, right-sizing, and the tools AWS gives you to catch runaway spend before the monthly bill does.
