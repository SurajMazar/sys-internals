AWS CERTIFICATION PREP · CHAPTER 24 / 24 · CAPSTONE

Capstone: Design It End to End

Twenty-three chapters gave you a decision framework for one concern at a time — a network, a database, a queue, a purchasing option, a failover tier, a deployment strategy. The real exam question, especially at the Professional level, is never one concern at a time. This chapter builds one company's architecture, piece by piece, against a single stated set of constraints, and computes — for real, not for show — whether each choice you make actually satisfies the requirement it was supposed to satisfy.

You will be able toAssemble a multi-account landing zone, a resilient multi-tier application, an event-driven integration layer, a cost strategy, a DR plan, and a CI/CD strategy as one coherent design instead of six unrelated answers.
You will understandWhy a locally "correct" answer in one area routinely breaks a stated constraint in another — and why the Professional exam is built to catch exactly that.
You will predictWhich of the four real DR tiers, which deployment strategy, and which account structure a stated RTO/RPO and blast-radius requirement actually forces — not which one sounds the most sophisticated.
You will buildA full six-decision architecture in the flagship simulator and get a computed pass/fail against six concrete constraints, with the reasoning shown for every result.
ARC 01 · EXAM CONTEXT

Every domain from both exams, at once

This is the one chapter in the course that deliberately does not map to a single domain, because a capstone scenario is exactly what the Professional exam's hardest questions look like: several domains' worth of constraints layered into one scenario, where the "correct" answer to any single piece depends on the other pieces you've already committed to.

Keep both blueprints in view while you work through this chapter:

  • SAA-C03 (Associate): Design Secure Architectures 30%, Design Resilient Architectures 26%, Design High-Performing Architectures 24%, Design Cost-Optimized Architectures 20%.
  • SAP-C02 (Professional): Design Solutions for Organizational Complexity 26%, Design for New Solutions 29%, Continuous Improvement for Existing Solutions 25%, Accelerate Workload Migration and Modernization 20%.

Notice how the capstone's six building blocks distribute across both lists at once: a landing zone with SCP guardrails is squarely Organizational Complexity; a resilient multi-tier application is Design Resilient Architectures and Design High-Performing Architectures; a decoupled integration layer and a chosen deployment strategy are Design for New Solutions and Continuous Improvement; a purchasing strategy across accounts is Design Cost-Optimized Architectures; and a DR plan matched to a stated RTO/RPO is Design Resilient Architectures again, reappearing under a harder, org-wide lens. A Professional-level candidate is expected to hold all of this in their head simultaneously — the real exam's hardest questions are written exactly this way, stacking a networking constraint, a cost constraint, and a compliance constraint into one paragraph and asking for the single choice that satisfies all three, not the choice that's individually best for one.

ARC 02 · THE COMMON WRONG INSTINCT

Solve each concern in its own room

Given a brief like Solace's, the plausible-sounding first move — the one most candidates and, frankly, most real early-career architects make — is to split the problem into independent workstreams and solve each one to local perfection: a networking engineer designs the "best" VPC layout, a DBA picks the "best" database configuration, a cost analyst finds the "best" purchasing mix, a resilience owner picks the "best" DR tier, and a release engineer picks the "best" deployment strategy. Each answer, graded in isolation, would earn full marks in the chapter that taught it.

The instinct feels efficient — parallelize the work, let each specialist own their slice — and on the exam it shows up as the tempting option that correctly describes a real AWS feature, correctly applied to the sentence right in front of it, while ignoring a constraint stated two sentences earlier in the same scenario. That is exactly the trap the Professional exam is built around: every answer choice on a hard SAP-C02 question is usually individually true; the wrong ones are wrong because they silently violate a constraint from a different domain than the one the question sentence you're focused on belongs to.

ARC 03 · WHY IT'S WRONG

Locally optimal choices routinely break someone else's constraint

Two concrete versions of this, both plausible inside Solace's own scenario:

EXAMPLE 1 — A COST WIN THAT ASSUMES AWAY A GUARDRAIL

Solace's cost team (channeling chapter 14/21's framework) sizes a multi-year Savings Plan commitment against 100% of today's combined Storefront + Fulfillment compute spend, all running in a single approved Region. Six months later, a compliance requirement forces Fulfillment's new EU subsidiary account onto a second Region — a decision the landing-zone SCP now correctly enforces at the OU level (chapter 16), and the cost team wasn't in that meeting. If 25% of Fulfillment's steady-state compute unexpectedly has to run in a Region the commitment doesn't cover, that 25% reverts to full On-Demand pricing, and the fleet-wide blended discount collapses — a plan sized for a ~30% blended discount at 100% coverage effectively pays for only ~75% of the fleet, so the realized blended saving drops by roughly a quarter of what was promised in the budget deck. The Savings Plan itself was correctly designed for the workload the cost team saw. It was never checked against the guardrail the security team was simultaneously tightening.

EXAMPLE 2 — A DR TIER THAT INHERITS A BROKEN DEPLOY

Solace's resilience owner (chapter 13) correctly picks warm standby to hit a stated RTO/RPO. The release engineer, separately, picks in-place deployment (chapter 20) "because it's simplest and fastest to ship." The two choices look unrelated — until a bad deploy corrupts data on the primary. Warm standby's replication has been faithfully copying that same broken state to the standby the entire time, because in-place deployment gives no isolated, health-checked cohort to catch the bad build before it reaches everything, including whatever the DR tier is continuously replicating from. Failing over doesn't help — the standby is a warm copy of the same mistake. The DR framework and the deployment framework were each applied correctly in isolation and are jointly useless together.

Both examples share a mechanism: a decision made against local information silently changes the premise another decision, made elsewhere, was relying on. Multiply that across six areas and the failure mode is not rare — it's the default outcome of treating a whole-architecture problem as six independent ones.

ARC 04 · THE CORRECT APPROACH

A systematic order for whole-architecture design

The fix isn't "communicate more" as a vague platitude — it's a concrete ordering of decisions, so that each later decision is made with the earlier, load-bearing ones already fixed as known constraints rather than as separately-optimized variables. Step through it.

The whole-architecture design methodSTEP-BY-STEP
WHY THIS ORDER, SPECIFICALLY

Each step is placed after the steps whose output it depends on and before the steps that depend on it. Account/network/compute/data come first because every other layer — integration, cost, DR, deployment — is described in terms of accounts, VPCs, and data stores that must already exist as a stated shape. Cost strategy comes after integration and network because a purchasing commitment is a bet on a workload shape that hasn't fully settled until the messaging layer decides how many services actually run, and where. DR validation comes right before — not after — the deployment-strategy choice, because (per §3's second example) the deployment strategy determines whether a DR tier's replicated state can ever be trusted.

ARC 06 · LIMITS & EDGE CASES

What even the correct design still has to respect

Getting the ordering right (§4) and picking the right building block per area (§5, §9) doesn't make the underlying service limits go away. These are the ones this capstone's design is most likely to collide with:

  • SCPs can only restrict, never grant. An SCP attached to the Fulfillment OU cannot hand a role inside it any permission it doesn't already have from an identity or resource policy — it can only narrow the ceiling. If a scenario says "grant this extra permission," the answer is never "add an SCP."
  • Aurora Multi-AZ failover is synchronous and is not a read-scaling mechanism. The standby exists to take over automatically if the primary fails; it does not serve read traffic day to day the way a read replica (asynchronous, promotable, built for read scaling) does. Solace's design needs both concepts kept straight: Multi-AZ answers "survive an AZ failure," not "handle more read traffic."
  • SNS, SQS, and EventBridge each have real, different throughput and ordering characteristics. Standard SQS queues and SNS topics are high-throughput but only best-effort ordered; FIFO queues guarantee strict ordering and exactly-once processing but only within a single message group, which caps how much you can parallelize a single ordered stream. EventBridge adds content-based filtering and multi-producer routing on top of pub/sub, but every one of these is still a real service with real per-account and per-resource quotas — "decoupled" does not mean "unbounded."
  • A DR tier's achievable RTO/RPO is a property of its architecture, not a dial you can turn. Backup & restore cannot be configured to hit a 5-minute RTO no matter how the runbook is written, because restoring from a backup and bringing up fresh infrastructure takes the time it takes. Choosing a DR tier is choosing an RTO/RPO envelope, not requesting one.
  • Multi-account isolation has a cost of its own. Every workload account under the landing zone adds real operational surface — its own guardrails to verify, its own CI/CD role to trust, its own billing line to reconcile — and an organization that spins up accounts faster than it can govern them has traded one failure mode (shared blast radius) for another (ungoverned sprawl).

solace-landing-zone/ — illustrative layoutREPO TREE
solace-landing-zone/ ├── organizations/ │  ├── ous.yaml  # Security, Shared-Services, Storefront, Fulfillment OUs │  └── scps/ │      ├── deny-region-outside-allowlist.json │      └── deny-leave-organization.json ├── accounts/ │  ├── storefront-prod/ │  │   ├── network.yaml  # VPC, public + private subnets, NAT │  │   ├── app-tier.yaml  # ALB + ASG + launch template │  │   └── database.yaml  # Aurora, Multi-AZ │  └── fulfillment-prod/  # same three files, own account ├── integration/ │  ├── eventbridge-bus.yaml │  └── rules/order-placed.json └── pipelines/     ├── buildspec.yml     └── appspec-canary.yml
JSONscps/deny-region-outside-allowlist.json — illustrative SCP
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [{
    "Sid": "DenyOutsideApprovedRegions",
    "Effect": "Deny",
    "NotAction": ["iam:*", "organizations:*", "support:*"],
    "Resource": "*",
    "Condition": { "StringNotEquals": { "aws:RequestedRegion": ["us-east-1", "us-west-2"] } }
  }]
}

This SCP restricts — it can only narrow what every account under the OU it's attached to is allowed to do, never grant a permission an identity policy doesn't already give. Attached at the root or a top-level OU, it applies to every current and future account underneath, which is precisely why it's the correct guardrail mechanism instead of relying on every team to remember a regional convention.

YAMLaccounts/storefront-prod/app-tier.yaml — illustrative IaC excerpt
# Illustrative — not a deployable template
Resources:
  StorefrontALB:
    Type: AWS::ElasticLoadBalancingV2::LoadBalancer
    Properties: { Scheme: internet-facing, Subnets: PublicSubnetIds }
  StorefrontASG:
    Type: AWS::AutoScaling::AutoScalingGroup
    Properties:
      VPCZoneIdentifier: PrivateSubnetIds  # spans 2+ AZs
      TargetGroupARNs: [StorefrontTG]
      MinSize: 2, MaxSize: 10
  StorefrontAurora:
    Type: AWS::RDS::DBCluster
    Properties: { Engine: aurora-mysql, MultiAZ: true }
JSONintegration/rules/order-placed.json — illustrative EventBridge rule
{
  "source": ["solace.storefront"],
  "detail-type": ["OrderPlaced"],
  "detail": { "fulfillmentRegion": ["us-east-1", "us-west-2"] }
}

Storefront's account publishes; it never needs to know that Fulfillment (a different account) or a future third consumer exists — content-based filtering on the detail block is what lets one event bus serve many independent, decoupled subscribers (chapter 11's SNS/EventBridge framework, now crossing an account boundary).

YAMLpipelines/appspec-canary.yml — illustrative CodeDeploy config
version: 0.0
DeploymentConfig: CodeDeployDefault.LambdaCanary10Percent5Minutes
Hooks:
  - AfterAllowTraffic: "CheckCanaryErrorRateAlarm"  # CloudWatch alarm gates promotion
# On alarm breach: CodeDeploy shifts traffic back automatically — no manual rollback step
WHY THE EXAM CARES

The SAP-C02 exam does not test whether you can write this YAML — it tests whether you recognize which mechanism a scenario is describing when it says "automatically shift traffic back if errors increase" (canary + alarm-triggered rollback) versus "swap the entire environment with one clean cutover and an instant full rollback path" (blue/green) versus "an event bus filters by content so publishers don't need to know their consumers" (EventBridge). Recognize the mechanism from the plain-English description; the exact CLI/YAML syntax is not what's being graded.

GATE · KNOWLEDGE CHECK

The whole course, reflected on

These have no multiple choice, and they don't point at a next chapter — there isn't one. Formulate a full answer in your head (or out loud), then reveal.

Before that: the five things this capstone was really about, compressed. One — every one of the course's per-topic decision frameworks (Multi-AZ vs read replicas, SQS vs SNS vs EventBridge, the four DR tiers, in-place vs blue/green vs canary, On-Demand vs Reserved vs Spot) is a genuine, real AWS decision rule, and every one of them still has to be re-checked against every other framework's fixed decisions before it's actually correct for a whole system. Two — blast-radius isolation is not a feeling, it's a structural property: an SCP-backed account boundary plus a decoupled integration layer, not a promise written in a runbook. Three — a DR tier's RTO/RPO is bounded by its architecture, and the cheapest-sufficient tier is always the correct exam answer, never the most sophisticated-sounding one. Four — a deployment strategy and a DR strategy are not independent: whichever one lets bad state propagate silently defeats whatever the other one promised. Five — the exam's hardest questions, and this capstone's hardest design decisions, are both won the same way: restate every explicit constraint before picking a single answer, because the wrong options are almost always individually true statements about AWS that simply ignore a constraint from two sentences earlier.
