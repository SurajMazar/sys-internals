WHAT'S DELIBERATELY NOT ON THIS DIAGRAM

There is no global channel-balance registry, no central router, and no on-chain record of who paid whom through a channel — only that a channel with some total capacity was opened and, eventually, closed with some final split. Every payment in between is known only to the parties directly involved in each hop. This is a privacy property paid for with a UX cost you will feel directly in §10 and §15: nobody, including the paying node, can be certain a route works until they try it, because the one thing the network deliberately does not gossip is each channel's directional available liquidity.

ARC 08 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

The bytes, the keys, and the state machine, in order

Four pieces, built in the order a real channel needs them: (a) the commitment transaction's exact output layout, (b) the revocation key/secret machinery — including the storage trick that makes it practical, (c) HTLCs as the primitive that makes a multi-hop payment atomic, (d) the onion packet that lets a route exist without any hop seeing it.

(a) The commitment transaction: two outputs become up to 2 + 2·N_htlc

Every commitment transaction spends the single funding outpoint and pays out according to BOLT #3. In its simplest form (no in-flight HTLCs) it has exactly two outputs, and they are not symmetric between the two versions Alice and Bob each hold:

  • to_local — pays the owner of this copy of the commitment, but only after a relative delay to_self_delay (typically hundreds of blocks, negotiated at open) enforced by OP_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY — unless the spend instead reveals the revocation key for this exact commitment, in which case the counterparty can take it immediately, no delay.
  • to_remote — pays the counterparty directly, immediately spendable (a plain P2WPKH output, one confirmation and it's theirs) — since it is not this party's own delayed output, there is nothing to punish there.

Because to_local in Alice's copy pays Alice-with-a-delay while to_remote in that same copy pays Bob-immediately, and Bob's copy is the mirror image, the two parties are holding different transactions that nonetheless encode the identical balance split. That asymmetry is precisely the requirement derived in §6: only the broadcaster's own funds are ever delayed, which is what gives the other party a window to react.

THE OBSCURED COMMITMENT NUMBER — A REAL BOLT #3 DETAIL WORTH KNOWING

A channel's commitment transactions must all spend the same funding outpoint, so they'd normally be trivially linkable and orderable by anyone who saw two of them. BOLT #3 hides the sequence number: the 48-bit commitment index is XORed with a value derived from both parties' payment_basepoints (SHA256(open_payment_basepoint || accept_payment_basepoint), lower 48 bits), and the result is split across the transaction's nLockTime (lower 24 bits, with the upper byte forced to 0x20 so parsers don't mistake it for a real locktime) and the input's nSequence (lower 24 bits, with the top bits forced to mark it non-final). An outside observer sees two numbers that look like protocol noise; only the two channel parties can recover which commitment number produced them — a small, characteristic Lightning move: hide metadata inside fields the base layer already validates for something else, rather than adding new fields that would need a soft fork.

(b) Revocation: one basepoint, one per-commitment secret, and a tree that stores neither in full

Deriving a fresh throwaway key pair for every single commitment and remembering each one individually is the §6 cost made concrete. BOLT #3 avoids it with two linked constructions.

First, the revocation key itself is not a random per-state secret — it's a combination. Each party publishes a long-term revocation_basepoint once, at channel open. For commitment number i, the actual key that can sweep a revoked to_local output is derived by tweaking that basepoint with the counterparty's per-commitment secret for state i — elliptic-curve point/scalar arithmetic from chapter 4, applied so that knowing the basepoint alone reveals nothing, but basepoint + the one secret for state i reveals exactly the key needed to punish state i, and nothing about any other state.

Second — the storage trick. Rather than remember every per-commitment secret handed over, BOLT #3 Appendix D specifies shachain: secrets are generated from one 32-byte seed by a deterministic bit-flip procedure, so a party who once held the seed can regenerate any past secret on demand, and a party who receives secrets (and must give up the seed-holder's ability to regenerate, since revealing a secret must not reveal the seed) needs to retain at most 47 secrets to derive all 2⁴⁸ possible ones below any given index. (c) HTLCs: the primitive that makes a stranger-to-stranger payment atomic

  
Revocable commitments solve cheating within one channel. They say nothing about paying someone you have
  no channel with at all — which, in a network where opening a direct channel with every counterparty is exactly
  the on-chain cost explosion §1 ruled out, is the common case. You need to route through intermediaries you do not
  trust, without ever letting an intermediary either steal the funds or take a fee without actually forwarding.

  
Derive the mechanism from the requirement. Carol (the final recipient) generates a random 32-byte secret
  R (the preimage) and gives Alice (the sender) only its hash H = SHA-256(R), packaged as a
  BOLT11 invoice. Alice cannot pay Carol directly — no channel — but she has a channel with Bob, who has a channel
  with Carol. Alice offers Bob a conditional payment: "this output is yours if you reveal a preimage of H within
  144 blocks; otherwise it reverts to me." This is a Hash Time-Locked Contract — a special HTLC output on
  the commitment transaction, not a separate transaction. Bob cannot claim it without knowing R, and only Carol
  knows R. So Bob extends the identical offer to Carol — same hash H, but a shorter timeout, say 100 blocks.
  Carol, who has R, claims Bob's HTLC immediately by revealing R on their shared commitment update. Bob now holds
  R. He turns around and uses that same R to claim Alice's HTLC before her 144-block timeout. Money moved
  Alice → Bob → Carol, Bob never had to be trusted with anything except forwarding correctly, and if he tries to
  claim from Alice without paying Carol, he cannot — he doesn't have R until Carol reveals it, and Carol has no
  reason to reveal it without being paid.

WHY THE TIMEOUT MUST STRICTLY DECREASE TOWARD THE DESTINATION

    
Bob only turns his knowledge of R into a claim against Alice after Carol reveals it to him. If Bob's
    outgoing timeout to Carol expired at the same block height as his incoming timeout from Alice, a slow or
    congested mempool could let Alice's HTLC time out and revert to her before Bob finishes claiming from
    Carol and turning around — leaving Bob having paid Carol out of his own pocket with no way to recover it from
    Alice. Each hop therefore subtracts a safety margin, the cltv_expiry_delta, from the timeout it
    received before offering it onward — commonly tens of blocks per hop in real deployments (implementation
    defaults vary; on the order of 40 blocks is typical, with the final recipient's own minimum, BOLT11's
    min_final_cltv_expiry_delta, commonly around 18). The direct cost: a route's total worst-case
    lock time is the sum of every hop's delta, so a 6-hop route can tie up Alice's outbound HTLC for several times
    longer than a 1-hop route, even though the payment itself settles in milliseconds — capital that is committed,
    not spent, for the entire timeout window if something upstream stalls.

  


  

    
THE PRIVACY LEAK HTLCs CANNOT FIX — AND WHAT PTLCs DO ABOUT IT

    
Every hop's HTLC uses the identical hash H — it has to, or Bob couldn't prove to Carol he's forwarding the
    same payment Alice is funding. That means any two nodes who both happen to be on the route (or who collude, or
    who simply watch enough of the network) can correlate "an HTLC with hash H passed through me and also through
    you" and infer they're part of the same payment, even without knowing the sender or recipient. Chapter 13's
    Taproot machinery offers a fix: replace the hash-lock with a point-time-locked contract (PTLC), where
    each hop's condition is "produce a valid Schnorr signature under this specific curve point," built with an
    adaptor signature. The point Carol reveals to complete the payment can be randomized independently at
    every hop via the same key-blinding math MuSig2 uses for aggregation, so no single correlating value crosses
    the whole route. PTLCs also compress naturally — an adaptor-signature spend looks identical to a normal
    single-sig spend on-chain, unlike an HTLC's distinctive hash-and-timelock script. As of this writing PTLC
    support is experimental in the major implementations, not yet a deployed default — an honest case of "the fix
    is designed and mostly prototyped, not yet how payments actually route."

  


  
(d) Sphinx onion routing: a fixed 1,300-byte envelope, no matter how many hops

  
Even with PTLCs fixing the hash-correlation leak, a route is still useless privacy-wise if each hop can see
  the entire path or count how many hops remain. BOLT #4 adapts the Sphinx packet format (Danezis &
  Goldberg, 2009) so that every intermediary sees only: the immediate next hop, the amount and timeout to forward,
  and cryptographic material it cannot use to learn anything else. The construction Alice builds is layered
  encryption — she encrypts Carol's instructions, then Bob's instructions wrapping that, using a fresh shared
  secret with each hop derived via Diffie–Hellman from a single ephemeral key she generates once. Each hop peels
  exactly one layer and cannot see what's inside the next.

  
The field that makes this actually hide the route length is fixed-size padding. Drag the hop count below — the

WHAT EACH HOP ACTUALLY LEARNS — AND CANNOT LEARN

    
Bob, forwarding for Alice, learns: the previous hop (Alice, since she's his direct peer), the next hop
    (Carol), the amount to forward, and the CLTV to set. He cannot learn: how many hops came before Alice, how many
    come after Carol, or the total route length — the padding is bitwise indistinguishable from a real, still-onion-
    wrapped next layer. A per-hop HMAC lets each node detect tampering (a modified packet fails the check and the
    payment fails cleanly) without revealing what was tampered with. This is why a failed payment in Lightning
    returns an encrypted error, onion-wrapped back through the same path Alice built — only Alice, holding
    the original shared secrets, can decrypt which hop reported the failure and why (§10 returns to this).
