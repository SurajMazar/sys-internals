APPLIED CRYPTOGRAPHY INTERNALS · CHAPTER 01

Genesis: Confidentiality
Before Computers

Long before a transistor existed, two parties who could not meet in private still needed to exchange a message that a hostile third party could read but not understand. Every idea in this course — block ciphers, AEAD, RSA, TLS — is a modern answer to a problem that is centuries old. This chapter derives cryptography's fundamental vocabulary from first principles using ciphers you can compute by hand: substitution, transposition, Vigenère's polyalphabetic scheme, its Kasiski-examination break, the Enigma, and the one classical construction that is mathematically unbreakable — the one-time pad.

ARC 01 · THE PROBLEM

Two people, one hostile channel

Strip cryptography down to its irreducible scenario, the one that has been identical since antiquity and remains identical today: Alice wants to send Bob a message over a channel that Eve can read. The channel might be a courier who can be captured, a wax-sealed letter that can be steamed open, a radio transmission anyone with an antenna can intercept, or a TCP/IP packet traversing routers Alice and Bob don't control. The physics of the channel changes constantly across 2,500 years; the adversary model does not.

Unpack "confidentiality over a hostile channel" and four requirements fall out immediately, and each one will reappear, formalized, for the rest of this course:

  • Confidentiality — Eve, observing the transmitted message in full, must learn nothing about its content.
  • A shared secret — Alice and Bob must agree, in advance and out of Eve's view, on something Eve does not know — a key — because if Eve knows everything Alice and Bob know, no transformation of the message can hide it from her.
  • Efficient, repeatable transformation — encryption and decryption must be procedures Alice and Bob can actually execute (by hand, by machine, by CPU) fast enough to be useful, using the shared secret.
  • An adversary who gets the algorithm for free — Eve may eventually learn how the scheme works (through espionage, reverse engineering, or publication), so the scheme's security cannot depend on that mechanism staying secret — only the key can.

That fourth requirement has a name — Kerckhoffs's principle, formulated by the Dutch cryptographer Auguste Kerckhoffs in his 1883 essay La Cryptographie Militaire — and it is the single most important idea in this chapter. Every cipher below will be judged by one question: assume Eve knows the algorithm completely and only the key is secret — how much work does she now need to recover the plaintext? Ciphers that only look strong when their mechanism stays hidden are not cryptography; they are "security through obscurity," and this chapter's central historical arc — clever substitution schemes rising, and falling to systematic mathematical attack — is a 500-year demonstration of exactly why that approach always eventually fails.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE NEXT 15 CHAPTERS

Every cipher in this course — AES (ch. 3), ChaCha20 (ch. 4), RSA (ch. 9) — is a more rigorous answer to exactly the four requirements above. The vocabulary you build now — key, keyspace, plaintext, ciphertext, cryptanalysis, Kerckhoffs's principle — is not historical color; it is the load-bearing terminology the rest of the course assumes you already have cold.

DEEP CONTEXT · WHY THE 300-YEAR GAP BETWEEN VIGENÈRE AND ITS BREAK MATTERS

The cipher this chapter centers on was considered unbreakable — nicknamed le chiffre indéchiffrable, "the indecipherable cipher" — for roughly three centuries after its 1586 publication, until Babbage's private 1854 work and Kasiski's public 1863 method. That gap is not evidence the cipher was strong; it is evidence that nobody with the right mathematical tool (systematic frequency statistics applied to repeating-key structure) had focused on it. Confusing "nobody has broken this yet" with "this cannot be broken" is the single most common category error in applied cryptography, and it recurs — RC4 (ch. 4), MD5, WEP, and Dual_EC_DRBG (ch. 14) all enjoyed years of unearned trust before the math caught up with the marketing.

ARC 03 · NAIVE SOLUTION

Attempt #1: shift every letter by the same amount

Derive the simplest possible scheme honestly, the way a smart engineer with no cryptographic training would. Pick a number k between 1 and 25. To encrypt, shift every letter of the alphabet forward by k positions, wrapping Z back to A. To decrypt, shift backward by the same k. This is the Caesar cipher — Suetonius's Life of the Deified Julius reports that Julius Caesar used exactly this scheme, with k=3, for military correspondence.

pseudocodecaesar.txt — the strawman, annotated
function caesarEncrypt(plaintext, k):
    ciphertext = ""
    for each letter c in uppercase(plaintext):
        i = alphabet_index(c)                  // A=0 .. Z=25
        ciphertext += alphabet[(i + k) mod 26]
    return ciphertext

// HELLO, k=3  →  KHOOR
// decrypting is the same shift, negated: (i - k) mod 26

It is not a joke architecture, and it gets the shared-secret requirement from §1 technically right: Alice and Bob agree on k in advance, Eve does not know it, and encryption/decryption are trivial to compute by hand — genuinely practical for a general dictating orders in the field with no equipment beyond a memorized number. The obvious "improvement," and the next naive attempt worth taking seriously, generalizes the idea: instead of a uniform shift, pick any permutation of the 26 letters as the substitution — a full monoalphabetic substitution cipher. Now there isn't one key out of 25; there are as many keys as there are permutations of 26 symbols. Surely that is enough.

ARC 04 · WHY IT FAILS

26! keys and it still falls in seconds

Count the keyspace honestly before judging it. A Caesar shift has only 25 non-identity keys — trivial to try by hand in under a minute, which is why nobody would call it secure today. But a general monoalphabetic substitution — any bijection from 26 letters to 26 letters — has:

THE KEYSPACE LOOKS ENORMOUS

26! = 403,291,461,126,605,635,584,000,000 ≈ 4.03 × 1026 possible keys — roughly log2(26!) ≈ 88.4 bits of raw keyspace. For comparison, that is a larger keyspace than a 56-bit DES key (ch. 2) by a factor of over 500 million. By the "just count the keys" intuition that a naive engineer reaches for, this cipher should be spectacularly secure.

It is broken in seconds by hand, and the reason is the chapter's first fundamental lesson: a substitution cipher does not hide the statistical shape of the language — it only relabels it. English letters are not drawn uniformly; E appears roughly 12.7% of the time, T roughly 9.1%, A roughly 8.2%, while Z, Q, and X each appear well under 0.2%. A monoalphabetic substitution maps that exact frequency distribution onto a different set of 26 letters — unchanged in shape, just shuffled in label. The technique that exploits this — counting letter frequencies in ciphertext and matching the shape against known-language statistics — is frequency analysis, first described in writing by the Arab polymath Al-Kindi in his 9th-century manuscript A Manuscript on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages, roughly 700 years before Europe caught up.

THE NUMBERS THAT MATTER

Keyspace size (88.4 bits) measures how hard brute force is — trying every key. Frequency analysis isn't brute force; it never tries a single key from the 4×1026-key space. It directly reads the shape of the ciphertext's letter distribution, matches the most frequent ciphertext letter to E, the second-most-frequent to T, and so on, then uses short high-frequency words (THE, AND) to resolve the remaining ambiguity. A message of a few hundred letters is routinely enough. The lesson: keyspace is necessary for security, but nowhere near sufficient — a cipher must also destroy the statistical structure of the input, not merely relabel it.

So the naive fix — bigger keyspace, same mechanism — doesn't work. What if, instead of substituting each letter with the same replacement every time, we changed the substitution as we moved through the message?

ARC 05 · BETTER SOLUTION

Polyalphabetic substitution: let the key repeat instead of the alphabet

The idea, independently converged on by several 16th-century cryptographers (Giovan Battista Bellaso published an early version in 1553; Blaise de Vigenère's 1586 treatise Traicté des Chiffres is the version whose name stuck) is to use not one Caesar shift but a whole sequence of them, chosen by a repeating keyword. Every letter of the keyword names a different shift; the keyword repeats for as long as the message runs. The same plaintext letter, appearing twice, will very often encrypt to two different ciphertext letters — because each occurrence lands under a different letter of the repeating key. That single property is precisely what breaks frequency analysis: the ciphertext's letter-frequency shape is smeared across up to len(key) different shifted distributions, and the elegant "count letters, match to English" attack from §4 no longer has a single shape to match against.

Encrypting ATTACKATDAWN with key LEMONSTEP-BY-STEP
THE MECHANISM, PRECISELY

Assign A=0 … Z=25. For plaintext letter Pi and the repeating key's letter at that position Ki, the ciphertext letter is Ci = (Pi + Ki) mod 26. Decryption inverts it: Pi = (Ci − Ki + 26) mod 26. This is exactly a Caesar shift whose amount is re-chosen, letter by letter, by the key — modular addition over the alphabet, which is the same algebraic idea (a group operation, applied position-wise) you will see generalized to XOR over bits with stream ciphers in chapter 4.

With a key as long as the keyword LEMON (5 letters), the ciphertext's overall letter distribution is now an average of 5 different shifted copies of the English frequency curve — visibly flatter, and no longer a simple match to any single reference distribution. For three centuries, that flattening was mistaken for unbreakability.

ARC 06 · WHY THAT STILL FAILS

The key repeats — and repetition is a fingerprint

Flattening the overall frequency distribution does not make each column of the cipher secure — it only hides them by mixing them together. If you knew the key length L, you could split the ciphertext into L separate streams (every L-th letter), and each stream is just a plain Caesar shift, breakable by the exact frequency analysis from §4. The entire attack, formalized independently by Charles Babbage (worked out privately around 1854, never published — Crimean War secrecy is one hypothesis) and published openly by Prussian officer Friedrich Kasiski in his 1863 book Die Geheimschriften und die Dechiffrir-Kunst, reduces to one question: how do you find the key length without being told it?

Kasiski's insight: if the same short sequence of plaintext letters happens to line up with the same phase of the repeating key twice, it produces the same ciphertext sequence twice — and the distance between those two repeats must be a multiple of the key length. THE COMPLICATION THE TEXTBOOKS OFTEN SKIP

Raw "which factor appears most often" voting is biased toward small numbers: any distance that is a multiple of the true key length L is automatically also a multiple of every divisor of L — so 2 and 3 tend to out-vote the real answer whenever L is even or divisible by 3. Real cryptanalysts resolve the ambiguity with a second, independent statistic: the Index of Coincidence (defined precisely in §8), which directly measures whether a candidate-length column looks like monoalphabetic English rather than a mix of several shifts. §15's simulator implements both, genuinely, and shows you exactly this ambiguity on real computed data before resolving it.

Once the key length is known with confidence, each of the L columns is an ordinary Caesar cipher — §4's chi-squared frequency match recovers each column's shift independently, and the assembled shifts spell the key. Le chiffre indéchiffrable falls to arithmetic anyone in this course already knows.

THE PATTERN TO CARRY FORWARD

Every step in this progression attacked the same weakness: a key that is shorter than the message, reused across it. Caesar reuses a 1-letter key across the whole message. Vigenère reuses an L-letter key. Enigma's rotors create an enormously longer effective period before repeating (on the order of 17,576 positions before the fastest rotor's cycle realigns with the others) but it is still, mathematically, a repeating-key polyalphabetic cipher — which is exactly the structural property Polish and British cryptanalysts exploited (§11). The one-time pad is the only construction on this board whose key never repeats, not even once, for messages of any length. Chapter 4's stream ciphers and chapter 8's key-derivation functions exist entirely to approximate the one-time pad's guarantee using a short, practical key — and to manage exactly what happens when that approximation's assumptions are violated.

ARC 08 · INTERNAL IMPLEMENTATION

Real machinery: modular arithmetic, rotor permutations, and the statistics that break them

Now the level of detail you'd need to actually implement and attack these schemes. Three pieces: (a) the Vigenère cipher as pure modular arithmetic (already shown in §5 — this section formalizes the statistics used to attack it), (b) the Enigma's rotor wiring as function composition over permutations, and (c) the two statistics — chi-squared goodness-of-fit and the Index of Coincidence — that do the actual cryptanalytic work.

(a) The two statistics that break monoalphabetic and polyalphabetic ciphers

Chi-squared goodness-of-fit scores how well an assumed key-letter shift explains an observed column of ciphertext. For each of the 26 candidate shifts k, "undo" the shift on every letter in the column, count how often each letter A–Z appears, and compare that observed count to the count you'd expect if the column were real English (letter frequency × column length). The shift that minimizes Σ (observed − expected)² / expected across all 26 letters is, overwhelmingly likely, the true key letter — because only the correct shift un-scrambles the ciphertext back into English's actual, very non-uniform, frequency shape.

Index of Coincidence (IC) answers a different question: does this stretch of text look like it was produced by one fixed substitution, or by several mixed together? For a text of length N with letter counts fA…fZ, IC = Σ fi(fi−1) / [N(N−1)] — essentially, the probability that two letters picked at random from the text are identical. Real English has an IC around 0.066 (because English's skewed letter frequencies make coincidental matches more likely than chance); a uniform, random-looking stream has an IC around 1/26 ≈ 0.0385. Splitting Vigenère ciphertext into L columns and averaging their IC gives a sharp signal: at the true key length (and any of its multiples), every column is monoalphabetic English, so the average IC jumps back up near 0.066; at any other length, columns mix multiple key letters and the IC stays close to random. §15 computes this live, on real ciphertext, and you will see the exact peak.

(b) The Enigma: permutation composition, made physical

The Enigma machine, patented by German engineer Arthur Scherbius in 1918 and adopted by the German military through the 1920s–40s, is a polyalphabetic cipher built from hardware: pressing a key sends an electrical signal through a plugboard, then through three (later four) rotors, each wired as a fixed permutation of the alphabet, into a reflector that sends the signal back through the same rotors in reverse, and finally lights a lamp. Each keypress also advances the rightmost rotor by one position — a mechanical odometer — so the very next letter uses a different composed permutation. A REAL STRUCTURAL WEAKNESS, VISIBLE IN THE TABLE ABOVE

Watch the substitution table at any rotor position: no letter ever maps to itself. That is not this simulator cutting a corner — it is a real, physically forced property of every Enigma, a direct consequence of the reflector wiring signal back through a different path than it entered. Bletchley Park's cryptanalysts used exactly this fact as a crib-placement filter: if a suspected plaintext word, slid to a given position under the ciphertext, ever required a letter to encrypt to itself, that alignment was provably wrong and could be discarded instantly — turning an astronomically large search into a tractable one. Kerckhoffs's principle in action: this weakness lives in the machine's design, not any particular day's key, so knowing the design (which the Allies did, from Polish reconstruction work before the war) exposed it for every message, forever.

The one-time pad is the single point on this chart that sidesteps the entire argument, by refusing to let the key ever repeat. That single design choice, and its practical cost, is where the chapter's mathematics gets sharpest.

WHERE THE ONE-TIME PAD DOESN'T EVEN FIT ON THIS CHART

A one-time pad's keyspace for an N-letter message is 26N — exactly as large as the space of all possible N-letter messages. It is not merely "very large" on the scale above; it is a category different from every other bar, because it scales with the message you're trying to protect, not with a fixed key length someone chose in advance. That is precisely the property Claude Shannon proved, in 1949, is necessary and sufficient for what he called perfect secrecy — the formal target the rest of this course approximates practically. §15's simulator lets you feel the consequence directly: no amount of frequency analysis, Kasiski examination, or raw computation recovers so much as one bit of the plaintext, because (with a truly random, never-reused key) every possible plaintext of that length is equally consistent with the observed ciphertext.

ARC 11 · REAL-WORLD INCIDENTS

When a broken cipher was a matter of life and death

Incident 1: The Babington Plot, 1586 — a cipher's failure ends in an execution

Mary, Queen of Scots, corresponded with conspirators plotting to assassinate Elizabeth I using a nomenclator cipher — symbol substitutions for letters plus code-words for common terms. Sir Francis Walsingham's cryptanalyst Thomas Phelippes broke the scheme and, critically, was skilled enough to forge an addition in Mary's own cipher asking the conspirators to name their accomplices — all while the letters continued flowing through what Mary believed was a secure channel. The decrypted (and partially forged) correspondence was used as evidence at her 1586 trial; she was executed the following year. Root cause: the cipher's designer trusted a fixed nomenclator's obscurity rather than a provably strong, regularly-changing key — exactly the Kerckhoffs violation from §1, with the highest possible stakes.

Incident 2: The Confederacy's reused Vigenère keywords, 1861–65

The Confederate States Army's Signal Corps used the Vigenère cipher during the American Civil War, but with a critical operational flaw: it relied on a small, fixed set of keywords — phrases like MANCHESTER BLUFF, COMPLETE VICTORY, and, in the war's final months, the defiantly-named COME RETRIBUTION — reused across enormous volumes of traffic for months at a time. Union cryptanalysts, working manually, could apply exactly the Kasiski-style analysis of §6 against the accumulated traffic and frequently read Confederate dispatches. Root cause: a mathematically sound cipher, undermined by an operational failure to rotate keys — a pattern that recurs constantly in this course (weak nonce reuse in ch. 5 and 7 is the modern descendant of exactly this mistake).

Incident 3: Bletchley Park breaks the Enigma, 1939–1945

Polish Cipher Bureau mathematician Marian Rejewski first reconstructed the German military Enigma's internal wiring using permutation-group theory in 1932 — years before the war — and Poland shared that work with Britain and France in July 1939, weeks before the invasion of Poland. At Bletchley Park, Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman extended the Polish "bomba" concept into the electromechanical Bombe, which exploited exactly the structural weakness shown in §8 — no letter ever enciphers to itself — combined with predictable message content ("cribs": stereotyped weather-report headers, standardized sign-offs) to eliminate wrong rotor/plugboard settings at scale. Operator errors compounded the design weakness: reused message keys, lazy near-keyboard settings, and predictable daily patterns ("cillies") gave cryptanalysts a running supply of cribs. The resulting intelligence, codenamed Ultra, is widely credited by historians with shortening the war. Root cause, in this chapter's vocabulary: Enigma is fundamentally a polyalphabetic substitution cipher with an enormous but still-finite, still-structured period — and structure, once understood, is exactly what systematic cryptanalysis is built to exploit.

THE THROUGH-LINE

All three incidents share one root cause, stated three different ways: a cryptosystem was trusted beyond what its actual mathematical properties justified. Phelippes exploited a static nomenclator. Union cryptanalysts exploited keyword reuse. Bletchley Park exploited rotor determinism plus human operational sloppiness. Every subsequent chapter's "real-world incidents" section is a modern retelling of this same lesson against AES, RSA, and TLS.

ARC 13 · CODE WALKTHROUGH

Reading the reference implementation: a small classical-crypto toolkit

There is no OpenSSL module for the Caesar cipher — these constructions predate every cryptographic library on Earth by centuries. What follows is precise reference pseudocode, structured the way a real cryptanalysis toolkit is structured: ciphers (how to encrypt/decrypt) are kept strictly separate from cryptanalysis (how to attack a ciphertext without the key) — the same separation of concerns you'll find in real security codebases, where "implement the algorithm" and "attack the algorithm" are different files, often different teams, because conflating them is how implementation bugs hide.

The core cipher — everything else in this chapter's mathematics reduces to this function pair:

JSciphers/vigenere.js
const AL = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ";
function clean(s) { return s.toUpperCase().replace(/[^A-Z]/g, ""); }

function encrypt(plaintext, key) {
  const p = clean(plaintext), k = clean(key);
  let out = "";
  for (let i = 0; i < p.length; i++) {
    const pi = p.charCodeAt(i) - 65;             // A=0..Z=25
    const ki = k.charCodeAt(i % k.length) - 65;  // key repeats: i % key.length
    out += AL[(pi + ki) % 26];
  }
  return out;
}
function decrypt(ciphertext, key) {
  const c = clean(ciphertext), k = clean(key);
  let out = "";
  for (let i = 0; i < c.length; i++) {
    const ci = c.charCodeAt(i) - 65;
    const ki = k.charCodeAt(i % k.length) - 65;
    out += AL[(ci - ki + 26) % 26];             // +26 keeps JS's % non-negative
  }
  return out;
}

The attack side. Note it never receives the key — that's the entire point:

JScryptanalysis/kasiski.js + ioc.js (condensed)
function kasiskiExamination(cipher) {
  const positions = {};                          // trigram -> [indices where it occurs]
  for (let i = 0; i <= cipher.length - 3; i++)
    (positions[cipher.substr(i, 3)] ??= []).push(i);

  const votes = {};                              // factor -> how many repeat-distances it divides
  for (const seq in positions) {
    const p = positions[seq];
    if (p.length < 2) continue;              // no repeat, no signal
    for (let a = 0; a < p.length; a++)
      for (let b = a+1; b < p.length; b++) {
        const dist = p[b] - p[a];
        for (let f = 2; f <= 20; f++)
          if (dist % f === 0) votes[f] = (votes[f]||0) + 1;
      }
  }
  return votes;                                  // small factors will over-vote; cross-check with IC below
}

function indexOfCoincidence(letters) {
  const N = letters.length, counts = {};
  for (const ch of letters) counts[ch] = (counts[ch]||0) + 1;
  let num = 0;
  for (const c in counts) num += counts[c] * (counts[c]-1);
  return N < 2 ? 0 : num / (N * (N-1));    // English ≈ 0.066, uniform ≈ 0.0385
}

function guessKeyLength(cipher, maxLen=20, threshold=0.06) {
  for (let L = 1; L <= maxLen; L++) {
    const cols = Array.from({length:L}, ()=> "");
    for (let i = 0; i < cipher.length; i++) cols[i % L] += cipher[i];
    const avgIC = cols.map(indexOfCoincidence).reduce((a,b)=>a+b,0) / L;
    if (avgIC >= threshold) return L;             // smallest L whose columns look monoalphabetic
  }
  return -1;                                    // no periodic structure found — see §15's OTP run
}
WHY IT WAS BUILT THIS WAY

1. The attack never touches the key. kasiskiExamination and guessKeyLength take only ciphertext — enforcing, in the code's own signature, the same discipline Kerckhoffs's principle demands of the attacker's model. 2. "Smallest L that crosses the threshold," not "largest vote." Both the Kasiski factor votes and the IC scan can show elevated signal at multiples of the true key length (12, 18… for a true length of 6) — taking the smallest passing candidate, not the strongest one, is what correctly isolates the fundamental period rather than a harmonic of it. 3. `guessKeyLength` returning -1 is a feature, not a bug — a well-designed cryptanalysis tool must be able to report "no periodic key-length structure is present here" as cleanly as it reports a found length, because that null result is exactly what a one-time pad should produce, and §15 uses that exact return value as the simulator's punchline.

WHAT TO WATCH FOR

1. The Kasiski vote table's top entries will be small numbers (2, 3) that are divisors of the true key length, not the length itself — exactly the ambiguity §6 warned about. 2. The IC bar chart will show one unmistakable spike; note where its multiples (12, 18…) also show elevated bars, and that the algorithm correctly reports the smallest one. 3. The recovered key should exactly match the hidden key, and the recovered plaintext should exactly match the original sample text, letter for letter. 4. Run the one-time pad attack and watch the IC bars go flat everywhere — no spike, no candidate, "guessed length: none." That flat chart is Shannon's perfect secrecy, made visible.
